    Model Questions — Unit IV: Estimation Theory

    UGC NET Statistics (Code 107) · 50 questions · Click "Show Answer" for the worked solution

    About this set

    Unbiasedness, consistency and efficiency; method of moments and maximum likelihood; the Cramér–Rao bound; sufficiency, completeness and ancillarity; Rao–Blackwell, Lehmann–Scheffé and Basu; confidence intervals; order statistics and rank correlation.

    These are model questions written for practice at the standard of the paper; they are not questions from a past paper. Try each one before opening the answer. Every answer gives the working and a link to the section of the Unit IV notes that teaches it.

    The questions

    Q1. If $X\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)$ with $n\ge2$, an unbiased estimator of $p^2$ is:
    1. $\left(\dfrac Xn\right)^2$
    2. $\dfrac{X(X-1)}{n(n-1)}$
    3. $\dfrac{X^2}{n(n-1)}$
    4. $\dfrac{X(n-X)}{n(n-1)}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\dfrac{X(X-1)}{n(n-1)}$.

    The second factorial moment of the binomial is $E[X(X-1)]=n(n-1)p^2$, so $\dfrac{X(X-1)}{n(n-1)}$ is unbiased for $p^2$. $(X/n)^2$ has expectation $p^2+\dfrac{pq}{n}$, and option D is unbiased for $pq$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Unbiasedness

    Q2. Let $X$ be a single observation from the Poisson distribution with mean $\lambda$. The only unbiased estimator of $e^{-2\lambda}$ is:
    1. $e^{-2X}$
    2. $2^{-X}$
    3. $e^{-X}$
    4. $(-1)^X$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $(-1)^X$.

    $$\begin{aligned}E[(-1)^X] &= \sum_x(-1)^x\dfrac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!} \\[4pt] &= e^{-\lambda}e^{-\lambda} = e^{-2\lambda}\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ and by completeness of the Poisson family it is the only unbiased estimator. It takes only the values $\pm1$ for a quantity lying in $(0,1)$: an unbiased, indeed UMVU, estimator can be absurd.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Unbiasedness

    Q3. Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be a random sample from $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, both parameters unknown. Among estimators of $\sigma^2$ of the form $c\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$, the mean squared error is least for $c=$
    1. $\dfrac1{n+1}$
    2. $\dfrac1n$
    3. $\dfrac1{n-1}$
    4. $\dfrac1{n-2}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $\dfrac1{n+1}$.

    With $W=\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2\sim\sigma^2\chi^2_{n-1}$: $E(W)=(n-1)\sigma^2$, $E(W^2)=(n^2-1)\sigma^4$. $$\begin{aligned}&\text{MSE}(cW) \\[4pt] &\quad = \sigma^4\left[c^2(n^2-1)-2c(n-1)+1\right]\end{aligned}$$ is least at $c=\dfrac{1}{n+1}$. The divisor $n-1$ gives the unbiased estimator and $n$ the maximum likelihood estimator; neither minimises the MSE.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Unbiasedness

    Q4. Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ $(n\gt1)$ be a random sample from the uniform distribution on $(0,\theta)$. Consider the statements:(I) $2\bar X$ is unbiased for $\theta$.(II) $X_{(n)}$ is unbiased for $\theta$.(III) $\dfrac{n+1}{n}X_{(n)}$ is unbiased for $\theta$.(IV) $\dfrac{n+1}{n}X_{(n)}$ has smaller variance than $2\bar X$.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and III only
    2. I and II only
    3. I, III and IV only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) I, III and IV only.

    $E(\bar X)=\tfrac\theta2$: (I) true. $E(X_{(n)})=\dfrac{n}{n+1}\theta$: (II) false and (III) true. $V(2\bar X)=\dfrac{\theta^2}{3n}$, while $V\left(\tfrac{n+1}{n}X_{(n)}\right)=\dfrac{\theta^2}{n(n+2)}$, smaller for every $n\gt1$ and of order $1/n^2$: (IV) true.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Unbiasedness

    Q5. For a random sample from the Cauchy distribution with location $\theta$, $f(x)=\dfrac{1}{\pi[1+(x-\theta)^2]}$, which of the following is a consistent estimator of $\theta$?
    1. The sample mean
    2. The sample median
    3. The mid-range $\tfrac12(X_{(1)}+X_{(n)})$
    4. The first observation $X_1$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) The sample median.

    The mean of $n$ Cauchy observations has the same Cauchy distribution as one observation, so it does not settle down; the mid-range is even worse. The sample median is asymptotically normal about $\theta$ with variance $\dfrac{1}{4nf(\theta)^2}=\dfrac{\pi^2}{4n}\to0$, hence consistent.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Consistency

    Q6. Consider the statements:(I) An unbiased estimator whose variance tends to zero is consistent.(II) Every consistent estimator is unbiased.(III) If $T_n$ is consistent for $\theta$ and $g$ is continuous, then $g(T_n)$ is consistent for $g(\theta)$.(IV) A maximum likelihood estimator is always unbiased.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and III only
    2. I and II only
    3. I, III and IV only
    4. II and IV only
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) I and III only.

    (I) follows from Chebyshev's inequality and (III) is the invariance of consistency under continuous maps. (II) is false: $\tfrac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$ is consistent but biased for $\sigma^2$. (IV) is false for the same example, which is the maximum likelihood estimator in the normal case.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Consistency

    Q7. Let $X_{(n)}$ be the largest of $n$ independent observations from the uniform distribution on $(0,\theta)$. As $n\to\infty$, the distribution of $n(\theta-X_{(n)})$ converges to:
    1. $N(0,\theta^2)$
    2. the uniform distribution on $(0,\theta)$
    3. the exponential distribution with mean $1/\theta$
    4. the exponential distribution with mean $\theta$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) the exponential distribution with mean $\theta$.

    $$\begin{aligned}&P\{n(\theta-X_{(n)})\gt t\} \\[4pt] &\quad = P\left\{X_{(n)}\lt\theta-\tfrac tn\right\} \\[4pt] &\quad = \left(1-\dfrac{t}{n\theta}\right)^n \to e^{-t/\theta}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ So the limit is exponential with mean $\theta$. The rate is $n$, not $\sqrt n$, and the limit is not normal: the usual regularity conditions fail because the support depends on $\theta$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Consistency

    Q8. A random sample from a gamma distribution with shape $\alpha$ and scale $\beta$ (mean $\alpha\beta$) has sample mean 6 and sample variance 12. The method-of-moments estimates $(\hat\alpha,\hat\beta)$ are:
    1. (2, 3)
    2. (6, 1)
    3. (3, 2)
    4. (12, 0.5)
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) (3, 2).

    Mean $\alpha\beta=6$ and variance $\alpha\beta^2=12$ give $\hat\beta=\dfrac{12}{6}=2$ and $\hat\alpha=\dfrac{6^2}{12}=3$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Method of Moments (MoM)

    Q9. A random sample from the density $f(x;\theta)=\theta x^{\theta-1}$, $0\lt x\lt1$, has mean $\bar x=0.6$. The method-of-moments estimate of $\theta$ is:
    1. 1.5
    2. 0.6
    3. 2.5
    4. 0.667
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 1.5.

    $E(X)=\dfrac{\theta}{\theta+1}$. Setting this equal to $\bar x$: $\hat\theta=\dfrac{\bar x}{1-\bar x}=\dfrac{0.6}{0.4}=1.5$. The maximum likelihood estimator, $-n/\sum\ln x_i$, is a different function of the sample.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Method of Moments (MoM)

    Q10. A random sample of size 10 from the density $f(x;\theta)=\theta x^{\theta-1}$, $0\lt x\lt1$, gives $\sum\ln x_i=-4$. The maximum likelihood estimate of $\theta$ is:
    1. 0.4
    2. 4
    3. 0.25
    4. 2.5
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) 2.5.

    $\ln L=n\ln\theta+(\theta-1)\sum\ln x_i$, so $\dfrac{\partial\ln L}{\partial\theta}=\dfrac n\theta+\sum\ln x_i=0$ gives $\hat\theta=-\dfrac{n}{\sum\ln x_i}=\dfrac{10}{4}=2.5$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Maximum Likelihood Estimation

    Q11. For a random sample from the uniform distribution on $(\theta,\theta+1)$, the maximum likelihood estimator of $\theta$ is:
    1. $X_{(1)}$ only
    2. $X_{(n)}-1$ only
    3. any value in the interval $\left[X_{(n)}-1,\;X_{(1)}\right]$
    4. $\bar X-\tfrac12$ only
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) any value in the interval $\left[X_{(n)}-1,\;X_{(1)}\right]$.

    The likelihood is 1 when $\theta\le X_{(1)}$ and $X_{(n)}\le\theta+1$, and 0 otherwise. It is constant on $[X_{(n)}-1,X_{(1)}]$, so every point of this interval maximises it: the maximum likelihood estimator need not be unique.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Maximum Likelihood Estimation

    Q12. For a random sample of odd size from the double exponential density $f(x;\theta)=\tfrac12e^{-|x-\theta|}$, the maximum likelihood estimator of $\theta$ is:
    1. the sample mean
    2. the sample median
    3. the mid-range
    4. the sample mode
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) the sample median.

    $\ln L=-n\ln2-\sum|x_i-\theta|$. Maximising it means minimising $\sum|x_i-\theta|$, which is done by the sample median. (For the normal density the same argument with squares gives the mean.)

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Maximum Likelihood Estimation

    Q13. A random sample of size 10 from $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ gives $\bar x=20$ and $\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2=90$. The maximum likelihood estimate of the 95th percentile $\mu+1.645\,\sigma$ is:
    1. 24.935
    2. 25.202
    3. 34.805
    4. 21.645
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 24.935.

    The maximum likelihood estimates are $\hat\mu=\bar x=20$ and $\hat\sigma^2=\tfrac1n\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2=9$, so $\hat\sigma=3$. By the invariance property, the estimate of $\mu+1.645\sigma$ is $20+1.645(3)=24.935$. Using the divisor $n-1$ ($s=3.162$) gives 25.202, which is not the maximum likelihood estimate.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Maximum Likelihood Estimation

    Q14. For a random sample from $f(x;\mu,\theta)=\dfrac1\theta e^{-(x-\mu)/\theta}$, $x\gt\mu$, with both parameters unknown, the maximum likelihood estimators of $\mu$ and $\theta$ are:
    1. $\bar X$ and $S$
    2. $X_{(1)}$ and $\bar X$
    3. $X_{(1)}$ and $\bar X-X_{(1)}$
    4. $X_{(n)}$ and $\bar X-X_{(n)}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $X_{(1)}$ and $\bar X-X_{(1)}$.

    $L=\theta^{-n}\exp\{-\sum(x_i-\mu)/\theta\}$ for $\mu\le x_{(1)}$. For any $\theta$ it increases with $\mu$, so $\hat\mu=X_{(1)}$. Then maximising over $\theta$ gives $$\begin{aligned}\hat\theta &= \tfrac1n\sum(X_i-X_{(1)}) \\[4pt] &= \bar X-X_{(1)}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Maximum Likelihood Estimation

    Q15. Let $\hat\lambda=\bar X$ be the maximum likelihood estimator of the mean of a Poisson distribution from a sample of size $n$. The asymptotic variance of the maximum likelihood estimator of $P(X=0)=e^{-\lambda}$ is:
    1. $\dfrac{\lambda}{n}$
    2. $\dfrac{e^{-2\lambda}}{n}$
    3. $\dfrac{e^{-\lambda}(1-e^{-\lambda})}{n}$
    4. $\dfrac{\lambda e^{-2\lambda}}{n}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\dfrac{\lambda e^{-2\lambda}}{n}$.

    By invariance the estimator is $e^{-\bar X}$, and by the delta method its asymptotic variance is $[g'(\lambda)]^2\,\dfrac\lambda n=\dfrac{\lambda e^{-2\lambda}}{n}$. Option C is the variance of the proportion of zeros in the sample, a less efficient estimator.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Maximum Likelihood Estimation

    Q16. A random sample from $N(\theta,\theta)$, $\theta\gt0$ (mean and variance both equal to $\theta$), has $\tfrac1n\sum x_i^2=6$. The maximum likelihood estimate of $\theta$ is:
    1. 6
    2. 2
    3. $\sqrt6$
    4. 3
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) 2.

    $$\begin{aligned}&\ln L \\[4pt] &\quad = -\tfrac n2\ln\theta-\dfrac{\sum x_i^2}{2\theta}+\sum x_i-\dfrac{n\theta}{2}+\text{const}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Differentiating, $\theta^2+\theta-\tfrac1n\sum x_i^2=0$, so $\hat\theta=\dfrac{-1+\sqrt{1+4(6)}}{2}=2$. Note that it depends on the data only through $\sum x_i^2$, the sufficient statistic.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Maximum Likelihood Estimation

    Q17. For large samples from a normal population, the efficiency of the sample median relative to the sample mean as an estimator of $\mu$ is:
    1. $\dfrac2\pi$
    2. $\dfrac\pi2$
    3. $\dfrac12$
    4. $\dfrac3\pi$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $\dfrac2\pi$.

    $V(\bar X)=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}$ and the asymptotic variance of the median is $\dfrac{1}{4nf(\mu)^2}=\dfrac{\pi\sigma^2}{2n}$. The ratio is $\dfrac2\pi\approx0.637$: the median wastes about 36% of the observations. $\tfrac3\pi$ is the corresponding figure for the Hodges–Lehmann estimator.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Efficiency and UMVUE

    Q18. Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be a random sample from $N(\theta,1)$. The uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimator of $\theta^2$ is:
    1. $\bar X^2$
    2. $\bar X^2-\dfrac1n$
    3. $\dfrac1n\sum X_i^2-1$
    4. $\bar X^2+\dfrac1n$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\bar X^2-\dfrac1n$.

    $\bar X$ is complete and sufficient, and $E(\bar X^2)=\theta^2+\tfrac1n$. So $\bar X^2-\tfrac1n$ is an unbiased function of the complete sufficient statistic, hence the UMVUE by Lehmann–Scheffé. Option C is also unbiased but is not a function of $\bar X$ alone.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Efficiency and UMVUE

    Q19. For a random sample of size $n$ from $N(0,\sigma^2)$, the Cramér–Rao lower bound for the variance of an unbiased estimator of $\sigma^2$ is:
    1. $\dfrac{\sigma^4}{n}$
    2. $\dfrac{2\sigma^4}{n-1}$
    3. $\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}$
    4. $\dfrac{2\sigma^4}{n}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\dfrac{2\sigma^4}{n}$.

    With $\phi=\sigma^2$: $$\begin{aligned}&\ln f \\[4pt] &\quad = -\tfrac12\ln\phi-\dfrac{x^2}{2\phi}+\text{const}\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ and $$\begin{aligned}I(\phi) &= -E\left[\dfrac{1}{2\phi^2}-\dfrac{x^2}{\phi^3}\right] \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{1}{2\phi^2}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ The bound is $\dfrac{1}{nI}=\dfrac{2\sigma^4}{n}$, attained by $\tfrac1n\sum X_i^2$. $\dfrac{2\sigma^4}{n-1}$ is the variance of $S^2$ when the mean is unknown.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Cramér–Rao Lower Bound (CRLB)

    Q20. For a random sample of size $n$ from the Bernoulli distribution with parameter $p$, the Cramér–Rao lower bound for the variance of an unbiased estimator of $p(1-p)$ is:
    1. $\dfrac{(1-2p)^2\,p(1-p)}{n}$
    2. $\dfrac{p(1-p)}{n}$
    3. $\dfrac{p^2(1-p)^2}{n}$
    4. $\dfrac{(1-2p)^2}{n\,p(1-p)}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $\dfrac{(1-2p)^2\,p(1-p)}{n}$.

    For $g(p)=p(1-p)$ the bound is $\dfrac{[g'(p)]^2}{nI(p)}$ with $g'(p)=1-2p$ and $I(p)=\dfrac{1}{p(1-p)}$, giving $\dfrac{(1-2p)^2p(1-p)}{n}$. At $p=\tfrac12$ the bound is zero, although no estimator has zero variance: the bound is not attained.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Cramér–Rao Lower Bound (CRLB)

    Q21. For a random sample of size $n$ from the Cauchy density $\dfrac{1}{\pi[1+(x-\theta)^2]}$, the Cramér–Rao lower bound for unbiased estimators of $\theta$ is:
    1. $\dfrac1n$
    2. $\dfrac{\pi^2}{4n}$
    3. $\dfrac2n$
    4. $\dfrac{1}{2n}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $\dfrac2n$.

    $$\begin{aligned}&\dfrac{\partial\ln f}{\partial\theta} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{2(x-\theta)}{1+(x-\theta)^2}\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ and $$\begin{aligned}&I(\theta) \\[4pt] &\quad = \int\frac{4u^2}{(1+u^2)^2}\cdot\frac{du}{\pi(1+u^2)} \\[4pt] &\quad = \frac12\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ The bound is $\dfrac{1}{nI}=\dfrac2n$. The sample median has asymptotic variance $\dfrac{\pi^2}{4n}\approx\dfrac{2.47}{n}$, so its efficiency is $8/\pi^2\approx81\%$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Cramér–Rao Lower Bound (CRLB)

    Q22. Consider the statements about the Cramér–Rao lower bound for a parametric function $g(\theta)$:(I) An unbiased estimator $T$ attains the bound if and only if $\dfrac{\partial\ln L}{\partial\theta}=A(\theta)\,[T-g(\theta)]$.(II) The bound can be attained only when the distribution belongs to a one-parameter exponential family.(III) For the uniform distribution on $(0,\theta)$ there is an unbiased estimator of $\theta$ with variance smaller than $\theta^2/n$.(IV) A uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimator always attains the bound.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and II only
    2. I, II and III only
    3. I, III and IV only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) I, II and III only.

    (I) is the condition for equality in the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and integrating it gives the exponential family form: (II). (III) $\tfrac{n+1}{n}X_{(n)}$ has variance $\dfrac{\theta^2}{n(n+2)}$; the regularity conditions fail, so there is no contradiction. (IV) is false: the UMVUE of $e^{-\lambda}$ in the Poisson case has variance above the bound.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Cramér–Rao Lower Bound (CRLB)

    Q23. Given below are two statements, one labelled Assertion (A) and the other Reason (R).Assertion (A): For a Poisson sample, the UMVUE $\left(1-\tfrac1n\right)^{\sum X_i}$ of $e^{-\lambda}$ does not attain the Cramér–Rao lower bound.Reason (R): In the Poisson family the bound is attained only by estimators of linear functions $a\lambda+b$.Choose the correct answer.
    1. Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
    2. Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
    3. (A) is true but (R) is false
    4. (A) is false but (R) is true
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

    $\dfrac{\partial\ln L}{\partial\lambda}=\dfrac n\lambda(\bar X-\lambda)$, so equality holds only for $T=\bar X$ and linear functions of it, estimating $a\lambda+b$. $e^{-\lambda}$ is not linear in $\lambda$, so its UMVUE, though best among unbiased estimators, has variance $e^{-2\lambda}(e^{\lambda/n}-1)\gt\dfrac{\lambda e^{-2\lambda}}{n}$. Both statements are true and (R) explains (A).

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Cramér–Rao Lower Bound (CRLB)

    Q24. For a random sample from the uniform distribution on $(-\theta,\theta)$, a sufficient statistic for $\theta$ is:
    1. $\bar X$
    2. $X_{(n)}$
    3. $X_{(n)}-X_{(1)}$
    4. $\max_i|X_i|$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\max_i|X_i|$.

    $L=(2\theta)^{-n}$ if $-\theta\lt x_{(1)}$ and $x_{(n)}\lt\theta$, i.e. if $\max|x_i|\lt\theta$, and 0 otherwise. By the factorisation theorem $\max|X_i|=\max\{-X_{(1)},X_{(n)}\}$ is sufficient. $X_{(n)}$ alone is not, because a large negative observation also restricts $\theta$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Sufficiency & Factorization Theorem

    Q25. For a random sample from the density $f(x;\theta)=\theta(1+x)^{-(\theta+1)}$, $x\gt0$, a sufficient statistic for $\theta$ is:
    1. $\sum X_i$
    2. $\prod X_i$
    3. $\sum\ln(1+X_i)$
    4. $X_{(n)}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $\sum\ln(1+X_i)$.

    $$\begin{aligned}L &= \theta^n\exp\{-(\theta+1)\sum\ln(1+x_i)\}\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ which depends on the sample only through $\sum\ln(1+x_i)$ (equivalently $\prod(1+x_i)$). It is a one-parameter exponential family, so this statistic is also complete.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Sufficiency & Factorization Theorem

    Q26. Consider the statements about sufficiency for a random sample $X_1,\dots,X_n$:(I) The whole sample is a sufficient statistic.(II) A one-to-one function of a sufficient statistic is sufficient.(III) Every function of a sufficient statistic is sufficient.(IV) The set of order statistics is sufficient.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I, II and IV only
    2. I and II only
    3. I, II and III only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) I, II and IV only.

    (I) is trivially true, (II) holds because nothing is lost by a one-to-one map, and (IV) holds because the joint density of independent, identically distributed observations is symmetric. (III) is false: a constant is a function of any statistic. The correct statement is that any statistic of which a sufficient statistic is a function is itself sufficient.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Sufficiency & Factorization Theorem

    Q27. $X_1$ and $X_2$ are independent Poisson variates with the same mean $\lambda$. Then $P(X_1=1\mid X_1+X_2=2)$ equals:
    1. $\lambda e^{-\lambda}$
    2. $\dfrac14$
    3. $\dfrac13$
    4. $\dfrac12$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\dfrac12$.

    Given $X_1+X_2=t$, $X_1\sim\text{Bin}\left(t,\tfrac12\right)$, so the probability is $\binom21\left(\tfrac12\right)^2=\tfrac12$. It does not involve $\lambda$, which is what the sufficiency of $X_1+X_2$ means.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Sufficiency & Factorization Theorem

    Q28. For a random sample from the uniform distribution on $(0,\theta)$, which of the following statistics is ancillary?
    1. $X_{(n)}$
    2. $X_{(n)}-X_{(1)}$
    3. $\dfrac{X_{(1)}}{X_{(n)}}$
    4. $\bar X$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $\dfrac{X_{(1)}}{X_{(n)}}$.

    $\theta$ is a scale parameter: $X_i=\theta U_i$ with $U_i$ uniform on $(0,1)$. Any ratio such as $\dfrac{X_{(1)}}{X_{(n)}}=\dfrac{U_{(1)}}{U_{(n)}}$ has a distribution free of $\theta$. The range would be ancillary in a location family, not here: its distribution scales with $\theta$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Minimal Sufficiency & Ancillarity

    Q29. For a random sample of size $n\ge2$ from the uniform distribution on $\left(\theta-\tfrac12,\theta+\tfrac12\right)$, the statistic $(X_{(1)},X_{(n)})$ is:
    1. sufficient and complete
    2. minimal sufficient but not complete
    3. complete but not sufficient
    4. neither sufficient nor complete
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) minimal sufficient but not complete.

    The likelihood is the indicator of $x_{(n)}-\tfrac12\lt\theta\lt x_{(1)}+\tfrac12$, so $(X_{(1)},X_{(n)})$ is minimal sufficient. The range $R=X_{(n)}-X_{(1)}$ is ancillary with $E(R)=\dfrac{n-1}{n+1}$, so $R-\dfrac{n-1}{n+1}$ is a non-zero function of the statistic with zero expectation for all $\theta$: not complete.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Minimal Sufficiency & Ancillarity

    Q30. Let $X$ be a single observation from $N(0,\sigma^2)$, $\sigma\gt0$. Consider the statements:(I) $X$ is a sufficient statistic.(II) $X$ is a complete statistic.(III) $X^2$ is a complete sufficient statistic.(IV) $X^2$ is a minimal sufficient statistic.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I, III and IV only
    2. I and II only
    3. II and III only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) I, III and IV only.

    (I) The sample itself is always sufficient. (II) is false: $E(X)=0$ for every $\sigma$ although $X$ is not zero. (III) The family is a one-parameter exponential family in $X^2$, whose natural parameter space contains an interval. (IV) A complete sufficient statistic is minimal sufficient.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Completeness

    Q31. Given below are two statements, one labelled Assertion (A) and the other Reason (R).Assertion (A): A complete sufficient statistic is minimal sufficient.Reason (R): Every minimal sufficient statistic is complete.Choose the correct answer.
    1. Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
    2. Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
    3. (A) is true but (R) is false
    4. (A) is false but (R) is true
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) (A) is true but (R) is false.

    (A) is Bahadur's theorem (when a minimal sufficient statistic exists). (R) is false: for the uniform distribution on $(\theta-\tfrac12,\theta+\tfrac12)$, or for the Cauchy location family, the minimal sufficient statistic is not complete.

    Q32. Let $X\sim\text{Bin}(2,p)$ where the parameter space is only $\left\{\tfrac14,\tfrac34\right\}$. Then the family of distributions of $X$ is:
    1. complete, because every binomial family is complete
    2. complete, because $X$ is sufficient
    3. not complete, because $X$ is not sufficient
    4. not complete, because there is a non-zero $g$ with $E_p\,g(X)=0$ for both values of $p$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) not complete, because there is a non-zero $g$ with $E_p\,g(X)=0$ for both values of $p$.

    $$\begin{aligned}&E_pg(X) \\[4pt] &\quad = g(0)q^2+2g(1)pq+g(2)p^2 \\[4pt] &\quad = 0\end{aligned}$$ at the two values of $p$ gives two linear equations in three unknowns: $9g(0)+6g(1)+g(2)=0$ and $g(0)+6g(1)+9g(2)=0$. A non-zero solution is $g(0)=g(2)=1$, $g(1)=-\tfrac53$. Completeness depends on the parameter space: with $p\in(0,1)$ the family is complete.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Completeness

    Q33. $X_1,\dots,X_5$ is a random sample from the Poisson distribution with mean $\lambda$, and $\sum x_i=10$. The Rao–Blackwell improvement of the estimator $I(X_1=0)$ of $e^{-\lambda}$ takes the value:
    1. 0.107
    2. 0.135
    3. 0.200
    4. 0.328
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 0.107.

    Given $T=\sum X_i=t$, $X_1\sim\text{Bin}\left(t,\tfrac1n\right)$, so $$\begin{aligned}E[I(X_1=0)\mid T=t] &= \left(1-\tfrac1n\right)^t \\[4pt] &= (0.8)^{10} = 0.107\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ This is the UMVUE of $e^{-\lambda}$. The maximum likelihood estimate is $e^{-\bar x}=e^{-2}=0.135$.

    Q34. $X_1,\dots,X_{10}$ are independent Bernoulli($p$) variates and $T=\sum X_i=4$ is observed. The value of the UMVUE of $p^2$, obtained by conditioning $X_1X_2$ on $T$, is:
    1. $\dfrac{4}{25}$
    2. $\dfrac{2}{15}$
    3. $\dfrac{8}{45}$
    4. $\dfrac15$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\dfrac{2}{15}$.

    $$\begin{aligned}&E(X_1X_2\mid T=t) \\[4pt] &\quad = P(X_1=1,X_2=1\mid T=t) \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{\binom{n-2}{t-2}}{\binom nt} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{t(t-1)}{n(n-1)} = \dfrac{12}{90} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{2}{15}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ The maximum likelihood estimate is $(0.4)^2=\tfrac4{25}$, which is biased upward.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Rao–Blackwell Theorem

    Q35. For a random sample from the uniform distribution on $(0,\theta)$, the UMVUE of $\theta$ is:
    1. $2\bar X$
    2. $X_{(n)}$
    3. $\dfrac{n+2}{n+1}X_{(n)}$
    4. $\dfrac{n+1}{n}X_{(n)}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\dfrac{n+1}{n}X_{(n)}$.

    $X_{(n)}$ is complete and sufficient, with $E(X_{(n)})=\dfrac{n}{n+1}\theta$. So $\dfrac{n+1}{n}X_{(n)}$ is an unbiased function of it and is the UMVUE by Lehmann–Scheffé. $\dfrac{n+2}{n+1}X_{(n)}$ is the multiple of $X_{(n)}$ with least mean squared error, but it is biased.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Lehmann–Scheffé Theorem

    Q36. For a random sample of size $n$ from the exponential distribution with mean $\theta$, the UMVUE of $\theta^2$ is:
    1. $\dfrac{n\bar X^2}{n+1}$
    2. $\bar X^2$
    3. $\dfrac{(n+1)\bar X^2}{n}$
    4. $\dfrac{1}{2n}\sum X_i^2-\bar X^2$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $\dfrac{n\bar X^2}{n+1}$.

    $T=\sum X_i$ is complete and sufficient and is gamma with shape $n$: $E(T^2)=n(n+1)\theta^2$. So $\dfrac{T^2}{n(n+1)}=\dfrac{n\bar X^2}{n+1}$ is the UMVUE. $\bar X^2$ overestimates $\theta^2$ on average by the factor $\tfrac{n+1}{n}$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Lehmann–Scheffé Theorem

    Q37. For a random sample of size $n\ge2$ from the density $f(x;\lambda)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}$, $x\gt0$, the UMVUE of $\lambda$ is:
    1. $\dfrac{1}{\bar X}$
    2. $\dfrac{n+1}{\sum X_i}$
    3. $\dfrac{n-1}{\sum X_i}$
    4. $\dfrac{n}{\sum X_i+1}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $\dfrac{n-1}{\sum X_i}$.

    $T=\sum X_i$ is complete and sufficient, gamma with shape $n$ and rate $\lambda$, so $E\left(\dfrac1T\right)=\dfrac{\lambda}{n-1}$. Hence $\dfrac{n-1}{T}$ is the UMVUE. The maximum likelihood estimator $\dfrac1{\bar X}=\dfrac nT$ is biased upward by the factor $\tfrac{n}{n-1}$.

    Q38. Given below are two statements, one labelled Assertion (A) and the other Reason (R).Assertion (A): In sampling from $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, the sample mean $\bar X$ and the sample variance $S^2$ are independent.Reason (R): For any fixed $\sigma^2$, $\bar X$ is a complete sufficient statistic for $\mu$ and the distribution of $S^2$ does not depend on $\mu$.Choose the correct answer.
    1. Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
    2. Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
    3. (A) is true but (R) is false
    4. (A) is false but (R) is true
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

    This is Basu's theorem: a complete sufficient statistic is independent of every ancillary statistic. For fixed $\sigma^2$, $\bar X$ is complete sufficient for $\mu$ and $S^2$ is ancillary (it is unchanged when a constant is added to every observation). Since this holds for each $\sigma^2$, the independence holds throughout. Both statements are true and (R) is the reason.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Basu's Theorem

    Q39. $X_1,\dots,X_4$ is a random sample from the exponential distribution with mean $\theta$, and $U=\dfrac{X_1}{X_1+X_2+X_3+X_4}$. Then $E(U^2)$ equals:
    1. $\dfrac1{16}$
    2. $\dfrac1{10}$
    3. $\dfrac18$
    4. $\dfrac1{20}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\dfrac1{10}$.

    $U$ is ancillary (a ratio in a scale family) and $T=\sum X_i$ is complete sufficient, so by Basu's theorem they are independent: $E(X_1^2)=E(U^2)E(T^2)$, i.e. $2\theta^2=E(U^2)\cdot n(n+1)\theta^2$. With $n=4$, $E(U^2)=\dfrac{2}{20}=\dfrac1{10}$. (Directly, $U\sim\text{Beta}(1,3)$.)

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Basu's Theorem

    Q40. The largest of 4 independent observations from the uniform distribution on $(0,\theta)$ is 8. Using the pivot $X_{(n)}/\theta$, the shortest confidence interval for $\theta$ with confidence coefficient $\tfrac{15}{16}$ is:
    1. (8, 12)
    2. (4, 16)
    3. (8, 32)
    4. (8, 16)
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) (8, 16).

    $P\left(X_{(n)}/\theta\le u\right)=u^n$. The density of the pivot increases on $(0,1)$, so the shortest interval uses $\alpha^{1/n}\le X_{(n)}/\theta\le1$, giving $\left(X_{(n)},\;X_{(n)}\alpha^{-1/n}\right)$. With $\alpha=\tfrac1{16}$, $n=4$: $\alpha^{-1/4}=2$ and the interval is $(8,16)$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit IV, Method of Pivoting

    Q41. A random sample of size 5 from the exponential distribution with mean $\theta$ has $\sum x_i=20$. Given $\chi^2_{10;\,0.975}=20.48$ and $\chi^2_{10;\,0.025}=3.25$ (upper-tail areas 0.025 and 0.975), the 95% equal-tailed confidence interval for $\theta$ is:
    1. (1.95, 12.31)
    2. (0.98, 6.15)
    3. (3.25, 20.48)
    4. (2.05, 7.80)
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) (1.95, 12.31).

    $\dfrac{2\sum X_i}{\theta}\sim\chi^2_{2n}=\chi^2_{10}$. So $\dfrac{2\sum x_i}{20.48}\lt\theta\lt\dfrac{2\sum x_i}{3.25}$, i.e. $\dfrac{40}{20.48}=1.95$ to $\dfrac{40}{3.25}=12.31$. Forgetting the factor 2 gives option B.

    Q44. Consider the statements about a confidence interval for a normal mean with known variance:(I) For a given sample, its width increases with the confidence coefficient.(II) For a given confidence coefficient, its width decreases as the sample size increases.(III) Once a 95% interval has been computed from the data, the probability that $\mu$ lies in it is 0.95.(IV) To halve the width, the sample size must be made four times as large.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and II only
    2. I, II and III only
    3. II, III and IV only
    4. I, II and IV only
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) I, II and IV only.

    The width is $2z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma/\sqrt n$: (I), (II) and (IV) follow. (III) is false in the frequentist sense: $\mu$ is a fixed constant and the computed interval either contains it or not; 0.95 is the proportion of such intervals, over repeated samples, that cover $\mu$.

    Q47. Let $F_n$ be the empirical distribution function of a random sample of size 25 from a continuous distribution $F$. At a point $x$ where $F(x)=0.2$, the variance of $F_n(x)$ is:
    1. 0.16
    2. 0.0064
    3. 0.008
    4. 0.032
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) 0.0064.

    $nF_n(x)$ is the number of observations not exceeding $x$, which is $\text{Bin}(n,F(x))$. So $$\begin{aligned}V[F_n(x)] &= \dfrac{F(x)[1-F(x)]}{n} \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{0.2\times0.8}{25} = 0.0064\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ $F_n(x)$ is unbiased and consistent for $F(x)$, uniformly in $x$ by the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem.

    Q48. The median $M$ of a random sample of size 3 from the uniform distribution on $(0,1)$ satisfies $P\left(M\lt\tfrac14\right)=$
