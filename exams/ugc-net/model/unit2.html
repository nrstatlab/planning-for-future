    Model Questions — Unit II: Real Analysis and Matrix Algebra

    UGC NET Statistics (Code 107) · 50 questions · Click "Show Answer" for the worked solution

    About this set

    Countability, sequences and series, power series, continuity and uniform convergence, differentiation, Riemann and improper integrals, functions of two variables, vector spaces, rank, determinants, linear equations, characteristic roots and quadratic forms.

    These are model questions written for practice at the standard of the paper; they are not questions from a past paper. Try each one before opening the answer. Every answer gives the working and a link to the section of the Unit II notes that teaches it.

    The questions

    Q1. Consider the following sets:(I) The set of all polynomials with integer coefficients.(II) The set of all infinite sequences of 0s and 1s.(III) The set of all finite subsets of $\mathbb N$.(IV) The set of all irrational numbers in $(0,1)$.Which of these sets are countable?
    1. I only
    2. I, II and III only
    3. I and III only
    4. III and IV only
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) I and III only.

    (I) For each degree there are countably many polynomials, and a countable union of countable sets is countable. (III) The finite subsets of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ are finitely many for each $n$; again a countable union. (II) is uncountable by Cantor's diagonal argument (it has the cardinality of $\mathbb R$). (IV) is uncountable, because $(0,1)$ is uncountable and its rational points are countable.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Finite, Countable and Uncountable Sets

    Q2. Let $A=\left\{\tfrac1n:n\in\mathbb N\right\}\subset\mathbb R$. Consider the statements:(I) $A$ is a closed set.(II) 0 is the only limit point of $A$.(III) $A\cup\{0\}$ is compact.(IV) $A$ is countable.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and IV only
    2. II and IV only
    3. I, II and III only
    4. II, III and IV only
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) II, III and IV only.

    $\tfrac1n\to 0$, so 0 is a limit point, and no other point is one: (II) true. $0\notin A$, so $A$ does not contain all its limit points: (I) false. $A\cup\{0\}$ is closed and bounded, hence compact by Heine–Borel: (III) true. $n\mapsto\tfrac1n$ is a bijection from $\mathbb N$: (IV) true.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Finite, Countable and Uncountable Sets

    Q3. $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{2}{n}\right)^{3n}$ equals:
    1. $e^{5}$
    2. $e^{6}$
    3. $e^{3/2}$
    4. $e^{2/3}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $e^{6}$.

    $$\begin{aligned}\left(1+\tfrac2n\right)^{3n} &= \left[\left(1+\tfrac2n\right)^{n/2}\right]^{6} \\[4pt] &\to e^{6}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ In general $\left(1+\tfrac an\right)^{bn}\to e^{ab}$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Sequences of Real Numbers

    Q4. For the sequence $a_n=\sin\dfrac{n\pi}{2}+(-1)^n$, the values of $\limsup a_n$ and $\liminf a_n$ are, respectively:
    1. $1$ and $-2$
    2. $2$ and $-2$
    3. $1$ and $-1$
    4. $2$ and $0$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $1$ and $-2$.

    The sequence has period 4: $n=1$: $1-1=0$; $n=2$: $0+1=1$; $n=3$: $-1-1=-2$; $n=4$: $0+1=1$. Its subsequential limits are $0,1,-2$, so $\limsup a_n=1$ and $\liminf a_n=-2$. The value 2 is never reached because $\sin\tfrac{n\pi}2=1$ only for odd $n$, where $(-1)^n=-1$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Sequences of Real Numbers

    Q5. Let $a_1=1$ and $a_{n+1}=\sqrt{6+a_n}$ for $n\ge1$. Then $\{a_n\}$:
    1. is unbounded
    2. converges to 2
    3. converges to 3
    4. converges to $\sqrt6$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) converges to 3.

    By induction $a_n\lt 3$ and $a_{n+1}\gt a_n$ (since $6+a\gt a^2$ for $0\lt a\lt 3$), so the sequence is increasing and bounded above, hence convergent. The limit satisfies $l^2=6+l$, i.e. $(l-3)(l+2)=0$, and $l\gt0$ gives $l=3$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Sequences of Real Numbers

    Q6. Consider the statements about real sequences:(I) Every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence.(II) Every Cauchy sequence of rational numbers converges to a rational number.(III) Every bounded monotone sequence converges.(IV) If $|a_n|\to|a|$, then $a_n\to a$.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and II only
    2. I and III only
    3. I, III and IV only
    4. I, II and III only
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) I and III only.

    (I) is the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem and (III) the monotone convergence theorem. (II) is false: the decimal approximations of $\sqrt2$ form a Cauchy sequence of rationals with no rational limit ($\mathbb Q$ is not complete). (IV) is false: $a_n=(-1)^n$ has $|a_n|\to1$ but does not converge.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Sequences of Real Numbers

    Q7. The sum of the series $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)}$ is:
    1. $\dfrac12$
    2. $\dfrac13$
    3. $\dfrac16$
    4. $\dfrac14$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\dfrac14$.

    $$\begin{aligned}&\dfrac{1}{n(n+1)(n+2)} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac12\left[\dfrac{1}{n(n+1)}-\dfrac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}\right]\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ which telescopes: the sum is $\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac{1}{1\cdot2}=\dfrac14$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Series of Real Numbers

    Q8. The series $\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n(\ln n)^{p}}$ converges if and only if:
    1. $p\gt 1$
    2. $p\ge 1$
    3. $p\gt 0$
    4. $p\gt 2$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $p\gt 1$.

    By the integral test (or Cauchy's condensation test), compare with $$\begin{aligned}&\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}{x(\ln x)^p} \\[4pt] &\quad = \int_{\ln2}^\infty\frac{du}{u^p}\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ which converges if and only if $p\gt1$. At $p=1$ the series $\sum\tfrac{1}{n\ln n}$ diverges, although very slowly.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Series of Real Numbers

    Q9. Consider the statements:(I) $\displaystyle\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt n}$ is conditionally convergent.(II) $\displaystyle\sum\sin\frac1n$ is convergent.(III) $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n}{2^n}=2$.(IV) Every rearrangement of an absolutely convergent series converges to the same sum.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and III only
    2. II and IV only
    3. I, II and IV only
    4. I, III and IV only
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) I, III and IV only.

    (I) Converges by Leibniz's test, but $\sum\tfrac1{\sqrt n}$ diverges: true. (II) $\sin\tfrac1n\big/\tfrac1n\to1$, so by limit comparison with $\sum\tfrac1n$ it diverges: false. (III) $\sum nx^n=\dfrac{x}{(1-x)^2}$, which is 2 at $x=\tfrac12$: true. (IV) is Dirichlet's rearrangement theorem: true.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Series of Real Numbers

    Q10. The sum of the series $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^2}{2^n}$ is:
    1. 2
    2. 4
    3. 6
    4. 8
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 6.

    Differentiating the geometric series twice gives $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}n^2x^n=\frac{x(1+x)}{(1-x)^3}$ for $|x|\lt1$. At $x=\tfrac12$: $\dfrac{\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac32}{\tfrac18}=6$. (Probabilistically: it is $E(N^2)$ for a geometric variate with $p=\tfrac12$, i.e. $\operatorname{Var}+\text{mean}^2=2+4$.)

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Series of Real Numbers

    Q11. Given below are two statements, one labelled Assertion (A) and the other Reason (R).Assertion (A): The terms of the series $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$ can be rearranged so that the new series converges to any prescribed real number.Reason (R): The series is conditionally convergent.Choose the correct answer.
    1. Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
    2. Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
    3. (A) is true but (R) is false
    4. (A) is false but (R) is true
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

    The series converges (to $\ln 2$) by Leibniz's test, while $\sum\tfrac1n$ diverges, so it is conditionally convergent: (R) is true. Riemann's rearrangement theorem says exactly that a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to converge to any real number (or to diverge), so (A) is true and (R) is the reason.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Series of Real Numbers

    Q12. The radius of convergence of the power series $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n!}{n^n}\,x^n$ is:
    1. 1
    2. $e$
    3. $\dfrac1e$
    4. $\infty$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $e$.

    With $a_n=\dfrac{n!}{n^n}$, $$\begin{aligned}&\dfrac{a_n}{a_{n+1}} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{(n+1)^{n+1}}{(n+1)\,n^n} \\[4pt] &\quad = \left(1+\dfrac1n\right)^n \to e\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ So $R=e$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Power Series and Radius of Convergence

    Q13. The interval of convergence of $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(x-1)^n}{n\,3^n}$ is:
    1. $(-2,4)$
    2. $[-2,4]$
    3. $[-2,4)$
    4. $(-2,4]$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $[-2,4)$.

    The radius is 3, so the series converges for $|x-1|\lt3$. At $x=4$ it is $\sum\tfrac1n$, divergent; at $x=-2$ it is $\sum\tfrac{(-1)^n}{n}$, convergent by Leibniz's test. The interval is $[-2,4)$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Power Series and Radius of Convergence

    Q14. Which of the following functions is uniformly continuous on the open interval $(0,1)$?
    1. $\sin\dfrac1x$
    2. $\dfrac1x$
    3. $x\sin\dfrac1x$
    4. $\ln x$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $x\sin\dfrac1x$.

    A continuous function on $(0,1)$ is uniformly continuous there if and only if it has finite limits at both end points. $x\sin\tfrac1x\to0$ as $x\to0^+$, so it extends continuously to $[0,1]$. $\sin\tfrac1x$ oscillates and has no limit at 0; $\tfrac1x$ and $\ln x$ are unbounded near 0.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Functions of a Real Variable

    Q15. Let $f(x)=x$ if $x$ is rational and $f(x)=0$ if $x$ is irrational. Then $f$ is continuous:
    1. only at $x=0$
    2. at every rational point
    3. at every irrational point
    4. nowhere
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) only at $x=0$.

    $|f(x)|\le|x|$, so $f(x)\to0=f(0)$ as $x\to0$: continuous at 0. At $c\ne0$, sequences of rationals and of irrationals tending to $c$ give the limits $c$ and $0$, which differ, so $f$ is discontinuous at every $c\ne0$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Functions of a Real Variable

    Q16. Consider the statements about uniform convergence as $n\to\infty$:(I) $f_n(x)=x^n$ converges uniformly on $\left[0,\tfrac12\right]$.(II) $f_n(x)=x^n$ converges uniformly on $[0,1]$.(III) $g_n(x)=\dfrac{x}{1+nx^2}$ converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$.(IV) $h_n(x)=\dfrac{nx}{1+n^2x^2}$ converges uniformly on $[0,1]$.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I only
    2. I and III only
    3. I, III and IV only
    4. II and IV only
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) I and III only.

    (I) $\sup|x^n|=2^{-n}\to0$: true. (II) The pointwise limit is 0 on $[0,1)$ and 1 at 1, discontinuous, so the convergence of continuous functions cannot be uniform: false. (III) $|g_n|$ is largest at $x=\pm\tfrac1{\sqrt n}$, where it equals $\tfrac1{2\sqrt n}\to0$: true. (IV) $h_n\to0$ pointwise but $h_n\left(\tfrac1n\right)=\tfrac12$ for every $n$: false.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Functions of a Real Variable

    Q17. For $f(x)=x^3-3x$ on $[0,2]$, the point $c\in(0,2)$ given by Lagrange's mean value theorem is:
    1. 1
    2. $\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$
    3. $\sqrt{\dfrac23}$
    4. $\dfrac{2}{\sqrt3}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\dfrac{2}{\sqrt3}$.

    $\dfrac{f(2)-f(0)}{2-0}=\dfrac{2-0}{2}=1$. Solve $f'(c)=3c^2-3=1$: $c^2=\tfrac43$, $c=\tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}\approx1.155$, which lies in $(0,2)$. ($c=1$ is where $f'=0$, the point Rolle's theorem would give on $[0,\sqrt3]$.)

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Differentiability and Mean Value Theorems

    Q18. The number of real roots of the equation $e^x=1+x+\dfrac{x^2}{2}$ is:
    1. exactly one
    2. exactly two
    3. exactly three
    4. zero
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) exactly one.

    Let $g(x)=e^x-1-x-\tfrac{x^2}2$. Then $g'(x)=e^x-1-x\ge0$, with equality only at $x=0$, so $g$ is strictly increasing and has at most one zero. $g(0)=0$, so $x=0$ is the only root. (By Taylor's theorem $g(x)=\tfrac{x^3}{6}e^{\xi}$, which has the sign of $x$.)

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Differentiability and Mean Value Theorems

    Q19. The coefficient of $x^4$ in the Maclaurin expansion of $e^x\cos x$ is:
    1. $\dfrac1{24}$
    2. $-\dfrac16$
    3. $-\dfrac1{12}$
    4. $0$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $-\dfrac16$.

    $e^x\cos x=\operatorname{Re}\,e^{(1+i)x}$, so the coefficient of $x^4$ is $\operatorname{Re}\dfrac{(1+i)^4}{4!}$. $(1+i)^2=2i$, $(1+i)^4=-4$, giving $-\tfrac{4}{24}=-\tfrac16$. The expansion is $1+x-\tfrac{x^3}{3}-\tfrac{x^4}{6}-\cdots$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Differentiability and Mean Value Theorems

    Q20. Let $f(x)=x^2\sin\dfrac1x$ for $x\ne0$ and $f(0)=0$. Consider the statements:(I) $f$ is differentiable at every point of $\mathbb R$.(II) $f'$ is continuous at 0.(III) $f'$ is bounded on $[-1,1]$.(IV) $f$ is of bounded variation on $[0,1]$.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I only
    2. I and II only
    3. I and III only
    4. I, III and IV only
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) I, III and IV only.

    $f'(0)=\lim_{h\to0}h\sin\tfrac1h=0$ and for $x\ne0$, $f'(x)=2x\sin\tfrac1x-\cos\tfrac1x$: (I) true. $\cos\tfrac1x$ has no limit at 0, so $f'$ is not continuous there: (II) false. $|f'(x)|\le 2|x|+1\le3$ on $[-1,1]$: (III) true. A function with a bounded derivative is Lipschitz, hence of bounded variation: (IV) true.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Differentiability and Mean Value Theorems

    Q21. The value of $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-x^2}\,dx$ is:
    1. $\dfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2}$
    2. $\sqrt\pi$
    3. $\dfrac{\sqrt\pi}{4}$
    4. $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $\dfrac{\sqrt\pi}{4}$.

    Put $u=x^2$: the integral is $$\begin{aligned}&\dfrac12\int_0^\infty u^{1/2}e^{-u}du \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac12\Gamma\left(\tfrac32\right) = \dfrac12\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{\sqrt\pi}{4}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Over the whole line the integral is twice this, $\tfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2}$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Riemann Integration and Improper Integrals

    Q22. The improper integral $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{a-1}}{1+x}\,dx$ converges if and only if:
    1. $a\gt0$
    2. $0\lt a\lt1$
    3. $a\lt1$
    4. $0\lt a\lt2$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $0\lt a\lt1$.

    Near 0 the integrand behaves like $x^{a-1}$, integrable if and only if $a\gt0$. Near $\infty$ it behaves like $x^{a-2}$, integrable if and only if $a\lt1$. Both are needed: $0\lt a\lt1$, and then the value is $\dfrac{\pi}{\sin a\pi}=B(a,1-a)$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Riemann Integration and Improper Integrals

    Q23. $\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{n}{n^2+k^2}$ equals:
    1. $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$
    2. $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$
    3. $\ln 2$
    4. $1$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$.

    $$\begin{aligned}&\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{n}{n^2+k^2} \\[4pt] &\quad = \frac1n\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1+(k/n)^2}\end{aligned}$$ is a Riemann sum of $\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}$ on $[0,1]$, so the limit is $\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\tan^{-1}1=\frac\pi4$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Riemann Integration and Improper Integrals

    Q24. Consider the statements about Riemann integrability on $[a,b]$:(I) The function that is 1 at rational points and 0 at irrational points is not Riemann integrable.(II) Every monotone function is Riemann integrable.(III) Every bounded function with finitely many discontinuities is Riemann integrable.(IV) If $|f|$ is Riemann integrable, then $f$ is Riemann integrable.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I, II and III only
    2. I and III only
    3. II, III and IV only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) I, II and III only.

    (I) Every upper sum is $b-a$ and every lower sum is 0. (II) and (III) are standard theorems (a bounded function is Riemann integrable if and only if its discontinuities form a set of measure zero). (IV) is false: take $f=1$ on rationals and $-1$ on irrationals; $|f|\equiv1$ is integrable but $f$ is not. The converse of (IV) is the true statement.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Riemann Integration and Improper Integrals

    Q25. If $F(x)=\displaystyle\int_{x}^{x^2}e^{-t^2}\,dt$, then $F'(1)$ equals:
    1. $\dfrac2e$
    2. $0$
    3. $\dfrac3e$
    4. $\dfrac1e$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\dfrac1e$.

    By Leibniz's rule $F'(x)=2x\,e^{-x^4}-e^{-x^2}$. At $x=1$: $\dfrac2e-\dfrac1e=\dfrac1e$. Forgetting the lower limit gives $\tfrac2e$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Riemann Integration and Improper Integrals

    Q26. The value of $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4\theta\,\cos^2\theta\,d\theta$ is:
    1. $\dfrac{\pi}{16}$
    2. $\dfrac{3\pi}{32}$
    3. $\dfrac{\pi}{32}$
    4. $\dfrac{\pi}{8}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $\dfrac{\pi}{32}$.

    $$\begin{aligned}&\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{2m-1}\theta\cos^{2n-1}\theta\,d\theta \\[4pt] &\quad = \tfrac12B(m,n)\end{aligned}$$ with $m=\tfrac52$, $n=\tfrac32$: $$\begin{aligned}&\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac{\Gamma(5/2)\Gamma(3/2)}{\Gamma(4)} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac12\cdot\dfrac{\tfrac34\sqrt\pi\cdot\tfrac12\sqrt\pi}{6} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{\pi}{32}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Riemann Integration and Improper Integrals

    Q27. Let $f(x,y)=\dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2}$ for $(x,y)\ne(0,0)$ and $f(0,0)=0$. At the origin:
    1. $f$ is continuous but the partial derivatives do not exist
    2. both partial derivatives exist but $f$ is not continuous
    3. $f$ is continuous and both partial derivatives exist
    4. $f$ is differentiable
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) both partial derivatives exist but $f$ is not continuous.

    $f(x,0)=0$ and $f(0,y)=0$ for all $x,y$, so $f_x(0,0)=f_y(0,0)=0$ exist. Along $y=x$, $f=\tfrac12$ for all $x\ne0$, while along the axes $f=0$; the limit at the origin does not exist, so $f$ is not continuous, and therefore not differentiable. Existence of partial derivatives does not imply continuity.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Functions of Two Real Variables

    Q28. The function $f(x,y)=x^3+y^3-3xy$ has:
    1. a local maximum at $(1,1)$ and a saddle point at $(0,0)$
    2. local minima at both $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$
    3. a saddle point at $(1,1)$ and a local minimum at $(0,0)$
    4. a local minimum at $(1,1)$ and a saddle point at $(0,0)$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) a local minimum at $(1,1)$ and a saddle point at $(0,0)$.

    $f_x=3x^2-3y=0$, $f_y=3y^2-3x=0$ give $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$. With $f_{xx}=6x$, $f_{yy}=6y$, $f_{xy}=-3$: at $(1,1)$, $f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2=36-9\gt0$ and $f_{xx}\gt0$, a local minimum with $f=-1$; at $(0,0)$ the discriminant is $-9\lt0$, a saddle point.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Functions of Two Real Variables

    Q29. The value of $\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_x^1e^{y^2}\,dy\,dx$ is:
    1. $\dfrac{e-1}{2}$
    2. $e-1$
    3. $\dfrac e2$
    4. $\dfrac{e+1}{2}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $\dfrac{e-1}{2}$.

    $e^{y^2}$ has no elementary antiderivative, so change the order: the region is $0\le x\le y\le1$, and the integral is $$\begin{aligned}\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^ye^{y^2}dx\,dy &= \int_0^1ye^{y^2}dy \\[4pt] &= \left[\tfrac12e^{y^2}\right]_0^1 = \frac{e-1}{2}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Functions of Two Real Variables

    Q30. The maximum value of $xy$ subject to $\dfrac{x^2}{8}+\dfrac{y^2}{2}=1$ is:
    1. 1
    2. 4
    3. 2
    4. $2\sqrt2$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 2.

    Lagrange: $y=\lambda\tfrac x4$, $x=\lambda y$ give $y^2=\tfrac{x^2}{4}$, and the constraint then gives $x^2=4$, $y^2=1$: $xy=2$ at $(2,1)$ and $(-2,-1)$. Or put $x=2\sqrt2\cos\theta$, $y=\sqrt2\sin\theta$: $xy=2\sin2\theta\le2$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Functions of Two Real Variables

    Q31. The value of $\displaystyle\iint_{x^2+y^2\le1}\frac{dx\,dy}{1+x^2+y^2}$ is:
    1. $\dfrac{\pi}{2}\ln2$
    2. $2\pi\ln2$
    3. $\dfrac{\pi^2}{4}$
    4. $\pi\ln2$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\pi\ln2$.

    In polar coordinates the Jacobian is $r$: $$\begin{aligned}&\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1\frac{r\,dr\,d\theta}{1+r^2} \\[4pt] &\quad = 2\pi\cdot\left[\tfrac12\ln(1+r^2)\right]_0^1 \\[4pt] &\quad = \pi\ln2\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Functions of Two Real Variables

    Q32. The dimension of the real vector space of all $3\times3$ real symmetric matrices with trace zero is:
    1. 6
    2. 5
    3. 8
    4. 3
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) 5.

    A $3\times3$ symmetric matrix has $\tfrac{3\cdot4}{2}=6$ free entries. Trace zero is one non-trivial linear condition, which lowers the dimension by one: $6-1=5$. (All $3\times3$ matrices with trace zero form a space of dimension 8; the skew-symmetric ones, dimension 3.)

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Vector Spaces, Span, Linear Independence, Basis

    Q33. $W_1$ and $W_2$ are subspaces of $\mathbb R^5$ with $\dim W_1=3$ and $\dim W_2=4$. The possible values of $\dim(W_1\cap W_2)$ are:
    1. 1, 2 or 3
    2. only 2
    3. 2 or 3
    4. only 3
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 2 or 3.

    $$\begin{aligned}&\dim(W_1\cap W_2) \\[4pt] &\quad = \dim W_1+\dim W_2-\dim(W_1+W_2) \\[4pt] &\quad = 7-\dim(W_1+W_2)\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Since $W_2\subseteq W_1+W_2\subseteq\mathbb R^5$, $\dim(W_1+W_2)$ is 4 or 5, so the intersection has dimension 3 (when $W_1\subseteq W_2$) or 2.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Vector Spaces, Span, Linear Independence, Basis

    Q34. The vectors $(1,2,3)$, $(2,3,4)$ and $(3,5,k)$ of $\mathbb R^3$ are linearly dependent when $k$ equals:
    1. 7
    2. 6
    3. 8
    4. 5
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 7.

    $(1,2,3)+(2,3,4)=(3,5,7)$, so for $k=7$ the third vector is the sum of the first two. Check by the determinant: $$\begin{aligned}&\begin{vmatrix}1&2&3\\2&3&4\\3&5&k\end{vmatrix} \\[4pt] &\quad = 7-k\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ which vanishes only for $k=7$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Vector Spaces, Span, Linear Independence, Basis

    Q35. $A$ is a real $4\times5$ matrix of rank 3. The dimensions of the solution spaces of $Ax=0$ and of $A^{T}y=0$ are, respectively:
    1. 1 and 2
    2. 2 and 2
    3. 3 and 1
    4. 2 and 1
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) 2 and 1.

    Rank–nullity: $Ax=0$ has $x\in\mathbb R^5$, so the nullity is $5-3=2$. $A^T$ is $5\times4$ with the same rank 3 and $y\in\mathbb R^4$, so its nullity is $4-3=1$.

    Q36. The rank of the $4\times4$ matrix whose diagonal entries are all $-3$ and whose off-diagonal entries are all $1$ is:
    1. 4
    2. 3
    3. 2
    4. 1
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) 3.

    The matrix is $J-4I$, where $J$ is the matrix of ones, with eigenvalues $4,0,0,0$. So $J-4I$ has eigenvalues $0,-4,-4,-4$: exactly one zero eigenvalue, and since the matrix is symmetric its rank is $3$. (Each row sums to zero, which shows directly that it is singular.)

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Rank, Nullity, Row Reduced Echelon Form

    Q37. For real matrices $A$ and $B$ of suitable orders, consider the statements:(I) $\operatorname{rank}(AB)\le\min\{\operatorname{rank}A,\operatorname{rank}B\}$.(II) $\operatorname{rank}(A+B)\le\operatorname{rank}A+\operatorname{rank}B$.(III) $\operatorname{rank}(A^{T}A)=\operatorname{rank}A$.(IV) $\operatorname{rank}(AB)=\operatorname{rank}(BA)$ whenever $A$ and $B$ are square of the same order.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I, II and III only
    2. I and II only
    3. I, III and IV only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) I, II and III only.

    (I) and (II) are standard. (III) $$\begin{aligned}A^TAx &= 0 \Rightarrow x^TA^TAx = \|Ax\|^2 = 0 \\[4pt] &\Rightarrow Ax = 0\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ so $A^TA$ and $A$ have the same null space. (IV) is false: with $$\begin{aligned}&A \\[4pt] &\quad = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\end{aligned}$$ and $$\begin{aligned}&B \\[4pt] &\quad = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ $AB=0$ but $BA=A$ has rank 1.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Rank, Nullity, Row Reduced Echelon Form

    Q38. If $A$ is a $3\times3$ matrix with $\det A=2$, then $\det\big(\operatorname{adj}(2A)\big)$ equals:
    1. 16
    2. 32
    3. 64
    4. 256
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) 256.

    For an $n\times n$ matrix, $\operatorname{adj}(kA)=k^{n-1}\operatorname{adj}A$ and $\det(\operatorname{adj}A)=(\det A)^{n-1}$. So $\operatorname{adj}(2A)=4\operatorname{adj}A$ and $\det=4^3(\det A)^2=64\cdot4=256$. Equivalently $\det(2A)=16$ and $16^2=256$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Trace, Determinant, Inverse

    Q39. A $3\times3$ matrix $A$ has characteristic roots 1, 2 and 3. Then $\det(A^2-A+I)$ equals:
    1. 6
    2. 36
    3. 21
    4. 11
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 21.

    If $\lambda$ is a characteristic root of $A$, then $\lambda^2-\lambda+1$ is one of $A^2-A+I$. These are $1,3,7$, and the determinant is their product, $21$. (The trace would be their sum, 11.)

    Q40. The system $x+y+z=6$, $\;x+2y+3z=10$, $\;x+2y+\lambda z=\mu$ has no solution when:
    1. $\lambda\ne3$
    2. $\lambda=3$ and $\mu\ne10$
    3. $\lambda=3$ and $\mu=10$
    4. $\lambda\ne3$ and $\mu=10$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\lambda=3$ and $\mu\ne10$.

    Subtracting the second equation from the third gives $(\lambda-3)z=\mu-10$. If $\lambda\ne3$ there is a unique solution; if $\lambda=3$ and $\mu=10$ the third equation repeats the second and there are infinitely many; if $\lambda=3$ and $\mu\ne10$ the ranks of the coefficient and augmented matrices are 2 and 3, so the system is inconsistent.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Systems of Linear Equations

    Q41. The Gram–Schmidt process is applied to $v_1=(1,1,0)$, $v_2=(1,0,1)$ in that order. The second vector of the orthonormal set obtained is:
    1. $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt6}(1,-1,2)$
    2. $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1,0)$
    3. $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1,-1,1)$
    4. $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt6}(1,1,-2)$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt6}(1,-1,2)$.

    $u_1=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1,0)$. $$\begin{aligned}w_2 &= v_2-(v_2\cdot u_1)u_1 \\[4pt] &= (1,0,1)-\tfrac12(1,1,0) \\[4pt] &= \left(\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,1\right)\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ with $\|w_2\|^2=\tfrac32$. So $u_2=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt6}(1,-1,2)$; check $u_2\cdot u_1=0$. Option D is also orthogonal to $u_1$ but does not lie in the span of $v_1,v_2$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Gram–Schmidt Orthogonalization

    Q42. If $A=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}$, then the trace of $A^3$ is:
    1. 343
    2. 91
    3. 133
    4. 1000
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 133.

    The characteristic equation is $\lambda^2-7\lambda+10=0$, so the characteristic roots are 2 and 5. Those of $A^3$ are $8$ and $125$, and the trace is their sum, $133$. ($343=7^3$ is the cube of the trace; $1000=\det(A^3)$.)

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Characteristic Roots and Vectors

    Q43. $A$ is a real $3\times3$ matrix with $A^2=A$ and rank 2. Then $\det(I+A)$ equals:
    1. 2
    2. 4
    3. 8
    4. 1
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) 4.

    The characteristic roots of an idempotent matrix are 0 or 1, and it is diagonalisable with rank equal to trace, so the roots are $1,1,0$. Those of $I+A$ are $2,2,1$ and the determinant is $4$.

    Q44. For $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}$, the Cayley–Hamilton theorem gives $A^{-1}$ as:
    1. $\dfrac12(5I-A)$
    2. $\dfrac12(A+5I)$
    3. $A-5I$
    4. $\dfrac12(A-5I)$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\dfrac12(A-5I)$.

    The characteristic equation is $\lambda^2-5\lambda-2=0$, so $A^2-5A-2I=0$, i.e. $A(A-5I)=2I$ and $$\begin{aligned}A^{-1} &= \tfrac12(A-5I) \\[4pt] &= \begin{pmatrix}-2&1\\\tfrac32&-\tfrac12\end{pmatrix}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

    Q45. Let $A$ be a real $n\times n$ matrix with $A^3=A$. Consider the statements:(I) $A$ is diagonalisable over $\mathbb R$.(II) Every characteristic root of $A$ is $0$, $1$ or $-1$.(III) $A$ is non-singular.(IV) The trace of $A$ is an integer.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and II only
    2. II and IV only
    3. I, II and IV only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) I, II and IV only.

    $A$ satisfies $x^3-x=x(x-1)(x+1)$, so its minimal polynomial divides a product of distinct linear factors: $A$ is diagonalisable with roots in $\{0,1,-1\}$, and the trace, a sum of such numbers, is an integer. (III) is false: $A=0$ satisfies $A^3=A$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Cayley–Hamilton Theorem

    Q46. Consider the statements about real square matrices:(I) The characteristic roots of a symmetric matrix are real.(II) A skew-symmetric matrix of odd order is singular.(III) Every characteristic root of an orthogonal matrix has modulus 1.(IV) The product of two symmetric matrices is symmetric.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I, II and III only
    2. I and III only
    3. II, III and IV only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) I, II and III only.

    (I) and (III) are standard. (II) $$\begin{aligned}\det A &= \det A^T = \det(-A) \\[4pt] &= (-1)^n\det A\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ so $\det A=0$ for odd $n$. (IV) is false: $(AB)^T=B^TA^T=BA$, which equals $AB$ only when $A$ and $B$ commute.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Symmetric, Skew-symmetric, Orthogonal Matrices

    Q47. If $A$ is a real skew-symmetric matrix, then $(I-A)(I+A)^{-1}$ is:
    1. symmetric
    2. orthogonal
    3. skew-symmetric
    4. idempotent
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) orthogonal.

    The characteristic roots of $A$ are zero or purely imaginary, so $I+A$ is non-singular. With $P=(I-A)(I+A)^{-1}$ and $A^T=-A$: $P^T=(I-A)^{-1}(I+A)$, and since $I-A$ and $I+A$ commute, $$\begin{aligned}&P^TP \\[4pt] &\quad = (I-A)^{-1}(I+A)(I-A)(I+A)^{-1} \\[4pt] &\quad = I\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ This is Cayley's transform.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Symmetric, Skew-symmetric, Orthogonal Matrices

    Q48. The quadratic form $x^2+y^2+z^2+2a(xy+yz+zx)$ is positive definite if and only if:
    1. $-1\lt a\lt1$
    2. $a\lt1$
    3. $-\dfrac12\lt a\lt1$
    4. $a\gt-\dfrac12$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $-\dfrac12\lt a\lt1$.

    Its matrix is $(1-a)I+aJ$, with characteristic roots $1+2a$ (once) and $1-a$ (twice). All are positive if and only if $-\tfrac12\lt a\lt1$. The leading principal minors give the same: $1\gt0$, $1-a^2\gt0$, $(1-a)^2(1+2a)\gt0$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit II, Positive Definite Matrices and Quadratic Forms

    Q49. The rank and the signature of the quadratic form $2xy+2yz+2zx$ are, respectively:
    1. 2 and 0
    2. 3 and 1
    3. 3 and 3
    4. 3 and $-1$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) 3 and $-1$.

    The matrix is $J-I$ (zero diagonal, ones elsewhere) with characteristic roots $2,-1,-1$. All three are non-zero, so the rank is 3; there is one positive and two negative roots, so the signature is $1-2=-1$ and the form is indefinite.

    Q50. Given below are two statements, one labelled Assertion (A) and the other Reason (R).Assertion (A): If a real symmetric matrix $A$ is positive definite, then $A^{-1}$ is positive definite.Reason (R): The characteristic roots of $A^{-1}$ are the reciprocals of the characteristic roots of $A$.Choose the correct answer.
    1. Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
    2. Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
    3. (A) is true but (R) is false
    4. (A) is false but (R) is true

    Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

    A symmetric matrix is positive definite if and only if all its characteristic roots are positive. $A^{-1}$ is symmetric, and its roots are $1/\lambda_i\gt0$. So both statements are true and (R) is the reason for (A).
