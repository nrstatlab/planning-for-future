    Model Questions — Unit I: Probability and Distributions

    UGC NET Statistics (Code 107) · 50 questions · Click "Show Answer" for the worked solution

    About this set

    Events and Bayes' theorem, random variables, moments and generating functions, the standard distributions, joint distributions, sampling distributions, convergence, laws of large numbers and the central limit theorem.

    These are model questions written for practice at the standard of the paper; they are not questions from a past paper. Try each one before opening the answer. Every answer gives the working and a link to the section of the Unit I notes that teaches it.

    The questions

    Q1. For two events $A$ and $B$, $P(A)=0.5$, $P(B)=0.6$ and $P(A\cup B)=0.8$. Consider the statements:(I) $A$ and $B$ are independent.(II) $P(A^c\cap B^c)=0.2$.(III) $P(A\mid B^c)=0.4$.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I only
    2. I and II only
    3. II and III only
    4. I, II and III
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) I and II only.

    $$\begin{aligned}P(A\cap B) &= 0.5+0.6-0.8 = 0.3 \\[4pt] &= P(A)P(B)\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ so $A$ and $B$ are independent: (I) is true. $P(A^c\cap B^c)=1-P(A\cup B)=0.2$: (II) is true. Since $A$ and $B^c$ are also independent, $P(A\mid B^c)=P(A)=0.5$, not $0.4$ (directly: $P(A\cap B^c)/P(B^c)=0.2/0.4$): (III) is false.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Basic Concepts of Probability

    Q2. Four letters are placed at random into four addressed envelopes, one letter in each. The probability that no letter goes into its own envelope is:
    1. $\dfrac38$
    2. $\dfrac14$
    3. $\dfrac13$
    4. $\dfrac{5}{12}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $\dfrac38$.

    The number of derangements of 4 objects is $$\begin{aligned}&4!\left(1-\tfrac1{1!}+\tfrac1{2!}-\tfrac1{3!}+\tfrac1{4!}\right) \\[4pt] &\quad = 24\left(\tfrac12-\tfrac16+\tfrac1{24}\right) = 9\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ So the probability is $9/24=3/8$. (By inclusion and exclusion, $$\begin{aligned}&P(\text{at least one match}) \\[4pt] &\quad = 1-\tfrac12+\tfrac16-\tfrac1{24} = \tfrac58\text{.}\end{aligned}$$)

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Basic Concepts of Probability

    Q3. Machines $M_1$, $M_2$ and $M_3$ make 50%, 30% and 20% of a factory's output, and 2%, 3% and 5% of their respective outputs are defective. An item drawn at random is found defective. The probability that it was made by $M_3$ is:
    1. $\dfrac{9}{29}$
    2. $\dfrac25$
    3. $\dfrac{10}{29}$
    4. $\dfrac15$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $\dfrac{10}{29}$.

    $$\begin{aligned}&P(D) \\[4pt] &\quad = 0.5(0.02)+0.3(0.03)+0.2(0.05) \\[4pt] &\quad = 0.010+0.009+0.010 \\[4pt] &\quad = 0.029\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ By Bayes' theorem $$\begin{aligned}P(M_3\mid D) &= 0.010/0.029 \\[4pt] &= 10/29\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Note that $M_1$ has the same posterior, $10/29$, and $M_2$ has $9/29$; $1/5$ is only the prior of $M_3$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Conditional Probability and Bayes' Theorem

    Q4. A fair die is rolled repeatedly until a six appears. The probability that the first six appears on an even-numbered roll is:
    1. $\dfrac12$
    2. $\dfrac{6}{11}$
    3. $\dfrac{5}{12}$
    4. $\dfrac{5}{11}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\dfrac{5}{11}$.

    With $p=\tfrac16$, $q=\tfrac56$: $$\begin{aligned}P(\text{even}) &= qp+q^3p+q^5p+\cdots \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{qp}{1-q^2} = \dfrac{q}{1+q} \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{5/6}{11/6} = \dfrac{5}{11}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ The odd-numbered rolls have the remaining $6/11$, which is larger because the first roll is odd.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Conditional Probability and Bayes' Theorem

    Q5. Let $\{A_n\}$ be independent events with $P(A_n)=\dfrac1n$ and let $\{B_n\}$ be any events with $P(B_n)=\dfrac1{n^2}$. Then $P(A_n\text{ occurs infinitely often})$ and $P(B_n\text{ occurs infinitely often})$ are, respectively:
    1. 1 and 0
    2. 0 and 0
    3. 1 and 1
    4. 0 and 1
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 1 and 0.

    Borel–Cantelli. $\sum P(B_n)=\sum 1/n^2\lt\infty$, so $P(\limsup B_n)=0$ whatever the dependence. $\sum P(A_n)=\sum 1/n=\infty$ and the $A_n$ are independent, so by the second lemma $P(\limsup A_n)=1$. Independence is needed only for the second lemma.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Basic Concepts of Probability

    Q6. A random variable $X$ has distribution function $$F(x)=\begin{cases}0,& x\lt 0\\[2pt] \dfrac{x+1}{4},& 0\le x\lt 1\\[4pt] \dfrac34,& 1\le x\lt 2\\[2pt] 1,& x\ge 2.\end{cases}$$ Then $P\left(\tfrac12\lt X\le 2\right)$ and $P(X=1)$ are, respectively:
    1. $\dfrac58$ and $0$
    2. $\dfrac12$ and $\dfrac14$
    3. $\dfrac58$ and $\dfrac14$
    4. $\dfrac34$ and $\dfrac12$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $\dfrac58$ and $\dfrac14$.

    $$\begin{aligned}P(\tfrac12\lt X\le 2) &= F(2)-F(\tfrac12) \\[4pt] &= 1-\tfrac38 = \tfrac58\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ The jump at 1 is $$\begin{aligned}P(X=1) &= F(1)-F(1^-) \\[4pt] &= \tfrac34-\tfrac24 = \tfrac14\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ $X$ is of mixed type: it also has jumps of $\tfrac14$ at 0 and at 2, and a density $\tfrac14$ on $(0,1)$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Random Variables and Distribution Functions

    Q7. Let $X$ have density $f(x)=3x^2$, $0\lt x\lt 1$. Then $P\left(X\gt\tfrac12\ \middle|\ X\gt\tfrac14\right)$ equals:
    1. $\dfrac78$
    2. $\dfrac89$
    3. $\dfrac{63}{64}$
    4. $\dfrac12$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\dfrac89$.

    $F(x)=x^3$, so $P(X\gt\tfrac12)=1-\tfrac18=\tfrac78$ and $P(X\gt\tfrac14)=1-\tfrac1{64}=\tfrac{63}{64}$. The conditional probability is $\dfrac{7/8}{63/64}=\dfrac{8}{9}$. The other options are the two unconditional probabilities.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Random Variables and Distribution Functions

    Q8. If $X\sim N(0,4)$, then $E|X|$ equals:
    1. $\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{\pi}}$
    2. $2$
    3. $\dfrac{4}{\sqrt\pi}$
    4. $2\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{\pi}}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $2\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{\pi}}$.

    For $Z\sim N(0,1)$, $E|Z|=2\displaystyle\int_0^\infty z\,\phi(z)\,dz=\sqrt{2/\pi}$. Here $X=2Z$, so $E|X|=2\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx 1.596$. In general the mean deviation about the mean of $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ is $\sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx 0.798\,\sigma$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Expectation and Moments

    Q9. A random variable $X$ takes the values $1,2,3,\dots$ and satisfies $P(X\ge k)=\dfrac1{k^2}$ for $k=1,2,\dots$ Then $E(X)$ is:
    1. $\dfrac{\pi^2}{6}$
    2. $2$
    3. infinite
    4. $\dfrac{\pi^2}{6}-1$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $\dfrac{\pi^2}{6}$.

    For a random variable taking positive integer values, $$\begin{aligned}E(X) &= \sum_{k\ge1}P(X\ge k) \\[4pt] &= \sum_{k\ge1}\dfrac1{k^2} = \dfrac{\pi^2}{6}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ The mean is finite; it is $E(X^2)=\sum(2k-1)P(X\ge k)$ that diverges, so the variance does not exist.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Expectation and Moments

    Q10. The number of claims $N$ in a year is Poisson with mean 4. The claim amounts $X_1,X_2,\dots$ are independent of $N$ and of each other, each with mean 2 and variance 3. The variance of the total claim $S=X_1+\cdots+X_N$ is:
    1. 12
    2. 16
    3. 28
    4. 24
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 28.

    $$\begin{aligned}&\operatorname{Var}(S) \\[4pt] &\quad = E(N)\operatorname{Var}(X)+\operatorname{Var}(N)[E(X)]^2 \\[4pt] &\quad = 4(3)+4(4) = 28\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ For a Poisson count this is $\lambda E(X^2)=4(3+4)$. The options 12 and 16 are the two parts taken separately.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Expectation and Moments

    Q11. The moment generating function of $X$ is $M(t)=(1-2t)^{-3}$, $t\lt\tfrac12$. Consider the statements:(I) $X$ has the chi-square distribution with 6 degrees of freedom.(II) $\operatorname{Var}(X)=12$.(III) The mode of $X$ is 4.(IV) $E\left(\dfrac1X\right)=\dfrac16$.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and II only
    2. I, II and III only
    3. II, III and IV only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) I, II and III only.

    $(1-2t)^{-k/2}$ is the MGF of $\chi^2_k$; here $k=6$: (I) true. $\operatorname{Var}=2k=12$: (II) true. The mode of $\chi^2_k$ is $k-2=4$: (III) true. $E(1/X)=\dfrac1{k-2}=\dfrac14$, not $\dfrac16$ (the reciprocal of the mean is not the mean of the reciprocal): (IV) false.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Moment Generating Function (MGF)

    Q12. If $X$ has moment generating function $M(t)=\left(\dfrac{2}{2-t}\right)^{3}$, $t\lt 2$, then the coefficient of skewness $\gamma_1=\mu_3/\mu_2^{3/2}$ of $X$ is:
    1. $\dfrac23$
    2. $\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$
    3. $2$
    4. $\dfrac{2}{\sqrt3}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\dfrac{2}{\sqrt3}$.

    $X$ is gamma with shape $\alpha=3$ and rate 2. The cumulant generating function is $-3\ln(1-t/2)$, so $\kappa_r=3\,(r-1)!/2^r$: $\kappa_2=\tfrac34$, $\kappa_3=\tfrac34$. Hence $\gamma_1=\dfrac{3/4}{(3/4)^{3/2}}=\dfrac{2}{\sqrt3}$. For any gamma, $\gamma_1=2/\sqrt\alpha$ and the excess kurtosis is $6/\alpha=2$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Moment Generating Function (MGF)

    Q13. If $X$ is a Poisson variate with $P(X=1)=P(X=2)$, then $P(X=4)$ equals:
    1. $e^{-2}$
    2. $\dfrac{4}{3e^{2}}$
    3. $\dfrac{2}{3e^{2}}$
    4. $\dfrac{1}{3e^{2}}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $\dfrac{2}{3e^{2}}$.

    $\lambda e^{-\lambda}=\tfrac{\lambda^2}{2}e^{-\lambda}$ gives $\lambda=2$. Then $$\begin{aligned}P(X=4) &= e^{-2}\dfrac{2^4}{4!} \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{16}{24}e^{-2} = \dfrac{2}{3e^{2}}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ ($P(X=3)=\tfrac{4}{3}e^{-2}$ is option B.)

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Standard Discrete Distributions

    Q14. Let $X$ be the number of independent trials needed to get the first success, the probability of success in each trial being $\tfrac13$. Then $P(X\gt 5\mid X\gt 2)$ is:
    1. $\dfrac{8}{27}$
    2. $\dfrac{32}{243}$
    3. $\dfrac{4}{9}$
    4. $\dfrac{19}{27}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $\dfrac{8}{27}$.

    $P(X\gt k)=q^k$ with $q=\tfrac23$. By the lack-of-memory property $$\begin{aligned}P(X\gt 5\mid X\gt 2) &= P(X\gt 3) \\[4pt] &= \left(\tfrac23\right)^3 = \tfrac{8}{27}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ $\tfrac{32}{243}$ is the unconditional $P(X\gt5)$ and $\tfrac{19}{27}$ is $P(X\le 3)$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Standard Discrete Distributions

    Q15. A lot of 20 items contains 8 defectives. A sample of 5 items is drawn without replacement. The variance of the number of defectives in the sample is:
    1. $\dfrac65$
    2. $\dfrac{24}{19}$
    3. $\dfrac{15}{19}$
    4. $\dfrac{18}{19}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\dfrac{18}{19}$.

    Hypergeometric with $N=20$, $M=8$, $n=5$, $p=M/N=0.4$: $$\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Var} &= npq\,\dfrac{N-n}{N-1} \\[4pt] &= 5(0.4)(0.6)\dfrac{15}{19} \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{18}{19}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ $\tfrac65$ is the binomial variance, which holds for sampling with replacement.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Standard Discrete Distributions

    Q16. The mode(s) of the binomial distribution with $n=9$ and $p=0.4$ is/are:
    1. 4 only
    2. 3 and 4
    3. 3 only
    4. 4 and 5
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) 3 and 4.

    $(n+1)p=10(0.4)=4$ is an integer, so the distribution is bimodal with modes $(n+1)p-1=3$ and $(n+1)p=4$. Check: $$\begin{aligned}&\dfrac{P(X=4)}{P(X=3)} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{n-3}{4}\cdot\dfrac pq = \dfrac64\cdot\dfrac23 \\[4pt] &\quad = 1\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Standard Discrete Distributions

    Q17. $X$ and $Y$ are independent Poisson variates with means 2 and 3. The conditional variance of $X$ given $X+Y=5$ is:
    1. $2$
    2. $\dfrac54$
    3. $\dfrac65$
    4. $1$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $\dfrac65$.

    Given $X+Y=n$, $X$ is binomial with index $n$ and $p=\dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda+\mu}=\dfrac25$. With $n=5$: $E(X\mid X+Y=5)=2$ and $$\begin{aligned}&\operatorname{Var}(X\mid X+Y=5) \\[4pt] &\quad = 5\cdot\tfrac25\cdot\tfrac35 = \tfrac65\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Option A is the unconditional variance of $X$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Standard Discrete Distributions

    Q18. The lifetimes of three components are independent exponential variates, each with mean 6 hours. The expected time until all three have failed is:
    1. 2 hours
    2. 6 hours
    3. 9 hours
    4. 11 hours
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) 11 hours.

    With rate $\lambda=\tfrac16$ each, the waiting times between successive failures are exponential with rates $3\lambda,2\lambda,\lambda$, so $$\begin{aligned}E(X_{(3)}) &= \dfrac1\lambda\left(\tfrac13+\tfrac12+1\right) \\[4pt] &= 6\cdot\tfrac{11}{6} = 11\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ 2 hours is the expected time to the first failure.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Standard Continuous Distributions

    Q19. For a normal distribution, the ratio quartile deviation : mean deviation about the mean : standard deviation is approximately:
    1. 10 : 12 : 15
    2. 12 : 10 : 15
    3. 2 : 3 : 4
    4. 10 : 15 : 12
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 10 : 12 : 15.

    $\text{Q.D.}=0.6745\,\sigma\approx\tfrac23\sigma$ and $\text{M.D.}=\sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}=0.7979\,\sigma\approx\tfrac45\sigma$. So $$\begin{aligned}&\text{Q.D.}:\text{M.D.}:\text{S.D.} \\[4pt] &\quad = \tfrac23:\tfrac45:1 = 10:12:15\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Standard Continuous Distributions

    Q20. Let $X_1$ and $X_2$ be independent standard Cauchy variates. Consider the statements:(I) $E(X_1)$ does not exist.(II) $\dfrac{X_1+X_2}{2}$ is a standard Cauchy variate.(III) $\dfrac1{X_1}$ is a standard Cauchy variate.(IV) $X_1^2$ has the $F$ distribution with $(1,1)$ degrees of freedom.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and II only
    2. I, II and III only
    3. II, III and IV only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) I, II, III and IV.

    (I) $\int|x|\,\dfrac{dx}{\pi(1+x^2)}$ diverges. (II) The characteristic function is $e^{-|t|}$, and $\left(e^{-|t|/2}\right)^2=e^{-|t|}$. (III) A standard Cauchy is the ratio of two independent $N(0,1)$ variates, and the reciprocal of such a ratio is again one. (IV) Standard Cauchy is $t_1$, and $t_n^2\sim F(1,n)$. All four are true.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Standard Continuous Distributions

    Q21. $X$ and $Y$ are independent gamma variates with unit scale and shape parameters 2 and 3. The variance of $U=\dfrac{X}{X+Y}$ is:
    1. $\dfrac25$
    2. $\dfrac1{25}$
    3. $\dfrac{6}{25}$
    4. $\dfrac15$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\dfrac1{25}$.

    $U\sim\text{Beta}(2,3)$ (first kind), independently of $X+Y$. $$\begin{aligned}&\operatorname{Var}(U) \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{6}{25\cdot 6} = \dfrac1{25}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ $\tfrac25$ is its mean.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Standard Continuous Distributions

    Q22. Let $\ln X\sim N(0,1)$. Match the characteristics of $X$ in List I with their values in List II.
    List IList II
    (a) Mean(i) $1$
    (b) Median(ii) $e^{-1}$
    (c) Mode(iii) $e^{1/2}$
    (d) Variance(iv) $e(e-1)$
    1. (a)-(i), (b)-(iii), (c)-(ii), (d)-(iv)
    2. (a)-(iii), (b)-(ii), (c)-(i), (d)-(iv)
    3. (a)-(iii), (b)-(i), (c)-(ii), (d)-(iv)
    4. (a)-(iv), (b)-(i), (c)-(ii), (d)-(iii)
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) (a)-(iii), (b)-(i), (c)-(ii), (d)-(iv).

    For the lognormal with parameters $\mu,\sigma^2$: mean $e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}=e^{1/2}$; median $e^{\mu}=1$; mode $e^{\mu-\sigma^2}=e^{-1}$; variance $e^{2\mu+\sigma^2}(e^{\sigma^2}-1)=e(e-1)$. Mode $\lt$ median $\lt$ mean, as for any positively skewed distribution of this kind.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Standard Continuous Distributions

    Q23. The lifetime $T$ of a device has hazard rate $h(t)=2t$, $t\gt 0$. Then $E(T)$ is:
    1. $\dfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2}$
    2. $\dfrac12$
    3. $\sqrt\pi$
    4. $1$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $\dfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2}$.

    $S(t)=\exp\left(-\int_0^t 2u\,du\right)=e^{-t^2}$, a Weibull survival function with shape 2. $$\begin{aligned}E(T) &= \int_0^\infty S(t)\,dt = \int_0^\infty e^{-t^2}dt \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Standard Continuous Distributions

    Q24. $(X,Y)$ has joint density $f(x,y)=2$ for $0\lt x\lt y\lt 1$ and 0 elsewhere. The correlation coefficient between $X$ and $Y$ is:
    1. $\dfrac13$
    2. $\dfrac12$
    3. $\dfrac14$
    4. $\dfrac1{\sqrt2}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\dfrac12$.

    $f_X(x)=2(1-x)$, $f_Y(y)=2y$: $E(X)=\tfrac13$, $E(Y)=\tfrac23$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\operatorname{Var}(Y)=\tfrac1{18}$. $$\begin{aligned}E(XY) &= \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^y 2xy\,dx\,dy \\[4pt] &= \int_0^1 y^3dy = \tfrac14\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ so $\operatorname{Cov}=\tfrac14-\tfrac29=\tfrac1{36}$ and $\rho=\dfrac{1/36}{1/18}=\dfrac12$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Jointly Distributed Random Variables

    Q25. $(X,Y)$ is bivariate normal with $E(X)=E(Y)=0$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)=1$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=4$ and correlation $\rho=0.5$. Then $E(Y\mid X=1)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=1)$ are, respectively:
    1. $\dfrac14$ and $3$
    2. $1$ and $4$
    3. $2$ and $3$
    4. $1$ and $3$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $1$ and $3$.

    $$\begin{aligned}E(Y\mid X=x) &= \mu_Y+\rho\dfrac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}(x-\mu_X) \\[4pt] &= 0.5\cdot\dfrac21\cdot 1 = 1\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=x) &= \sigma_Y^2(1-\rho^2) \\[4pt] &= 4(0.75) = 3\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ which does not depend on $x$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Jointly Distributed Random Variables

    Q26. $(X,Y)$ has joint density $f(x,y)=e^{-y}$ for $0\lt x\lt y\lt\infty$ and 0 elsewhere. Then $P(Y\gt 2X)$ is:
    1. $\dfrac13$
    2. $\dfrac14$
    3. $\dfrac12$
    4. $\dfrac23$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $\dfrac12$.

    $$\begin{aligned}P(Y\gt 2X) &= \int_0^\infty\!\!\int_{2x}^\infty e^{-y}\,dy\,dx \\[4pt] &= \int_0^\infty e^{-2x}dx = \dfrac12\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Another way: given $Y=y$, $X$ is uniform on $(0,y)$, so $P(X\lt y/2\mid Y=y)=\tfrac12$ for every $y$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Jointly Distributed Random Variables

    Q27. If $\operatorname{Var}(X)=4$, $\operatorname{Var}(Y)=9$ and the correlation between $X$ and $Y$ is $-0.5$, then $\operatorname{Var}(2X-Y)$ is:
    1. 37
    2. 13
    3. 19
    4. 25
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 37.

    $\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=-0.5(2)(3)=-3$. $$\begin{aligned}&\operatorname{Var}(2X-Y) \\[4pt] &\quad = 4(4)+9-2(2)(-3) \\[4pt] &\quad = 16+9+12 = 37\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Taking the covariance term with the wrong sign gives 13; ignoring it gives 25.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Jointly Distributed Random Variables

    Q28. $(X_1,X_2,X_3)$ is multinomial with $n=12$ and cell probabilities $\left(\tfrac12,\tfrac13,\tfrac16\right)$. The correlation coefficient between $X_1$ and $X_2$ is:
    1. $-\dfrac12$
    2. $-\dfrac1{\sqrt2}$
    3. $-\dfrac13$
    4. $-2$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $-\dfrac1{\sqrt2}$.

    $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2)=-np_1p_2=-2$, $\operatorname{Var}(X_1)=np_1(1-p_1)=3$, $\operatorname{Var}(X_2)=np_2(1-p_2)=\tfrac83$. So $$\begin{aligned}\rho &= \dfrac{-2}{\sqrt{3\cdot 8/3}} = -\dfrac{2}{\sqrt8} \\[4pt] &= -\dfrac1{\sqrt2}\text{;}\end{aligned}$$ in general $\rho=-\sqrt{\dfrac{p_1p_2}{(1-p_1)(1-p_2)}}$, free of $n$. $-2$ is the covariance.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Jointly Distributed Random Variables

    Q29. A random variable $X$ has mean 10 and variance 4; nothing else is known about its distribution. Chebyshev's inequality gives, for $P(4\lt X\lt 16)$, the lower bound:
    1. $\dfrac34$
    2. $\dfrac59$
    3. $\dfrac19$
    4. $\dfrac89$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\dfrac89$.

    $$\begin{aligned}P(|X-10|\lt 6) &\ge 1-\dfrac{\sigma^2}{6^2} \\[4pt] &= 1-\dfrac4{36} = \dfrac89\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Here $6=3\sigma$, so this is the familiar $1-1/k^2$ with $k=3$; $\tfrac19$ is the upper bound for the complementary event.

    Read this in the notes: Unit I, Chebyshev's Inequality

    1. Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
    2. Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
    3. (A) is true but (R) is false
    4. (A) is false but (R) is true
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) (A) is true but (R) is false.

    (A) is true: $$\begin{aligned}P(|X-50|\lt 10) &\ge 1-\tfrac{25}{100} \\[4pt] &= 0.75\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ (R) is false: Chebyshev's inequality needs only a finite variance; no symmetry or any other shape is assumed, which is exactly why its bound is often crude.

    Q31. Let $S^2=\dfrac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i-\bar X)^2$ for a random sample of size $n=10$ from $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2=3$. Then $\operatorname{Var}(S^2)$ is:
    1. 2
    2. 1.8
    3. 6
    4. 0.9
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 2.

    $(n-1)S^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$, whose variance is $2(n-1)$. So $$\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Var}(S^2) &= \dfrac{2\sigma^4}{n-1} \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{2(9)}{9} = 2\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Dividing by $n$ in place of $n-1$ gives 1.8.

    Q34. Let $X_1,X_2,X_3,X_4$ be independent $N(0,1)$ variates. The distribution of $\dfrac{X_1-X_2}{\sqrt{X_3^2+X_4^2}}$ is:
    1. $N(0,1)$
    2. $t$ with 1 degree of freedom
    3. $F(1,2)$
    4. $t$ with 2 degrees of freedom
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $t$ with 2 degrees of freedom.

    $Z=\dfrac{X_1-X_2}{\sqrt2}\sim N(0,1)$ and $V=X_3^2+X_4^2\sim\chi^2_2$, independent. $$\begin{aligned}\dfrac{Z}{\sqrt{V/2}} &= \dfrac{X_1-X_2}{\sqrt{X_3^2+X_4^2}} \\[4pt] &\sim t_2\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Its square, not the ratio itself, is $F(1,2)$.

    Q35. $X_1,\dots,X_5$ is a random sample from the exponential distribution with mean $\theta$. The distribution of $\dfrac{2}{\theta}\sum_{i=1}^{5}X_i$ is:
    1. $\chi^2_5$
    2. $\chi^2_{10}$
    3. $\chi^2_{20}$
    4. exponential with mean 2
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\chi^2_{10}$.

    $2X_i/\theta$ is exponential with mean 2, which is $\chi^2_2$. The sum of 5 independent $\chi^2_2$ variates is $\chi^2_{10}$. This pivot gives exact confidence intervals for $\theta$.

    Q36. If $X$ is uniform on $(0,1)$, the distribution of $Y=-2\ln X$ is:
    1. $\chi^2$ with 2 degrees of freedom
    2. $\chi^2$ with 1 degree of freedom
    3. exponential with mean $\tfrac12$
    4. uniform on $(0,2)$

    $P(Y\gt y)=P(X\lt e^{-y/2})=e^{-y/2}$, $y\gt 0$: exponential with mean 2, which is the $\chi^2_2$ distribution. This is the basis of Fisher's method of combining independent $p$-values.

    List IList II
    (a) $X/Y$(i) $N(0,1)$
    (b) $X^2+Y^2$(ii) $\text{Beta}\left(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right)$
    (c) $\dfrac{X^2}{X^2+Y^2}$(iii) standard Cauchy
    (d) $\dfrac{X+Y}{\sqrt2}$(iv) exponential with mean 2

    Answer: (C) (a)-(iii), (b)-(iv), (c)-(ii), (d)-(i).

    Q38. If $X$ and $Y$ are independent and each is uniform on $(0,1)$, then $P\left(XY\le\tfrac12\right)$ is:

    2. $\ln 2$
    3. $\dfrac34$
    4. $\dfrac{1+\ln 2}{2}$

    If $x\le\tfrac12$ the event holds for every $y$; if $x\gt\tfrac12$ it needs $y\le\dfrac1{2x}$. So $$\begin{aligned}P &= \dfrac12+\int_{1/2}^{1}\dfrac{dx}{2x} \\[4pt] &= \dfrac12+\dfrac12\ln 2 \approx 0.847\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

    Q39. Given below are two statements, one labelled Assertion (A) and the other Reason (R).Assertion (A): If $X$ has a continuous distribution function $F$, then $F(X)$ is uniformly distributed on $(0,1)$.Reason (R): For every random variable $X$ with distribution function $F$, $P\{F(X)\le u\}=u$ for all $u\in(0,1)$.Choose the correct answer.
    1. Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
    2. Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
    3. (A) is true but (R) is false
    4. (A) is false but (R) is true
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) (A) is true but (R) is false.

    (A) is the probability integral transformation and is true. (R) is false: if $F$ has jumps, $F(X)$ cannot take values inside a jump. For a fair coin ($X=0,1$), $F(X)$ takes only the values $\tfrac12$ and 1, so $P\{F(X)\le 0.3\}=0\ne 0.3$. In general only $P\{F(X)\le u\}\le u$ holds.

    Q40. Which of the following functions is NOT the characteristic function of any random variable?
    1. $\cos t$
    2. $e^{-|t|}$
    3. $\dfrac{1}{1+t^2}$
    4. $e^{-t^4}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $e^{-t^4}$.

    $\cos t$: $X=\pm1$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each. $e^{-|t|}$: standard Cauchy. $1/(1+t^2)$: Laplace (double exponential). If $\phi(t)=e^{-t^4}$ were a characteristic function, $\phi''(0)=0$ would give $E(X^2)=0$, so $X=0$ almost surely and $\phi\equiv 1$, a contradiction.

    Q41. The characteristic function of $X$ is $\phi(t)=\dfrac{\sin t}{t}$ for $t\ne 0$, with $\phi(0)=1$. Then $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ is:
    1. $1$
    2. $\dfrac13$
    3. $\dfrac1{12}$
    4. $\dfrac12$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\dfrac13$.

    $\dfrac{\sin t}{t}=\dfrac{e^{it}-e^{-it}}{2it}$ is the characteristic function of the uniform distribution on $(-1,1)$, whose variance is $\dfrac{(b-a)^2}{12}=\dfrac4{12}=\dfrac13$. Or expand: $\phi(t)=1-\dfrac{t^2}{6}+\cdots$, so $E(X^2)=\tfrac13$ and $E(X)=0$.

Consider the statements:(I) $|\phi(t)|\le 1$ for all real $t$.(II) $\phi$ is uniformly continuous on the real line.(III) $\phi$ is real-valued if and only if $X$ is symmetric about 0.(IV) If $\phi_{X+Y}(t)=\phi_X(t)\,\phi_Y(t)$ for all $t$, then $X$ and $Y$ are independent.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I, II and III only
    2. I and II only
    3. I, III and IV only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) I, II and III only.

    (I), (II) and (III) are standard properties. (IV) is false: independence implies the product rule, but not conversely. If $X$ is standard Cauchy and $Y=X$, then $$\begin{aligned}\phi_{X+Y}(t) &= \phi_X(2t) = e^{-2|t|} \\[4pt] &= \phi_X(t)\phi_Y(t)\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ although $X$ and $Y$ are completely dependent.

    Q43. $X_1$ and $X_2$ are independent, each with characteristic function $e^{-|t|}$. The characteristic function of $2X_1-X_2$ is:
    1. $e^{-|t|}$
    2. $e^{-2|t|}$
    3. $e^{-3|t|}$
    4. $e^{-5|t|}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $e^{-3|t|}$.

    $$\begin{aligned}\phi_{2X_1-X_2}(t) &= \phi(2t)\,\phi(-t) \\[4pt] &= e^{-2|t|}e^{-|t|} = e^{-3|t|}\text{:}\end{aligned}$$ Cauchy with scale 3. Scales of Cauchy variates add in absolute value ($|2|+|-1|=3$); variances do not exist, so the ‘$4+1=5$’ rule does not apply.

    Q44. Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be independent with $P(X_n=n)=\dfrac1n$ and $P(X_n=0)=1-\dfrac1n$. Consider the statements:(I) $X_n\to 0$ in probability.(II) $X_n\to 0$ in mean (in $L^1$).(III) $X_n\to 0$ almost surely.(IV) $X_n\to 0$ in distribution.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I only
    2. I and IV only
    3. I, II and IV only
    4. I, III and IV only
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) I and IV only.

    $P(|X_n|\gt\varepsilon)=\tfrac1n\to0$: (I) true, hence (IV) true. $E|X_n|=1$ for every $n$: (II) false. $\sum P(X_n=n)=\infty$ and the $X_n$ are independent, so by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma $X_n=n$ infinitely often with probability 1: (III) false.

Consider the statements about a sequence of random variables $\{X_n\}$:(I) Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability.(II) Convergence in probability implies almost sure convergence.(III) Convergence in distribution to a constant implies convergence in probability to that constant.(IV) Convergence in $r$-th mean implies almost sure convergence.Which of the statements are correct?

    Q46. Let $\bar X_n$ be the mean of a random sample of size $n$ from the Poisson distribution with mean 4. The limiting distribution of $\sqrt n\left(\sqrt{\bar X_n}-2\right)$ is normal with mean 0 and variance:
    1. 4
    2. 1
    3. $\dfrac1{16}$
    4. $\dfrac14$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\dfrac14$.

    By the central limit theorem $\sqrt n(\bar X_n-4)\to N(0,4)$. By the delta method with $g(x)=\sqrt x$, $g'(4)=\tfrac14$, the variance is $4\cdot\tfrac1{16}=\tfrac14$. The square root is the variance-stabilising transformation for the Poisson: the limit variance is $\tfrac14$ whatever the mean.

    Q47. Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be independent with $P(X_k=k^{\alpha})=P(X_k=-k^{\alpha})=\tfrac12$, $\alpha\gt0$, and $S_n=X_1+\cdots+X_n$. Chebyshev's sufficient condition for the weak law of large numbers, $\operatorname{Var}(S_n)/n^2\to 0$, is satisfied precisely when:
    1. $\alpha\lt 1$
    2. $\alpha\lt\dfrac12$
    3. $\alpha\le 1$
    4. every $\alpha\gt 0$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\alpha\lt\dfrac12$.

    $\operatorname{Var}(X_k)=k^{2\alpha}$, so $$\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Var}(S_n) &= \sum_{k=1}^{n}k^{2\alpha} \\[4pt] &\sim \dfrac{n^{2\alpha+1}}{2\alpha+1}\end{aligned}$$ and $\operatorname{Var}(S_n)/n^2\sim\dfrac{n^{2\alpha-1}}{2\alpha+1}$, which tends to 0 if and only if $\alpha\lt\tfrac12$.

    Q48. Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be independent, each with density $f(x)=\dfrac{2}{x^3}$, $x\gt 1$. As $n\to\infty$, the sample mean $\bar X_n$:
    1. converges almost surely to 2
    2. does not converge, because the variance is infinite
    3. converges almost surely to 1

    $E(X)=\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\dfrac{2}{x^2}dx=2$ is finite, and Kolmogorov's strong law for independent, identically distributed variables needs only a finite mean. $E(X^2)=\int_1^\infty\tfrac2x\,dx$ diverges, but an infinite variance does not prevent the law of large numbers; it is the usual central limit theorem that fails here.
