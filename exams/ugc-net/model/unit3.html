    Model Questions — Unit III: Sampling Methods and Design of Experiments

    UGC NET Statistics (Code 107) · 50 questions · Click "Show Answer" for the worked solution

    About this set

    Simple random, stratified, systematic, cluster, two-stage, double and varying-probability sampling; ratio and regression estimators; analysis of variance; CRD, RBD and Latin squares; missing plots; factorial experiments, confounding and balanced incomplete block designs.

    These are model questions written for practice at the standard of the paper; they are not questions from a past paper. Try each one before opening the answer. Every answer gives the working and a link to the section of the Unit III notes that teaches it.

    The questions

    Q1. A population has $N=50$ units and mean square $S^2=\dfrac{1}{N-1}\sum(Y_i-\bar Y)^2=20$. For a simple random sample of size $n=10$, the variance of the sample mean under sampling without replacement and with replacement is, respectively:
    1. 2.00 and 1.96
    2. 1.60 and 1.96
    3. 1.60 and 2.00
    4. 1.96 and 1.60
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) 1.60 and 1.96.

    Without replacement: $$\begin{aligned}V(\bar y) &= \left(\dfrac1n-\dfrac1N\right)S^2 \\[4pt] &= (0.1-0.02)(20) = 1.60\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ With replacement: $V(\bar y)=\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}$, where $\sigma^2=\dfrac{N-1}{N}S^2=19.6$, giving $1.96$. Sampling without replacement is always the more precise.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Simple Random Sampling (SRS)

    Q2. A simple random sample of 4 units is drawn without replacement from a population of 10 units. The probability that two specified units are both included in the sample is:
    1. $\dfrac25$
    2. $\dfrac{4}{25}$
    3. $\dfrac{3}{25}$
    4. $\dfrac{2}{15}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\dfrac{2}{15}$.

    $$\begin{aligned}\pi_{ij} &= \dfrac{n(n-1)}{N(N-1)} \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{4\cdot3}{10\cdot9} = \dfrac{2}{15}\text{;}\end{aligned}$$ equivalently $\binom{8}{2}\big/\binom{10}{4}=\dfrac{28}{210}$. $\tfrac25$ is the inclusion probability $n/N$ of one unit, and $\tfrac4{25}$ is its square, which would hold only if inclusions were independent.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Simple Random Sampling (SRS)

    Q3. A proportion $P$ is to be estimated by simple random sampling from $N=1600$ units with margin of error $d=0.05$, using the normal deviate $z=2$ and the safest guess $P=0.5$. With $n_0=\dfrac{z^2PQ}{d^2}$ and the finite population adjustment $n=\dfrac{n_0}{1+n_0/N}$, the required sample size is:
    1. 320
    2. 400
    3. 267
    4. 356
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 320.

    $n_0=\dfrac{4(0.25)}{0.0025}=400$. Since $n_0/N=0.25$ is not negligible, $n=\dfrac{400}{1.25}=320$. Taking $P=0.5$ maximises $PQ$ and so gives the largest, safest $n$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Simple Random Sampling (SRS)

    Q4. For simple random sampling without replacement, with $s^2=\dfrac{1}{n-1}\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2$, $S^2=\dfrac{1}{N-1}\sum(Y_i-\bar Y)^2$ and $\sigma^2=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum(Y_i-\bar Y)^2$, consider the statements:(I) $\bar y$ is unbiased for $\bar Y$.(II) $s^2$ is unbiased for $\sigma^2$.(III) $s^2$ is unbiased for $S^2$.(IV) $V(\bar y)=\dfrac{N-n}{Nn}S^2$.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and II only
    2. I, II and IV only
    3. I, III and IV only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) I, III and IV only.

    Without replacement $E(s^2)=S^2$, so (III) is true and (II) is false; $s^2$ is unbiased for $\sigma^2$ only in sampling with replacement. (I) and (IV) are the basic results of simple random sampling.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Simple Random Sampling (SRS)

    Q5. A simple random sample of size 3 is drawn with replacement from a population of 5 units. The expected number of distinct units in the sample is:
    1. 3
    2. $\dfrac{61}{25}$
    3. $\dfrac{12}{5}$
    4. $\dfrac{64}{25}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\dfrac{61}{25}$.

    A given unit is missed in all $n$ draws with probability $\left(1-\tfrac1N\right)^n$. So the expected number of distinct units is $$\begin{aligned}&N\left[1-\left(1-\tfrac1N\right)^n\right] \\[4pt] &\quad = 5\left[1-\tfrac{64}{125}\right] = \tfrac{61}{25} \\[4pt] &\quad = 2.44\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Simple Random Sampling (SRS)

    Q6. A population consists of the four values 1, 2, 3 and 6. Two units are drawn one after the other by simple random sampling without replacement. The covariance between the values obtained on the first and the second draw is:
    1. $\dfrac76$
    2. $-\dfrac{14}{3}$
    3. $-\dfrac73$
    4. $-\dfrac76$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $-\dfrac76$.

    $\operatorname{Cov}(y_1,y_2)=-\dfrac{S^2}{N}$. Here $\bar Y=3$ and $S^2=\dfrac{4+1+0+9}{3}=\dfrac{14}{3}$, so the covariance is $-\dfrac{14}{12}=-\dfrac76$. Direct check: $$\begin{aligned}&E(y_1y_2) \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{2(2+3+6+6+12+18)}{12} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{47}{6}\end{aligned}$$ and $\dfrac{47}{6}-9=-\dfrac76$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Simple Random Sampling (SRS)

    Q7. A population is divided into two strata with $N_1=400$, $S_1=10$ and $N_2=600$, $S_2=20$. Under Neyman allocation, a total sample of $n=100$ is divided between the strata as $(n_1,n_2)=$
    1. (25, 75)
    2. (40, 60)
    3. (50, 50)
    4. (75, 25)
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) (25, 75).

    Neyman allocation: $n_h\propto N_hS_h$. $N_1S_1=4000$, $N_2S_2=12000$, in the ratio $1:3$, so $n_1=25$, $n_2=75$. Proportional allocation would give $(40,60)$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Stratified Random Sampling

    Q8. For a fixed total sample size, let $V_{ran}$, $V_{prop}$ and $V_{opt}$ denote the variances of the estimator of the population mean under simple random sampling, stratified sampling with proportional allocation and stratified sampling with Neyman allocation. Ignoring terms in $1/N_h$:
    1. $V_{opt}\le V_{ran}\le V_{prop}$
    2. $V_{prop}\le V_{opt}\le V_{ran}$
    3. $V_{opt}\le V_{prop}\le V_{ran}$
    4. $V_{ran}\le V_{prop}\le V_{opt}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $V_{opt}\le V_{prop}\le V_{ran}$.

    $$\begin{aligned}&V_{ran}-V_{prop} \\[4pt] &\quad \approx \dfrac{1-f}{n}\sum W_h(\bar Y_h-\bar Y)^2 \ge 0\end{aligned}$$ and $$\begin{aligned}&V_{prop}-V_{opt} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac1n\sum W_h(S_h-\bar S)^2 \ge 0\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ where $\bar S=\sum W_hS_h$. Proportional allocation gains from differences between stratum means, Neyman allocation additionally from differences between stratum standard deviations.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Stratified Random Sampling

    Q9. A population of $N=1000$ units has two strata with weights $W_1=0.4$, $W_2=0.6$ and mean squares $S_1^2=100$, $S_2^2=400$. For a stratified sample of $n=100$ with proportional allocation, the variance of $\bar y_{st}$ is:
    1. 2.80
    2. 2.28
    3. 0.28
    4. 2.52
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) 2.52.

    $$\begin{aligned}V_{prop}(\bar y_{st}) &= \dfrac{1-f}{n}\sum W_hS_h^2 \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{0.9}{100}(40+240) = 2.52\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Omitting the finite population correction gives 2.80.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Stratified Random Sampling

    Q10. Two strata have $N_1=400$, $S_1=10$, cost per unit $c_1=4$, and $N_2=600$, $S_2=20$, cost per unit $c_2=16$. For a linear cost function, the optimum allocation of a sample of $n=100$ is $(n_1,n_2)=$
    1. (50, 50)
    2. (40, 60)
    3. (25, 75)
    4. (60, 40)
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) (40, 60).

    Optimum allocation: $n_h\propto\dfrac{N_hS_h}{\sqrt{c_h}}$. $\dfrac{4000}{2}=2000$ and $\dfrac{12000}{4}=3000$, in the ratio $2:3$, so $(n_1,n_2)=(40,60)$. Compared with Neyman allocation $(25,75)$, fewer units are taken from the stratum where sampling is costly.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Stratified Random Sampling

    Q11. Given below are two statements, one labelled Assertion (A) and the other Reason (R).Assertion (A): Stratification gives the greatest gain in precision when the stratum means differ widely from one another.Reason (R): $V_{ran}-V_{prop}$ is approximately $\dfrac{1-f}{n}\sum W_h(\bar Y_h-\bar Y)^2$.Choose the correct answer.
    1. Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
    2. Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
    3. (A) is true but (R) is false
    4. (A) is false but (R) is true
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

    The gain of proportional stratification over simple random sampling is the between-strata component $\sum W_h(\bar Y_h-\bar Y)^2$, which is large exactly when the stratum means are far apart. Both statements are true and (R) explains (A): strata should be internally homogeneous and differ from each other.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Stratified Random Sampling

    Q12. A population of $N=1000$ units has two strata with weights $W_1=0.4$, $W_2=0.6$ and standard deviations $S_1=10$, $S_2=20$. For a stratified sample of $n=100$ with Neyman allocation, the variance of $\bar y_{st}$ is:
    1. 2.56
    2. 2.52
    3. 2.28
    4. 2.80
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 2.28.

    $$\begin{aligned}&V_{opt} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{(\sum W_hS_h)^2}{n}-\dfrac{\sum W_hS_h^2}{N} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{(4+12)^2}{100}-\dfrac{280}{1000} \\[4pt] &\quad = 2.56-0.28 = 2.28\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ It is smaller than the 2.52 of proportional allocation by $\dfrac1n\sum W_h(S_h-\bar S)^2=\dfrac{24}{100}$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Stratified Random Sampling

    Q13. For $N=nk$, the variance of the mean of a systematic sample is $\dfrac{S^2}{n}\cdot\dfrac{N-1}{N}\left[1+(n-1)\rho\right]$, where $\rho$ is the intraclass correlation between units of the same systematic sample. Systematic sampling is more precise than simple random sampling without replacement if and only if:
    1. $\rho\lt-\dfrac{1}{N-1}$
    2. $\rho\gt0$
    3. $\rho\lt\dfrac{1}{n-1}$
    4. $\rho=1$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $\rho\lt-\dfrac{1}{N-1}$.

    $V_{ran}=\dfrac{N-n}{Nn}S^2$. $V_{sy}\lt V_{ran}$ means $(N-1)[1+(n-1)\rho]\lt N-n$, i.e. $(N-1)(n-1)\rho\lt-(n-1)$, i.e. $\rho\lt-\dfrac1{N-1}$. Systematic samples must be internally heterogeneous.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Systematic Sampling

    Q14. For a population with a linear trend, $Y_i=\alpha+\beta i$ $(i=1,\dots,N)$, the variances of the estimators of the mean under stratified sampling with one unit per stratum ($V_{st}$), systematic sampling ($V_{sy}$) and simple random sampling ($V_{ran}$) satisfy:
    1. $V_{sy}\le V_{st}\le V_{ran}$
    2. $V_{ran}\le V_{sy}\le V_{st}$
    3. $V_{st}\le V_{ran}\le V_{sy}$
    4. $V_{st}\le V_{sy}\le V_{ran}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $V_{st}\le V_{sy}\le V_{ran}$.

    For a linear trend $V_{st}:V_{sy}:V_{ran}\approx\dfrac1n:1:n$. Systematic sampling removes most of the trend but every unit of the sample is in the same position within its stratum; stratified sampling lets these positions vary and does better still.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Systematic Sampling

    Q15. The population $Y_i=i$, $i=1,\dots,12$, is sampled systematically with interval $k=4$ (sample size 3). The variance of the systematic sample mean is:
    1. 3.25
    2. 0.75
    3. 1.25
    4. 1.67
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 1.25.

    The four possible samples are $\{1,5,9\},\{2,6,10\},\{3,7,11\},\{4,8,12\}$ with means 5, 6, 7, 8, each with probability $\tfrac14$. Their variance about 6.5 is $$\begin{aligned}&\dfrac{2.25+0.25+0.25+2.25}{4} \\[4pt] &\quad = 1.25\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ A simple random sample of 3 would have variance $\left(\tfrac13-\tfrac1{12}\right)13=3.25$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Systematic Sampling

    Q16. In simple random sampling, the coefficients of variation of $y$ and of the auxiliary variable $x$ are $C_y=0.5$ and $C_x=0.4$. To the first order of approximation the ratio estimator of $\bar Y$ is more efficient than the sample mean if the correlation $\rho$ between $x$ and $y$ satisfies:
    1. $\rho\gt0.8$
    2. $\rho\gt0.4$
    3. $\rho\gt0.625$
    4. $\rho\gt0.2$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\rho\gt0.4$.

    $V(\bar y_R)\lt V(\bar y)$ when $C_x^2-2\rho C_xC_y\lt0$, i.e. $$\begin{aligned}\rho\gt\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac{C_x}{C_y} &= \dfrac{0.4}{1.0} \\[4pt] &= 0.4\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ A positive correlation alone is not enough.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Ratio and Regression Estimators

    Q17. A sample gives $\bar y=22$ and $\bar x=44$; the population mean of $x$ is known to be $\bar X=50$ and the sample regression coefficient of $y$ on $x$ is $b=0.6$. The ratio and the linear regression estimates of $\bar Y$ are, respectively:
    1. 25.6 and 25
    2. 22 and 25.6
    3. 25 and 23.6
    4. 25 and 25.6
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) 25 and 25.6.

    Ratio: $$\begin{aligned}\bar y_R &= \dfrac{\bar y}{\bar x}\bar X = \dfrac{22}{44}(50) \\[4pt] &= 25\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Regression: $$\begin{aligned}\bar y_{lr} &= \bar y+b(\bar X-\bar x) \\[4pt] &= 22+0.6(6) = 25.6\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Both adjust the sample mean upward because the sample mean of $x$ fell below $\bar X$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Ratio and Regression Estimators

    Q18. In large samples, if the correlation between $y$ and $x$ is $\rho=0.8$, the efficiency of the linear regression estimator of $\bar Y$ relative to the simple random sample mean is about:
    1. 278%
    2. 125%
    3. 156%
    4. 500%
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 278%.

    $V(\bar y_{lr})\approx\dfrac{1-f}{n}S_y^2(1-\rho^2)$, so the relative efficiency is $\dfrac{1}{1-\rho^2}=\dfrac{1}{0.36}=2.78$, i.e. 278%.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Ratio and Regression Estimators

    Q19. Consider the statements about estimation with an auxiliary variable $x$ in simple random sampling:(I) The ratio estimator is in general biased, the bias being of order $1/n$.(II) The ratio estimator is the best linear unbiased estimator when the regression of $y$ on $x$ is a line through the origin and the variance of $y$ about it is proportional to $x$.(III) In large samples the variance of the regression estimator never exceeds that of the ratio estimator.(IV) The ratio estimator is more efficient than the sample mean whenever $\rho\gt0$.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and II only
    2. I and III only
    3. I, II and III only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) I, II and III only.

    (I), (II) and (III) are standard results; in (III) equality holds when the regression line passes through the origin, $\beta=R$. (IV) is false: the condition is $\rho\gt\dfrac{C_x}{2C_y}$, which can fail for a small positive $\rho$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Ratio and Regression Estimators

    Q20. In a large population $S_y=10$, $S_x=8$, the correlation between $x$ and $y$ is $0.8$ and the ratio $R=\bar Y/\bar X=1$. For a simple random sample of $n=36$ (ignore the finite population correction), the approximate variance of the ratio estimator of $\bar Y$ is:
    1. 8.11
    2. 1.00
    3. 2.78
    4. 4.56
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) 1.00.

    $$\begin{aligned}&V(\bar y_R) \\[4pt] &\quad \approx \dfrac1n\left(S_y^2+R^2S_x^2-2R\rho S_yS_x\right) \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{100+64-128}{36} = 1\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ The sample mean has variance $\tfrac{100}{36}=2.78$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Ratio and Regression Estimators

    Q21. Clusters of $M=10$ elements each are sampled, and the intraclass correlation between elements of the same cluster is $\rho=0.1$. Compared with a simple random sample of the same number of elements, the variance of the estimated mean is multiplied by about:
    1. 0.9
    2. 1.1
    3. 1.9
    4. 10
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 1.9.

    The design effect of single-stage cluster sampling is $1+(M-1)\rho=1+9(0.1)=1.9$. Even a small positive intraclass correlation is costly when clusters are large.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Cluster Sampling

    Q22. Given below are two statements, one labelled Assertion (A) and the other Reason (R).Assertion (A): For the same number of elements, cluster sampling is usually less precise than simple random sampling.Reason (R): Elements belonging to the same cluster tend to resemble one another.Choose the correct answer.
    1. Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
    2. Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
    3. (A) is true but (R) is false
    4. (A) is false but (R) is true
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

    Resemblance within clusters means a positive intraclass correlation $\rho$, and the variance is inflated by $1+(M-1)\rho\gt1$. Both statements are true and (R) is the reason for (A). Cluster sampling is used because it is cheaper and needs no list of elements, not because it is precise.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Cluster Sampling

    Q23. In two-stage sampling, $n=10$ first-stage units are selected and $m=5$ second-stage units from each. The mean squares between first-stage unit means and within first-stage units are $S_b^2=20$ and $S_w^2=100$. Ignoring both sampling fractions, the variance of the sample mean per element is:
    1. 2
    2. 22
    3. 6
    4. 4
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) 4.

    $$\begin{aligned}V(\bar{\bar y}) &\approx \dfrac{S_b^2}{n}+\dfrac{S_w^2}{nm} \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{20}{10}+\dfrac{100}{50} = 2+2 = 4\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Increasing $m$ reduces only the second term; the first can be reduced only by taking more first-stage units.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Cluster Sampling

    Q24. In double sampling for regression, a first sample of $n'=100$ units gives $\bar x'$ and a subsample of $n=20$ gives $y$. With $S_y^2=100$ and $\rho=0.8$, and ignoring finite population corrections, the approximate variance of the regression estimator $\bar y+b(\bar x'-\bar x)$ is:
    1. 1.80
    2. 2.44
    3. 5.00
    4. 0.64
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) 2.44.

    $$\begin{aligned}V &\approx \dfrac{S_y^2(1-\rho^2)}{n}+\dfrac{\rho^2S_y^2}{n'} \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{36}{20}+\dfrac{64}{100} \\[4pt] &= 1.80+0.64 = 2.44\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ The second term is the price of not knowing $\bar X$; the sample mean alone would have variance 5.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Double (Two-Phase) Sampling

    Q25. Two units are selected with replacement with probabilities proportional to size. The selected units have $y$-values 20 and 30 and selection probabilities 0.2 and 0.4. The Hansen–Hurwitz estimate of the population total is:
    1. 87.5
    2. 175
    3. 25
    4. 83.3
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 87.5.

    $$\begin{aligned}\hat Y_{HH} &= \dfrac1n\sum\dfrac{y_i}{p_i} \\[4pt] &= \dfrac12\left(\dfrac{20}{0.2}+\dfrac{30}{0.4}\right) \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{100+75}{2} = 87.5\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Each $y_i/p_i$ is by itself an unbiased estimate of the total.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Sampling with Varying Probability (PPS)

    Q26. A sample of three units drawn without replacement has $y$-values 10, 20, 30 and inclusion probabilities 0.5, 0.25, 0.5. The Horvitz–Thompson estimate of the population total is:
    1. 60
    2. 120
    3. 160
    4. 80
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 160.

    $$\begin{aligned}\hat Y_{HT} &= \sum\dfrac{y_i}{\pi_i} = 20+80+60 \\[4pt] &= 160\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Each sampled unit represents $1/\pi_i$ units of the population.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Sampling with Varying Probability (PPS)

    Q27. For a sampling design of fixed size $n$ with inclusion probabilities $\pi_i$ and $\pi_{ij}$, consider the statements:(I) The Horvitz–Thompson estimator of the total is unbiased if every $\pi_i\gt0$.(II) The Yates–Grundy estimator of its variance can take negative values.(III) If $\pi_i$ is exactly proportional to $Y_i$, the Horvitz–Thompson estimator has zero variance.(IV) $\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi_i=n$.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and IV only
    2. I, II and IV only
    3. I, III and IV only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) I, II, III and IV.

    (I) and (IV) are basic. (II) The Yates–Grundy estimator is non-negative only if $\pi_i\pi_j\ge\pi_{ij}$ for all pairs. (III) If $\pi_i=nY_i/Y$, every $y_i/\pi_i=Y/n$ and the estimator equals $Y$ for every sample. All four are true.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Sampling with Varying Probability (PPS)

    Q28. Lahiri's method is used to select a unit with probability proportional to size from four units of sizes 10, 20, 30 and 40, taking $M=40$. The probability that a single trial (a pair of random numbers) results in a selection is:
    1. $\dfrac14$
    2. $\dfrac58$
    3. $\dfrac25$
    4. $1$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\dfrac58$.

    A trial picks unit $i$ with probability $\tfrac1N$ and accepts it if a random number between 1 and $M$ does not exceed $X_i$. So $$\begin{aligned}P(\text{selection}) &= \dfrac1N\sum\dfrac{X_i}{M} \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{\bar X}{M} = \dfrac{25}{40} = \dfrac58\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ The method needs no cumulative totals.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, Sampling with Varying Probability (PPS)

    Q29. In a one-way analysis of variance with 4 treatments and 5 observations on each, the between-treatments sum of squares is 90 and the total sum of squares is 250. The value of the $F$ statistic is:
    1. 1.78
    2. 9
    3. 3
    4. 0.56
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 3.

    Error sum of squares $=250-90=160$ with $20-4=16$ degrees of freedom; treatments have 3. $F=\dfrac{90/3}{160/16}=\dfrac{30}{10}=3$, on $(3,16)$ degrees of freedom.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, One-way ANOVA (Fixed Effects)

    Q30. In a one-way random effects model $y_{ij}=\mu+a_i+e_{ij}$ with 5 observations in each group, the analysis of variance gives mean squares 50 between groups and 10 within groups. The estimate of the variance component $\sigma_a^2$ is:
    1. 8
    2. 40
    3. 10
    4. 12
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 8.

    $E(\text{MS between})=\sigma_e^2+n\sigma_a^2$ and $E(\text{MS within})=\sigma_e^2$. So $\hat\sigma_a^2=\dfrac{50-10}{5}=8$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit III, One-way ANOVA (Fixed Effects)

    Q31. Three treatments, each replicated 5 times in a completely randomised design, have totals 20, 30 and 40. The sum of squares for the linear contrast with coefficients $(-1,0,1)$ is:
    1. 20
    2. 80
    3. 10
    4. 40
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) 40.

    $L=-20+0+40=20$ and $$\begin{aligned}SS(L) &= \dfrac{L^2}{r\sum c_i^2} = \dfrac{400}{5\cdot2} \\[4pt] &= 40\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ The orthogonal quadratic contrast $(1,-2,1)$ gives $20-60+40=0$, so the whole treatment sum of squares, 40, is linear.

    Q33. Match the principles and devices of experimental design in List I with their main purpose in List II.
    List IList II
    (a) Replication(i) Reduces the block size in factorial experiments
    (b) Randomisation(ii) Provides an estimate of the experimental error
    (c) Local control(iii) Ensures the validity of the estimate of error and of the tests
    (d) Confounding(iv) Reduces the experimental error by grouping similar units
    1. (a)-(ii), (b)-(iii), (c)-(iv), (d)-(i)
    2. (a)-(iii), (b)-(ii), (c)-(iv), (d)-(i)
    3. (a)-(ii), (b)-(iv), (c)-(iii), (d)-(i)
    4. (a)-(iv), (b)-(iii), (c)-(i), (d)-(ii)
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) (a)-(ii), (b)-(iii), (c)-(iv), (d)-(i).

    Fisher's three principles: replication gives the estimate of error (and more precision); randomisation makes that estimate and the tests based on it valid; local control (blocking) removes known variation from the error. Confounding sacrifices unimportant interactions to keep blocks small.

    Q34. Given below are two statements, one labelled Assertion (A) and the other Reason (R).Assertion (A): Local control reduces the experimental error.Reason (R): Local control increases the number of replications of each treatment.Choose the correct answer.
    1. Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
    2. Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
    3. (A) is true but (R) is false
    4. (A) is false but (R) is true
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) (A) is true but (R) is false.

    Blocking groups similar units together, so differences between blocks are removed from the error: (A) is true. It does not change the number of replications; that is a separate choice of the experimenter: (R) is false.

    Answer: (B) 20.

    Q36. For which of the following orders does a Graeco-Latin square (a pair of orthogonal Latin squares) NOT exist?
    1. 4
    2. 5
    3. 7
    4. 6
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) 6.

    A pair of orthogonal Latin squares exists for every order except 2 and 6 (Euler's problem of the 36 officers, settled by Tarry). For a prime or prime-power order $m$, such as 4, 5 and 7, there is a complete set of $m-1$ mutually orthogonal Latin squares.

    Q37. A randomised block design with 5 treatments in 4 blocks has block mean square 30 and error mean square 5. Its efficiency relative to a completely randomised design is about:
    1. 1.00
    2. 6.00
    3. 1.79
    4. 0.56
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 1.79.

    $$\begin{aligned}&E \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{(r-1)\,MS_B+r(t-1)\,MS_E}{(rt-1)\,MS_E} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{3(30)+16(5)}{19(5)} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{170}{95} \approx 1.79\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Blocking has made the experiment about 79% more efficient.

    Q38. In a randomised block design with 4 blocks the error mean square is 8 on 12 degrees of freedom. Given $t_{0.025,12}=2.179$, the critical difference between two treatment means at the 5% level is:
    1. 4.36
    2. 8.72
    3. 3.08
    4. 6.16
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 4.36.

    $$\begin{aligned}CD &= t\sqrt{\dfrac{2\,MS_E}{r}} = 2.179\sqrt{\dfrac{16}{4}} \\[4pt] &= 2.179\times2 = 4.36\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Leaving out the factor 2 under the root gives 3.08, the half-width for a single mean.

    Q39. Consider the statements about an $m\times m$ Latin square design:(I) The numbers of rows, columns and treatments are equal.(II) A $2\times2$ Latin square leaves no degrees of freedom for error.(III) The model assumes that rows, columns and treatments do not interact.(IV) The error degrees of freedom are $(m-1)^2$.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and III only
    2. I, II and III only
    3. II and IV only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) I, II and III only.

    (I) and (III) are part of the definition and model. Error degrees of freedom are $(m-1)(m-2)$, which is 0 for $m=2$: (II) is true and (IV) is false; $(m-1)^2$ is the error degrees of freedom of a randomised block design with $m$ treatments in $m$ blocks.

    Q40. In a randomised block design with 5 treatments and 4 blocks one observation is missing. The total of the remaining observations in its block is 50, in its treatment 40, and the grand total is 340. The estimate of the missing value is:
    1. 2.5
    2. 4
    3. 6
    4. 5
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) 5.

    $$\begin{aligned}\hat x &= \dfrac{rB+tT-G}{(r-1)(t-1)} \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{4(50)+5(40)-340}{3\cdot4} \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{60}{12} = 5\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ The error degrees of freedom are then reduced by one, from 12 to 11.

    Q41. In a $4\times4$ Latin square one observation is missing. The totals of the remaining observations in its row, column and treatment are 20, 24 and 22, and the grand total is 120. The estimate of the missing value is:

    Q42. A $2^2$ factorial experiment in 5 replicates gives treatment totals $(1)=20$, $a=30$, $b=26$, $ab=44$. The main effect of $A$ and its sum of squares are, respectively:
    1. 5.6 and 39.2
    2. 2.8 and 78.4
    3. 2.8 and 39.2
    4. 0.8 and 3.2
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 2.8 and 39.2.

    Contrast $$\begin{aligned}[A] &= ab+a-b-(1) \\[4pt] &= 44+30-26-20 = 28\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Main effect $=\dfrac{[A]}{2r}=\dfrac{28}{10}=2.8$; $$\begin{aligned}SS_A &= \dfrac{[A]^2}{4r} = \dfrac{784}{20} \\[4pt] &= 39.2\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ Option D is the interaction: $[AB]=44+20-30-26=8$, effect 0.8, sum of squares 3.2.

    Q43. A $2^3$ factorial experiment is conducted in 3 randomised blocks, each a complete replicate. The error degrees of freedom are:
    1. 16
    2. 14
    3. 21
    4. 7

    Answer: (B) 14.

    Total $24-1=23$; blocks $2$; treatments $7$ (three main effects, three two-factor interactions and one three-factor interaction). Error $=23-2-7=14=(r-1)(2^3-1)$.

    Q44. In a $2^3$ factorial experiment, the treatment combinations that enter the contrast for the interaction $ABC$ with a positive sign are:
    1. $(1), ab, ac, bc$
    2. $a, b, c$ only
    3. $ab, ac, bc, abc$
    4. $a, b, c, abc$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $a, b, c, abc$.

    $$\begin{aligned}&ABC\propto(a-1)(b-1)(c-1) \\[4pt] &\quad = abc-ab-ac-bc+a+b+c-(1)\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ The combinations with an odd number of letters, $a,b,c,abc$, are positive; those with an even number, $(1),ab,ac,bc$, are negative.

    Q45. A $2^3$ factorial experiment is laid out in two blocks of four plots, confounding $ABC$. The principal block (the one containing the control) is:
    1. $(1), ab, ac, bc$
    2. $(1), a, b, c$
    3. $a, b, c, abc$
    4. $(1), a, bc, abc$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $(1), ab, ac, bc$.

    The principal block holds the combinations having an even number of letters in common with $ABC$: $(1),ab,ac,bc$. The other block is $a,b,c,abc$. The difference between block totals is exactly the $ABC$ contrast, so $ABC$ cannot be separated from blocks.

    Q46. A $2^4$ factorial experiment is arranged in four blocks of four plots, confounding $ABC$ and $BCD$. The third interaction that is automatically confounded is:
    1. $AB$
    2. $BC$
    3. $AD$
    4. $ABCD$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $AD$.

    Four blocks have 3 degrees of freedom, so three effects are confounded: the two chosen and their generalised interaction, $ABC\times BCD=AB^2C^2D=AD$ (squares drop out). Choosing $ABCD$ and $ABC$ instead would confound the main effect $D$, which is why the generators must be picked with care.

    Q47. In a $2^3$ experiment in 4 replicates, each in two blocks of four plots, the interactions $ABC$, $AB$, $AC$ and $BC$ are confounded in replicates 1, 2, 3 and 4 respectively. Relative to an unconfounded effect, the information recovered on each of these interactions is:
    1. $\dfrac14$
    2. $\dfrac34$
    3. $\dfrac12$
    4. $1$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\dfrac34$.

    This is partial confounding: each of the four interactions is confounded in one replicate and estimated from the other three, so the relative information is $\tfrac34$. Main effects are estimated from all four replicates with full information.

    Q48. Which of the following sets of parameters $(v,b,r,k,\lambda)$ can NOT be those of a balanced incomplete block design?
    1. (7, 7, 3, 3, 1)
    2. (9, 12, 4, 3, 1)
    3. (6, 10, 5, 3, 2)
    4. (8, 8, 4, 4, 2)
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) (8, 8, 4, 4, 2).

    A BIBD needs $bk=vr$ and $\lambda(v-1)=r(k-1)$. Option D satisfies $bk=vr=32$ but $\lambda(v-1)=14\ne r(k-1)=12$. The other three satisfy both relations and Fisher's inequality $b\ge v$, and designs with those parameters exist.

    Q49. The efficiency factor of the balanced incomplete block design with $v=b=7$, $r=k=3$, $\lambda=1$, relative to a randomised block design with the same number of replications and the same error variance, is:
    1. $\dfrac37$
    2. $\dfrac23$
    3. $\dfrac79$
    4. $\dfrac97$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $\dfrac79$.

    $E=\dfrac{\lambda v}{rk}=\dfrac{1\cdot7}{3\cdot3}=\dfrac79$. The variance of the difference between two adjusted treatment means is $\dfrac{2k\sigma^2}{\lambda v}$ against $\dfrac{2\sigma^2}{r}$ for complete blocks. $E\lt1$ always; the design pays only if the smaller blocks reduce $\sigma^2$ enough.

    Q50. Consider the statements about a balanced incomplete block design with parameters $(v,b,r,k,\lambda)$:(I) If $b=v$, then $r=k$.(II) $b\ge v$.(III) Every balanced incomplete block design is resolvable.(IV) If $b=v$, any two blocks have exactly $\lambda$ treatments in common.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I, II and IV only
    2. I and II only
    3. I, II and III only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) I, II and IV only.

    (I) follows from $bk=vr$. (II) is Fisher's inequality. (IV) is the characteristic property of a symmetric BIBD. (III) is false: a resolvable design needs $k$ to divide $v$ and satisfies $b\ge v+r-1$; the design $(7,7,3,3,1)$ cannot be resolvable since 3 does not divide 7.
