    Model Questions — Unit VIII: Multivariate Analysis

    UGC NET Statistics (Code 107) · 50 questions · Click "Show Answer" for the worked solution

    About this set

    The multivariate normal distribution, estimation of the mean vector and covariance matrix, the Wishart distribution, partial and multiple correlation, Hotelling's $T^2$, discriminant analysis, principal components and canonical correlations.

    These are model questions written for practice at the standard of the paper; they are not questions from a past paper. Try each one before opening the answer. Every answer gives the working and a link to the section of the Unit VIII notes that teaches it.

    The questions

    Q1. Let $X=(X_1,X_2,X_3)'\sim N_3(\mu,\Sigma)$ with $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}4&2&0\\2&9&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Which statement is correct?
    1. $X_1$ and $X_2$ are independent
    2. $X_3$ is independent of $(X_1,X_2)$
    3. $X_1,X_2,X_3$ are mutually independent
    4. $X_1+X_3$ and $X_1$ are independent
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $X_3$ is independent of $(X_1,X_2)$.

    In a multivariate normal distribution, sub-vectors are independent if and only if their covariance matrix is zero. $$\begin{aligned}\operatorname{Cov}(X_3,X_1) &= \operatorname{Cov}(X_3,X_2) \\[4pt] &= 0\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ so $X_3$ is independent of $(X_1,X_2)$. $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2)=2\ne0$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1+X_3,X_1)=4\ne0$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Multivariate Normal Distribution

    Q2. $(X_1,X_2)'$ is bivariate normal with mean $(1,2)'$ and covariance matrix $\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&9\end{pmatrix}$. The conditional mean and variance of $X_1$ given $X_2=5$ are approximately:
    1. 2.50 and 3.00
    2. 1.67 and 4.00
    3. 2.50 and 3.56
    4. 1.67 and 3.56
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) 1.67 and 3.56.

    $$\begin{aligned}&E(X_1\mid X_2=x_2) \\[4pt] &\quad = \mu_1+\dfrac{\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_{22}}(x_2-\mu_2) \\[4pt] &\quad = 1+\dfrac29(3) = 1.67\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}V(X_1\mid X_2) &= \sigma_{11}-\dfrac{\sigma_{12}^2}{\sigma_{22}} \\[4pt] &= 4-\dfrac49 = 3.56\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ the same for every $x_2$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Multivariate Normal Distribution

    Q3. If $X\sim N_2\left(\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}\right)$, the distribution of $X_1-X_2$ is:
    1. $N(-1,3)$
    2. $N(-1,5)$
    3. $N(-1,7)$
    4. $N(3,3)$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $N(-1,3)$.

    With $a=(1,-1)'$: $a'\mu=1-2=-1$ and $a'\Sigma a=2+3-2(1)=3$. Every linear combination of a multivariate normal vector is univariate normal. Ignoring the covariance gives 5; adding it gives 7.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Multivariate Normal Distribution

    Q4. If $X\sim N_3(\mu,\Sigma)$ with $\Sigma$ positive definite, the mean and variance of $Q=(X-\mu)'\Sigma^{-1}(X-\mu)$ are:
    1. 3 and 3
    2. 1 and 2
    3. 3 and 6
    4. 3 and 9
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 3 and 6.

    $Z=\Sigma^{-1/2}(X-\mu)\sim N_3(0,I)$ and $Q=Z'Z\sim\chi^2_3$, with mean $p=3$ and variance $2p=6$. This is why contours of constant density are ellipsoids and $P(Q\le\chi^2_{p,\alpha})=1-\alpha$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Multivariate Normal Distribution

    Q5. $(X_1,X_2)'$ is bivariate normal with unit variances and correlation $\rho$. Then $X_1+X_2$ and $X_1-X_2$ are independent:
    1. only when $\rho=0$
    2. for every value of $\rho$
    3. only when $\rho\gt0$
    4. for no value of $\rho$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) for every value of $\rho$.

    $$\begin{aligned}&\operatorname{Cov}(X_1+X_2,X_1-X_2) \\[4pt] &\quad = V(X_1)-V(X_2) = 1-1 = 0\end{aligned}$$ whatever $\rho$ is. The two are jointly normal (linear functions of a normal vector), so zero covariance implies independence. Equal variances are what matter, not $\rho$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Multivariate Normal Distribution

    Q6. Consider the statements about a random vector $X$:(I) If $X$ is multivariate normal, every marginal distribution is normal.(II) If every component of $X$ is normal, then $X$ is multivariate normal.(III) If $X$ is multivariate normal, components with zero covariance are independent.(IV) $X$ is multivariate normal if and only if every linear combination $a'X$ is univariate normal.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I, III and IV only
    2. I and III only
    3. I, II and III only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) I, III and IV only.

    (I), (III) and (IV) are standard; (IV) is often taken as the definition. (II) is false: take $X_1\sim N(0,1)$ and $X_2=SX_1$, where $S=\pm1$ with probability $\tfrac12$ independently. Both are $N(0,1)$, but $X_1+X_2$ equals 0 with probability $\tfrac12$, so the pair is not bivariate normal.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Multivariate Normal Distribution

    Q7. For the bivariate normal density with covariance matrix $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, the constant multiplying the exponential term is:
    1. $\dfrac{1}{2\pi}$
    2. $\dfrac{1}{6\pi}$
    3. $\dfrac{1}{4\pi}$
    4. $\dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt3}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt3}$.

    The constant is $(2\pi)^{-p/2}|\Sigma|^{-1/2}$ with $p=2$ and $|\Sigma|=4-1=3$: $\dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt3}$. $|\Sigma|$ is the generalised variance.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Multivariate Normal Distribution

    Q8. For $\mu=(0,0)'$ and $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1&0.5\\0.5&1\end{pmatrix}$, the squared Mahalanobis distance of the point $x=(1,1)'$ from $\mu$ is:
    1. 2.00
    2. 1.00
    3. 1.33
    4. 4.00
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 1.33.

    $$\begin{aligned}&\Sigma^{-1} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{1}{0.75}\begin{pmatrix}1&-0.5\\-0.5&1\end{pmatrix}\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ so $$\begin{aligned}x'\Sigma^{-1}x &= \dfrac{1-0.5-0.5+1}{0.75} \\[4pt] &= 1.33\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ It is less than the squared Euclidean distance 2 because the point lies along the direction in which the positively correlated variables vary together.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Multivariate Normal Distribution

    Q9. For a random sample of size $n=10$ from $N_p(\mu,\Sigma)$ with $S=\dfrac{1}{n-1}\sum(X_i-\bar X)(X_i-\bar X)'$, the maximum likelihood estimator of $\Sigma$ is:
    1. $S$
    2. $0.9\,S$
    3. $1.1\,S$
    4. $0.81\,S$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $0.9\,S$.

    $$\begin{aligned}\hat\Sigma &= \dfrac1n\sum(X_i-\bar X)(X_i-\bar X)' \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{n-1}{n}S = 0.9S\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ It is biased; $S$ is the unbiased estimator.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Estimation of Mean Vector and Covariance Matrix

    Q10. For a random sample of size $n$ from $N_p(\mu,\Sigma)$, consider the statements:(I) $\bar X\sim N_p\left(\mu,\tfrac1n\Sigma\right)$.(II) $\bar X$ and $S$ are independent.(III) $(n-1)S\sim W_p(n-1,\Sigma)$.(IV) $S$ is positive definite with probability 1 if and only if $n\gt p$.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and II only
    2. I, II and III only
    3. I, III and IV only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) I, II, III and IV.

    (I)–(III) are the multivariate analogue of the independence of $\bar X$ and $s^2$ in normal samples. (IV) $(n-1)S$ is a sum of $n-1$ independent rank-one matrices, so it has full rank $p$ (with probability 1) exactly when $n-1\ge p$. All four are true.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Estimation of Mean Vector and Covariance Matrix

    Q11. For a random sample from $N_p(\mu,\Sigma)$ with both parameters unknown, a sufficient statistic is:
    1. $(\bar X,S)$
    2. $\bar X$ alone
    3. $S$ alone
    4. $(\bar X,|S|)$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $(\bar X,S)$.

    The likelihood depends on the data only through $\bar X$ and $\sum(X_i-\bar X)(X_i-\bar X)'=(n-1)S$, so by the factorisation theorem $(\bar X,S)$ is sufficient; it is also complete.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Estimation of Mean Vector and Covariance Matrix

    Q12. For a random sample of size $n=5$ from $N_2(\mu,\Sigma)$, the maximum likelihood estimate of the generalised variance $|\Sigma|$, in terms of the sample covariance matrix $S$ (divisor $n-1$), is:
    1. $|S|$
    2. $0.8\,|S|$
    3. $0.64\,|S|$
    4. $1.25\,|S|$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $0.64\,|S|$.

    By invariance, the estimate is $$\begin{aligned}|\hat\Sigma| &= \left|\dfrac{n-1}{n}S\right| \\[4pt] &= \left(\dfrac{n-1}{n}\right)^p|S| = (0.8)^2|S| \\[4pt] &= 0.64|S|\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ A scalar multiplying a $p\times p$ matrix comes out of the determinant to the power $p$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Estimation of Mean Vector and Covariance Matrix

    Q13. Three observations on $(X_1,X_2)$ are $(1,2)$, $(3,4)$ and $(5,9)$. The sample covariance $s_{12}$ (divisor $n-1$) and the sample correlation coefficient are:
    1. 4.67 and 0.971
    2. 7 and 0.971
    3. 7 and 0.538
    4. 14 and 0.971
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) 7 and 0.971.

    The means are 3 and 5; the deviations are $(-2,0,2)$ and $(-3,-1,4)$. $s_{12}=\dfrac{6+0+8}{2}=7$, $s_{11}=\dfrac82=4$, $s_{22}=\dfrac{26}{2}=13$, and $r=\dfrac{7}{\sqrt{52}}=0.971$. The correlation does not depend on the divisor.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Estimation of Mean Vector and Covariance Matrix

    Q14. $\bar X$ is the mean of a random sample of size 25 from $N_2(\mu,\Sigma)$ with $\Sigma$ known. The distribution of $25(\bar X-\mu)'\Sigma^{-1}(\bar X-\mu)$ is:
    1. $\chi^2_{25}$
    2. $F(2,23)$
    3. $\chi^2_{24}$
    4. $\chi^2_{2}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $\chi^2_{2}$.

    $\sqrt n(\bar X-\mu)\sim N_p(0,\Sigma)$, so the quadratic form is $\chi^2_p=\chi^2_2$ whatever $n$ is. It gives an elliptical confidence region for $\mu$. When $\Sigma$ is replaced by $S$, the statistic becomes Hotelling's $T^2$, related to $F(2,23)$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Distribution of Sample Mean Vector

    Q15. Given below are two statements, one labelled Assertion (A) and the other Reason (R).Assertion (A): For a large random sample from any $p$-variate population with finite covariance matrix, $n(\bar X-\mu)'S^{-1}(\bar X-\mu)$ is approximately $\chi^2_p$.Reason (R): By the multivariate central limit theorem $\sqrt n(\bar X-\mu)$ is approximately $N_p(0,\Sigma)$, and $S$ converges in probability to $\Sigma$.Choose the correct answer.
    1. Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A)
    2. Both (A) and (R) are true but (R) is not the correct explanation of (A)
    3. (A) is true but (R) is false
    4. (A) is false but (R) is true
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) Both (A) and (R) are true and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

    The central limit theorem makes $\sqrt n(\bar X-\mu)$ approximately normal without any assumption of normality, and replacing $\Sigma$ by a consistent estimator does not change the limit (Slutsky). So the quadratic form is approximately $\chi^2_p$. Both statements are true and (R) is the reason.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Distribution of Sample Mean Vector

    Q16. $\bar X$ is the mean of a sample of size 7 from a bivariate population with covariance matrix $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$. The variance of $\bar X_1+\bar X_2$ is:
    1. 0.71
    2. 5
    3. 1
    4. 7
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 1.

    With $a=(1,1)'$: $$\begin{aligned}V(a'\bar X) &= \dfrac{a'\Sigma a}{n} \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{2+3+2(1)}{7} = 1\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Distribution of Sample Mean Vector

    Q17. If $A\sim W_p(10,\Sigma)$ and $\sigma_{11}=2$, the mean and variance of the element $a_{11}$ are:
    1. 20 and 40
    2. 20 and 80
    3. 2 and 8
    4. 10 and 20
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) 20 and 80.

    $\dfrac{a_{11}}{\sigma_{11}}\sim\chi^2_{10}$, so $E(a_{11})=10(2)=20$ and $$\begin{aligned}V(a_{11}) &= 2n\sigma_{11}^2 = 2(10)(4) \\[4pt] &= 80\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ In general $E(A)=n\Sigma$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Wishart Distribution

    Q18. Consider the statements about the Wishart distribution:(I) If $A_1\sim W_p(n_1,\Sigma)$ and $A_2\sim W_p(n_2,\Sigma)$ are independent, then $A_1+A_2\sim W_p(n_1+n_2,\Sigma)$.(II) If $A\sim W_p(n,\Sigma)$ and $C$ is a $q\times p$ matrix of rank $q$, then $CAC'\sim W_q(n,C\Sigma C')$.(III) If $A\sim W_p(n,\Sigma)$, then $\dfrac{a'Aa}{a'\Sigma a}\sim\chi^2_n$ for every fixed $a\ne0$.(IV) The sum of two independent Wishart matrices with different $\Sigma$ is again Wishart.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I, II and III only
    2. I and II only
    3. I and III only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) I, II and III only.

    (I) is the additive property, (II) the transformation property and (III) its special case $q=1$; they mirror the properties of chi-square, of which the Wishart is the matrix generalisation. (IV) is false, just as $\sigma_1^2\chi^2+\sigma_2^2\chi^2$ is not a multiple of a chi-square.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Wishart Distribution

    Q19. If $A\sim W_2(5,\Sigma)$, then $E|A|$ equals:
    1. $5|\Sigma|$
    2. $25|\Sigma|$
    3. $10|\Sigma|$
    4. $20|\Sigma|$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $20|\Sigma|$.

    $\dfrac{|A|}{|\Sigma|}$ is distributed as the product of independent $\chi^2_n,\chi^2_{n-1},\dots,\chi^2_{n-p+1}$ variables. So $$\begin{aligned}E|A| &= |\Sigma|\,n(n-1)\cdots(n-p+1) \\[4pt] &= |\Sigma|(5)(4) = 20|\Sigma|\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Wishart Distribution

    Q20. If $A\sim W_3(10,\Sigma)$ and $a$ is a fixed non-zero vector, then $\dfrac{a'\Sigma^{-1}a}{a'A^{-1}a}$ has the chi-square distribution with degrees of freedom:
    1. 10
    2. 7
    3. 8
    4. 3
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 8.

    For the inverse, the degrees of freedom drop to $n-p+1=10-3+1=8$. (Compare $\dfrac{a'Aa}{a'\Sigma a}\sim\chi^2_n$.) This result is what gives Hotelling's $T^2$ its $F$ distribution.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Wishart Distribution

    Q21. The Wishart distribution $W_p(n,\Sigma)$ with $\Sigma$ positive definite has a density if and only if:
    1. $n\gt2p$
    2. $n\ge p$
    3. $n\ge1$
    4. $n\ge p^2$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $n\ge p$.

    $A=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_iZ_i'$ with $Z_i$ independent $N_p(0,\Sigma)$ is non-singular with probability 1 exactly when $n\ge p$; for $n\lt p$ it is singular and has no density on the space of positive definite matrices.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Wishart Distribution

    Q22. If $r_{12}=0.6$, $r_{13}=0.5$ and $r_{23}=0.4$, the partial correlation coefficient $r_{12.3}$ is about:
    1. 0.504
    2. 0.400
    3. 0.476
    4. 0.600
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 0.504.

    $$\begin{aligned}&r_{12.3} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{r_{12}-r_{13}r_{23}}{\sqrt{(1-r_{13}^2)(1-r_{23}^2)}} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{0.6-0.2}{\sqrt{0.75\times0.84}} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{0.4}{0.794} = 0.504\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Simple, Partial & Multiple Correlation

    Q23. If $r_{12}=0.6$, $r_{13}=0.5$ and $r_{23}=0.4$, the multiple correlation coefficient $R_{1.23}$ is about:
    1. 0.781
    2. 0.440
    3. 0.600
    4. 0.664
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) 0.664.

    $$\begin{aligned}&R_{1.23}^2 \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{r_{12}^2+r_{13}^2-2r_{12}r_{13}r_{23}}{1-r_{23}^2} \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{0.36+0.25-0.24}{0.84} \\[4pt] &\quad = 0.440\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ so $R_{1.23}=0.664$. It exceeds both $|r_{12}|$ and $|r_{13}|$, as it must; $0.440$ is $R^2$, not $R$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Simple, Partial & Multiple Correlation

    Q24. Consider the statements about the multiple correlation coefficient $R_{1.23}$:(I) $R_{1.23}\ge|r_{12}|$ and $R_{1.23}\ge|r_{13}|$.(II) $0\le R_{1.23}\le1$.(III) $R_{1.23}$ can be negative.(IV) $1-R_{1.23}^2=(1-r_{12}^2)(1-r_{13.2}^2)$.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and II only
    2. I, II and III only
    3. I, II and IV only
    4. II and IV only
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) I, II and IV only.

    $R_{1.23}$ is the largest correlation between $X_1$ and any linear function of $X_2,X_3$, so it is at least as large as each simple correlation and is never negative: (I), (II) true, (III) false. (IV) is the standard identity: the variance left unexplained by $X_2$ is further reduced by the factor $1-r_{13.2}^2$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Simple, Partial & Multiple Correlation

    Q25. From a sample of 28 observations on three normal variables, $r_{12.3}=0.5$. The $t$ statistic for testing that the population partial correlation is zero, and its degrees of freedom, are:
    1. 2.94 on 26
    2. 2.89 on 25
    3. 3.06 on 25
    4. 2.89 on 26
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) 2.89 on 25.

    For a partial correlation with $k$ variables held fixed, $t=\dfrac{r\sqrt{n-2-k}}{\sqrt{1-r^2}}$ on $n-2-k$ degrees of freedom. Here $k=1$: $t=\dfrac{0.5\sqrt{25}}{\sqrt{0.75}}=2.89$ on 25 degrees of freedom.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Simple, Partial & Multiple Correlation

    Q26. From 23 observations on three normal variables, $R_{1.23}^2=0.5$. The $F$ statistic for testing that the population multiple correlation is zero is:
    1. 10
    2. 20
    3. 11
    4. 5
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 10.

    With $p-1=2$ explanatory variables, $$\begin{aligned}F &= \dfrac{R^2/(p-1)}{(1-R^2)/(n-p)} \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{0.5/2}{0.5/20} = 10\end{aligned}$$ on $(2,20)$ degrees of freedom.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Simple, Partial & Multiple Correlation

    Q27. A sample of 28 pairs from a bivariate normal population gives $r=0.6$. The standard error of Fisher's $z=\tfrac12\ln\dfrac{1+r}{1-r}$ is:
    1. 0.189
    2. 0.121
    3. 0.160
    4. 0.200
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) 0.200.

    $z$ is approximately normal with variance $\dfrac{1}{n-3}=\dfrac1{25}$, so the standard error is 0.200, whatever the value of $\rho$. Here $z=0.693$, and the 95% limits $0.693\pm0.392$ transform back to $(0.29,0.80)$ for $\rho$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Simple, Partial & Multiple Correlation

    Q28. Let $\Sigma^{-1}=(\sigma^{ij})$ be the inverse of the covariance matrix of $(X_1,\dots,X_p)'$. The partial correlation between $X_1$ and $X_2$, given all the other variables, is:
    1. $\dfrac{\sigma^{12}}{\sqrt{\sigma^{11}\sigma^{22}}}$
    2. $\dfrac{\sigma_{12}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{11}\sigma_{22}}}$
    3. $-\dfrac{\sigma^{12}}{\sqrt{\sigma^{11}\sigma^{22}}}$
    4. $1-\dfrac{1}{\sigma_{11}\sigma^{11}}$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) $-\dfrac{\sigma^{12}}{\sqrt{\sigma^{11}\sigma^{22}}}$.

    The off-diagonal elements of the concentration matrix $\Sigma^{-1}$, scaled and with the sign changed, are the partial correlations given the rest. So a zero in $\Sigma^{-1}$ means conditional independence in the normal case. Option B is the simple correlation, and option D is $R^2_{1.2\ldots p}$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Simple, Partial & Multiple Correlation

    Q29. A random sample of size $n=20$ from $N_3(\mu,\Sigma)$ is used to test $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$ by Hotelling's $T^2=n(\bar X-\mu_0)'S^{-1}(\bar X-\mu_0)$. Under $H_0$:
    1. $T^2\sim F(3,17)$
    2. $\dfrac{17}{57}T^2\sim F(3,17)$
    3. $\dfrac{17}{57}T^2\sim F(3,19)$
    4. $T^2\sim\chi^2_3$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\dfrac{17}{57}T^2\sim F(3,17)$.

    $\dfrac{n-p}{p(n-1)}T^2\sim F(p,n-p)$. With $n=20$, $p=3$: $\dfrac{17}{3\times19}=\dfrac{17}{57}$ and the degrees of freedom are $(3,17)$. $\chi^2_3$ is the limiting distribution for large $n$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Hotelling's $T^2$ Statistic

    Q30. For a sample of size 10 from a bivariate normal population, $\bar x-\mu_0=(1,2)'$ and $S=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&4\end{pmatrix}$. The values of Hotelling's $T^2$ and of the corresponding $F$ statistic are:
    1. 15 and 6.67
    2. 15 and 7.50
    3. 1.5 and 0.67
    4. 30 and 13.33
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) 15 and 6.67.

    $$\begin{aligned}T^2 &= n(\bar x-\mu_0)'S^{-1}(\bar x-\mu_0) \\[4pt] &= 10\left(\dfrac12+\dfrac44\right) = 15\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned}F &= \dfrac{n-p}{p(n-1)}T^2 \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{8}{18}(15) = 6.67\end{aligned}$$ on $(2,8)$ degrees of freedom.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Hotelling's $T^2$ Statistic

    Q31. Consider the statements about Hotelling's $T^2$ for testing $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$:(I) It is unchanged when the observations are transformed by $X\to CX+d$ with $C$ non-singular (and $\mu_0$ correspondingly).(II) For $p=1$ it reduces to the square of Student's $t$.(III) The likelihood ratio satisfies $\lambda^{2/n}=\left(1+\dfrac{T^2}{n-1}\right)^{-1}$.(IV) Its null distribution depends on the unknown $\Sigma$.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and II only
    2. II and III only
    3. I, II, III and IV
    4. I, II and III only
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) I, II and III only.

    (I)–(III) are standard: $T^2$ is invariant, generalises $t^2$ and is equivalent to the likelihood ratio test. (IV) is false: because of the invariance, the null distribution is the same for every $\Sigma$, namely a multiple of $F(p,n-p)$.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Hotelling's $T^2$ Statistic

    Q32. Two independent samples of sizes $n_1=n_2=10$ are drawn from trivariate normal populations with a common covariance matrix. The $F$ statistic derived from the two-sample Hotelling's $T^2$ has degrees of freedom:
    1. (3, 18)
    2. (3, 17)
    3. (3, 16)
    4. (2, 17)
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) (3, 16).

    $$\begin{aligned}&\dfrac{n_1+n_2-p-1}{p(n_1+n_2-2)}T^2 \\[4pt] &\quad \sim F(p,\;n_1+n_2-p-1) = F(3,16)\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

    Q33. Samples of sizes 10 and 15 from two $p$-variate normal populations with a common covariance matrix give the Mahalanobis distance $D^2=2$ between the sample mean vectors. The two-sample Hotelling's $T^2$ is:
    1. 50
    2. 12
    3. 6
    4. 25
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) 12.

    $$\begin{aligned}T^2 &= \dfrac{n_1n_2}{n_1+n_2}D^2 \\[4pt] &= \dfrac{150}{25}(2) = 12\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ where $$\begin{aligned}&D^2 \\[4pt] &\quad = (\bar x_1-\bar x_2)'S_p^{-1}(\bar x_1-\bar x_2)\end{aligned}$$ with $S_p$ the pooled covariance matrix.

    Q34. For testing $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$, let $t(a)$ be the one-sample $t$ statistic computed from the linear combination $a'X$. Then $\max_{a\ne0}t^2(a)$ equals:
    1. Hotelling's $T^2$
    2. $\dfrac{T^2}{p}$
    3. the largest characteristic root of $S$
    4. $\dfrac{n-p}{p(n-1)}T^2$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) Hotelling's $T^2$.

    $$\begin{aligned}t^2(a) &= \dfrac{n[a'(\bar x-\mu_0)]^2}{a'Sa}\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ and by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality its maximum over $a$ is $n(\bar x-\mu_0)'S^{-1}(\bar x-\mu_0)=T^2$, attained at $a\propto S^{-1}(\bar x-\mu_0)$. This is Roy's union–intersection derivation of $T^2$ and the basis of simultaneous confidence intervals.

    Q35. Two groups have $\bar x_1-\bar x_2=(2,1)'$ and pooled covariance matrix $S=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$. The coefficient vector of Fisher's linear discriminant function is proportional to:
    1. $(2,1)'$
    2. $(0,1)'$
    3. $(1,1)'$
    4. $(1,0)'$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) $(1,0)'$.

    $a=S^{-1}(\bar x_1-\bar x_2)$ with $$\begin{aligned}&S^{-1} \\[4pt] &\quad = \begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}\text{:}\end{aligned}$$ $a=(2-1,\,-2+2)'=(1,0)'$. Although the groups differ on both variables, the second adds nothing to the separation once the first is used.

    Q36. Fisher's linear discriminant function for two groups is $y=x_1$, and the group mean vectors are $(4,2)'$ and $(2,1)'$. With equal prior probabilities and equal costs, an observation $x=(3.5,0)'$ is allocated to:
    1. group 1, since $y=3.5$ exceeds the cut-off 3
    2. group 2, since $y=3.5$ exceeds the cut-off 3
    3. group 2, since $x_2=0$ is nearer to 1 than to 2
    4. neither group
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) group 1, since $y=3.5$ exceeds the cut-off 3.

    The cut-off is midway between the discriminant scores of the two means: $\tfrac12(4+2)=3$. Since group 1 has the larger mean score and $y=3.5\gt3$, the observation goes to group 1. The value of $x_2$ plays no part because its coefficient is zero.

    Read this in the notes: Unit VIII, Discriminant Analysis

    Q37. Two normal populations with a common covariance matrix have Mahalanobis distance $\Delta^2=4$. With equal prior probabilities, the probability of misclassification by the optimal linear rule is (given $\Phi(1)=0.8413$):
    1. 0.0228
    2. 0.3085
    3. 0.1587
    4. 0.5000
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 0.1587.

    Each error probability is $\Phi\left(-\dfrac\Delta2\right)=\Phi(-1)=0.1587$. The further apart the populations, in standard-deviation units, the smaller the error.

    Q38. Consider the statements about classification into one of two populations:(I) For normal populations with equal covariance matrices the optimal rule is linear.(II) For normal populations with unequal covariance matrices the optimal rule is quadratic.(III) Fisher's discriminant maximises the ratio of between-group to within-group variation of $a'x$.(IV) The linear discriminant function can be used only when the two samples are of equal size.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and II only
    2. I, II and III only
    3. I, III and IV only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) I, II and III only.

    The log of the ratio of two normal densities is linear in $x$ when the covariance matrices are equal and quadratic otherwise: (I), (II). (III) is Fisher's own criterion, which needs no normality. (IV) is false; unequal sample sizes only change the pooled covariance matrix, and unequal priors shift the cut-off.

    Q39. For the two groups with $\bar x_1-\bar x_2=(2,1)'$ and pooled covariance matrix $S=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, the Mahalanobis distance $D^2$ between the groups is:
    1. 5
    2. 3
    3. 1
    4. 2
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) 2.

    $D^2=d'S^{-1}d$ with $S^{-1}d=(1,0)'$, so $D^2=(2,1)(1,0)'=2$. It is also the difference between the two mean discriminant scores.

    Q40. For the covariance matrix $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}5&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}$, the proportion of the total variance explained by the first principal component is about:
    1. 71.4%
    2. 83.3%
    3. 85.7%
    4. 100%
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 85.7%.

    The characteristic equation is $\lambda^2-7\lambda+6=0$, so $\lambda_1=6$, $\lambda_2=1$. The total variance is the trace, 7, and the first component explains $\dfrac67=85.7\%$. ($71.4\%$ is the share of $X_1$ alone, $\tfrac57$.)

    Q41. For the covariance matrix $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}5&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}$, the first principal component is:
    1. $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(2X_1+X_2)$
    2. $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(X_1+2X_2)$
    3. $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(X_1+X_2)$
    4. $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(X_1-2X_2)$
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(2X_1+X_2)$.

    For $\lambda_1=6$: $(\Sigma-6I)e=0$ gives $-e_1+2e_2=0$, so $e\propto(2,1)'$, normalised by $\sqrt5$. Check: $V(2X_1+X_2)/5=\dfrac{20+2+8}{5}=6$. Option D is the second component, with variance 1.

    Q42. Principal components are extracted from the correlation matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&0.6\\0.6&1\end{pmatrix}$. The first component and the proportion of variance it explains are:
    1. $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(Z_1-Z_2)$ and 80%
    2. $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(Z_1+Z_2)$ and 80%
    3. $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(Z_1+Z_2)$ and 60%
    4. $Z_1$ and 50%
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(Z_1+Z_2)$ and 80%.

    The characteristic roots are $1+\rho=1.6$ and $1-\rho=0.4$, with vectors $(1,1)'/\sqrt2$ and $(1,-1)'/\sqrt2$. The first component explains $\dfrac{1.6}{2}=80\%$. For two standardised variables the components are always the sum and the difference, whatever $\rho$ is.

    Q43. Consider the statements about the principal components $Y_i=e_i'X$ of a covariance matrix $\Sigma$ with characteristic roots $\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_p$:(I) Different principal components are uncorrelated.(II) $\sum V(Y_i)=\operatorname{tr}\Sigma$.(III) The components are unchanged when the variables are measured in different units.(IV) $V(Y_i)=\lambda_i$.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I and II only
    2. I, II and III only
    3. I, II, III and IV
    4. I, II and IV only
    Show Answer

    Answer: (D) I, II and IV only.

    (I), (II) and (IV) follow from the spectral decomposition of $\Sigma$. (III) is false: principal components are not scale invariant; changing the units changes $\Sigma$ and its vectors, which is why the correlation matrix is used when the variables are on different scales.

    Q44. For $\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}5&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}$, the correlation between the first principal component $Y_1$ and $X_1$ is about:
    1. 0.894
    2. 0.816
    3. 0.980
    4. 0.447
    Show Answer

    Answer: (C) 0.980.

    $$\begin{aligned}&\operatorname{Corr}(Y_i,X_k) \\[4pt] &\quad = \dfrac{e_{ik}\sqrt{\lambda_i}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{kk}}}\text{.}\end{aligned}$$ With $e_{11}=\dfrac{2}{\sqrt5}$, $\lambda_1=6$, $\sigma_{11}=5$: $\dfrac{2\sqrt6}{5}=0.980$. The loading $e_{11}=0.894$ by itself is not the correlation.

    Q45. Four standardised variables all have pairwise correlation 0.5. The proportion of the total variance explained by the first principal component is:

    Q46. The characteristic roots of a $5\times5$ correlation matrix are 2.8, 1.2, 0.6, 0.3 and 0.1. By Kaiser's rule, the number of components retained and the cumulative proportion of variance they explain are:
    1. 2 and 80%
    2. 3 and 92%

    4. 2 and 56%

    Kaiser's rule keeps components whose roots exceed 1, the average root of a correlation matrix: here two, explaining $\dfrac{2.8+1.2}{5}=80\%$. A scree plot would show the same ‘elbow’ after the second root.

    Q48. When the first set consists of a single variable $X_1$ and the second set of $X_2,\dots,X_p$, the canonical correlation is:
    1. the largest simple correlation of $X_1$ with any $X_j$
    2. the largest partial correlation
    3. the multiple correlation coefficient $R_{1.23\ldots p}$
    4. always 1

    Q49. $X_1$ has unit variance; $(X_2,X_3)$ are uncorrelated with unit variances; and $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2)=0.6$, $\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_3)=0.3$. The canonical correlation between $\{X_1\}$ and $\{X_2,X_3\}$ is about:
    1. 0.600
    2. 0.671
    3. 0.450
    4. 0.900
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) 0.671.

    $$\begin{aligned}\rho^{*2} &= \Sigma_{11}^{-1}\Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21} \\[4pt] &= 0.6^2+0.3^2 = 0.45\text{,}\end{aligned}$$ so $\rho^*=0.671$. It is the multiple correlation of $X_1$ on $X_2,X_3$ and exceeds the larger simple correlation, 0.6.

    Q50. Consider the statements about canonical correlation analysis of two sets of variables:(I) Canonical correlations are unchanged by non-singular linear transformations within each set.(II) Canonical variates of the same set belonging to different pairs are uncorrelated.(III) The first canonical correlation is at least as large as the absolute value of any simple correlation between a variable of one set and a variable of the other.(IV) A canonical correlation can be negative.Which of the statements are correct?
    1. I, II and III only
    2. I and II only
    3. II and III only
    4. I, II, III and IV
    Show Answer

    Answer: (A) I, II and III only.

    (I) holds because the sets of linear combinations are unchanged. (II) is part of the construction: each new pair is chosen uncorrelated with the earlier ones. (III) holds because single variables are particular linear combinations. (IV) is false: canonical correlations are defined as non-negative square roots; a sign can always be absorbed into a canonical variate.
