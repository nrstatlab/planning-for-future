Topic Overview — What & Why

Unit IX is the environment unit, and the most fact-heavy of Paper I. Its questions turn on dates and counts (how many SDGs, when the Paris Agreement was adopted, how many missions the NAPCC has), on matching (a pollutant with its disease, an agreement with its subject), and on definitions (BOD, AQI, mitigation). Learn the tables in this unit; most questions are one of their rows.

  • Development goals: 8 MDGs for 2000–2015, then 17 SDGs with 169 targets for 2015–2030.
  • Pollution and waste: the main measures (AQI, BOD, decibels) and India's waste rules.
  • Climate: the greenhouse gases, the IPCC, and what climate change does to economies and politics.
  • Law and agreements: the 1986 Act, the 2008 plan with its eight missions, and the agreements from Stockholm 1972 to Paris 2015.

1. Development and Environment: the MDGs and the SDGs

Sustainable development, in the Brundtland Report, Our Common Future (1987), is "development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs".

Millennium Development GoalsSustainable Development Goals
AdoptedFrom the UN Millennium Declaration, September 2000In Transforming our World: the 2030 Agenda, September 2015
Period2000–20152015–2030
Number8 goals17 goals and 169 targets
Applied toMainly developing countriesAll countries

The eight MDGs: end extreme poverty and hunger; universal primary education; gender equality; reduce child mortality; improve maternal health; combat HIV/AIDS, malaria and other diseases; environmental sustainability; a global partnership for development.

The 17 SDGs: 1 no poverty; 2 zero hunger; 3 good health and well-being; 4 quality education; 5 gender equality; 6 clean water and sanitation; 7 affordable and clean energy; 8 decent work and economic growth; 9 industry, innovation and infrastructure; 10 reduced inequalities; 11 sustainable cities and communities; 12 responsible consumption and production; 13 climate action; 14 life below water; 15 life on land; 16 peace, justice and strong institutions; 17 partnerships for the goals.

EXAMPLE 1 A question asks which SDG is "quality education". SDG 4. Climate action is SDG 13; life below water, SDG 14.
EXAMPLE 2 In India, NITI Aayog publishes an SDG India Index that scores states on the goals. It is an example of turning the global goals into national measures.

2. Human and Environment Interaction

Anthropogenic means caused by human activity. The main activities and their impacts:

ActivityImpacts
AgricultureDeforestation, soil erosion, fertiliser and pesticide run-off, falling water tables
Industry and energyAir and water pollution, greenhouse gases, hazardous waste
UrbanisationLoss of land and wetlands, waste, heat islands, traffic pollution
MiningLand degradation, acid drainage, displacement of people
TransportVehicle emissions, noise, fragmentation of habitats

The IPAT identity sums it up: environmental Impact = Population × Affluence (consumption per person) × Technology (impact per unit of consumption).

EXAMPLE 1 Clearing mangroves for shrimp farms removes a coastal barrier against storm surges: an anthropogenic activity that raises a natural hazard's damage.
EXAMPLE 2 A city's population is stable, but each household doubles its electricity use from coal. Impact rises through A (affluence), not P.

EXAMPLE 1 Stubble burning in Punjab and Haryana adds to the air pollution of Delhi, hundreds of kilometres away. A regional issue.
EXAMPLE 2 Chlorofluorocarbons released anywhere thin the ozone layer over Antarctica. A global issue, which is why it needed a global treaty.

4. Pollution and Waste

Air

  • Main pollutants: particulate matter (PM10 and the finer PM2.5, under 2.5 micrometres), sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide, ground-level ozone, ammonia and lead.
  • India's National Air Quality Index (2014) reports air quality in six categories, from good to severe, based on eight pollutants.

Water

  • BOD (biochemical oxygen demand) is the oxygen micro-organisms need to break down the organic matter in water. A high BOD means heavy organic pollution, such as sewage.
  • Eutrophication: nutrients (nitrates, phosphates) feed algal blooms that use up the oxygen, and fish die.

Soil and noise

  • Soil pollution comes from pesticides, excess fertiliser, industrial waste and plastics; it reduces fertility and enters the food chain.
  • Noise is measured in decibels (dB), a logarithmic scale: every 10 dB is ten times the sound intensity. The Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules, 2000 set limits by zone: silence zones (near hospitals and schools) have the strictest.

Waste

WasteExamplesRules in India
SolidHousehold and market garbageSolid Waste Management Rules, 2016: segregation at source into wet, dry and domestic hazardous
LiquidSewage, industrial effluentThe Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974
BiomedicalHospital waste: dressings, sharps, body partsBio-Medical Waste Management Rules, 2016, with colour-coded bags (yellow, red, white, blue)
HazardousToxic, corrosive or flammable industrial wasteHazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Rules, 2016

The waste hierarchy, most preferred first: reduce, reuse, recycle, recover (energy), dispose.

EXAMPLE 1 A river stretch downstream of a town's sewage outfall shows a high BOD and low dissolved oxygen. The organic load is using up the oxygen.
EXAMPLE 2 Under extended producer responsibility, a phone maker must arrange for a share of its old phones to be collected and recycled. That is the principle of the E-Waste Rules.

5. Climate Change and its Dimensions

  • The greenhouse effect: gases in the atmosphere let sunlight in but trap outgoing heat. The six gases covered by the Kyoto Protocol are carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, hydrofluorocarbons, perfluorocarbons and sulphur hexafluoride. Carbon dioxide contributes most to warming; methane is far stronger per tonne but shorter-lived.
  • The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), set up in 1988 by the World Meteorological Organization and UNEP, assesses the science.
  • Socio-economic dimensions: lower crop yields, water stress, heat waves, coastal flooding, migration, and the heaviest burden on the poor, who contributed least.
  • Political dimensions: who should cut emissions and who should pay. The principle of common but differentiated responsibilities (CBDR), set out at Rio in 1992, holds that all countries must act but developed countries, which emitted most, should lead. Climate justice and the fund for loss and damage, agreed at the climate conference of 2022, follow from it.
  • Mitigation cuts emissions (renewable energy, efficiency, forests as carbon sinks); adaptation copes with change already happening (drought-resistant crops, sea walls, early warnings).
EXAMPLE 1 Planting mangroves along a coast both stores carbon (mitigation) and protects against storm surges (adaptation).
EXAMPLE 2 A question asks which principle lets developing countries take on less stringent commitments. Common but differentiated responsibilities.

Fossil fuels (coal, oil, gas) are non-renewable and the main source of carbon dioxide. India's updated Nationally Determined Contribution (2022) commits to reducing the emissions intensity of its GDP by 45% by 2030 from the 2005 level, and to about 50% of installed electric power capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030; India has announced a goal of net-zero emissions by 2070.

EXAMPLE 1 A village digester turns cattle dung into cooking gas and leaves slurry for fertiliser. Biomass energy, as biogas.
EXAMPLE 2 A question asks which source is renewable but location-bound and draws on the Earth's internal heat. Geothermal.

8. Natural Hazards and Disasters: Mitigation

  • A hazard is a threatening event (cyclone, earthquake, flood, drought, landslide, tsunami, heat wave); it becomes a disaster when it overwhelms a community's capacity to cope. Risk = hazard × vulnerability ÷ capacity, in the usual shorthand.
  • The disaster management cycle: mitigation and preparedness before, response during, recovery and reconstruction after.
  • Mitigation is structural (embankments, cyclone shelters, earthquake-resistant buildings) or non-structural (zoning, building codes, insurance, early warning, awareness).
  • In India, the Disaster Management Act, 2005 created the National Disaster Management Authority, chaired by the Prime Minister, and state and district authorities; the National Disaster Response Force is the specialised response force.
  • Internationally, the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction 2015–2030 followed the Hyogo Framework (2005–2015).
EXAMPLE 1 Odisha's cyclone shelters, early warnings and evacuation plans cut deaths sharply between the cyclone of 1999 and later storms of similar strength. Mitigation and preparedness at work.
EXAMPLE 2 A town forbids new houses on a river's flood plain. Non-structural mitigation, by land-use zoning.

9. The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986

  • Enacted after the Bhopal gas tragedy of 1984, under Article 253 of the Constitution, to give effect to the decisions of the Stockholm Conference of 1972.
  • An umbrella law: it empowers the Central Government to take all measures to protect and improve the environment, set standards for emissions and discharges, regulate hazardous substances, and close or restrict polluting industries.
  • Many rules are made under it: the Environment Impact Assessment notification (2006), and the waste rules above.
  • Other laws to know: the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972; the Water Act, 1974; the Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980; the Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981; the Biological Diversity Act, 2002; the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010.
EXAMPLE 1 A new highway project must have its environmental impact assessed before clearance. That requirement comes from the EIA notification issued under the 1986 Act.
EXAMPLE 2 A question asks which law followed the Bhopal disaster as an umbrella environmental statute. The Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.

10. The National Action Plan on Climate Change

The NAPCC was released in 2008. It has eight national missions:

  1. National Solar Mission
  2. National Mission for Enhanced Energy Efficiency
  3. National Mission on Sustainable Habitat
  4. National Water Mission
  5. National Mission for Sustaining the Himalayan Ecosystem
  6. National Mission for a Green India
  7. National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture
  8. National Mission on Strategic Knowledge for Climate Change

States prepare their own State Action Plans on Climate Change in the same frame.

EXAMPLE 1 A question lists five missions and asks which is not in the NAPCC. Anything outside the eight above, such as a "National Nuclear Mission", is the answer.
EXAMPLE 2 Afforestation of degraded land falls under the Green India Mission; rooftop solar targets, under the Solar Mission.
