    Topic Overview — What & Why

    Unit I asks whether you understand teaching as a planned, two-way process rather than as talking to a room. Its questions are of three kinds: who said what (the levels of teaching and their proponents, Bloom's domains, andragogy), which is the better practice (a learner-centred method for a given aim, the right kind of evaluation for a given purpose), and how the Indian system works (SWAYAM, CBCS grading, computer-based tests).

    • Teaching is an interaction between a teacher, learners and content, aimed at learning; its objectives are usually stated through Bloom's three domains.
    • Levels: memory (Herbart), understanding (Morrison), reflective (Hunt) — from teacher-dominated to learner-centred.
    • Learners differ by age (adolescents and adults learn differently: pedagogy and andragogy) and individually (intelligence, style, pace, background).
    • Methods run from teacher-centred (lecture, demonstration) to learner-centred (discussion, project, problem-solving, case study), and from classroom to online (SWAYAM, MOOCs).
    • Evaluation checks whether objectives were met: formative or summative, norm- or criterion-referenced; in Indian universities it runs on CBCS credits and grade points.

    1. Teaching: Concept and Objectives

    Teaching is a planned interaction in which a teacher arranges content, activities and conditions so that learners learn. Its test is the learning that results, not the talk that was given.

    Three ideas about teaching recur in the paper:

    • Teaching is a tripolar process. It joins the teacher, the learner and the content (or curriculum), in a social setting. Older views were bipolar: teacher and learner only.
    • Teaching is both an art and a science. An art because it depends on the teacher's skill and judgement in the moment; a science because its methods can be studied, tested and improved.
    • Teaching has phases. Philip Jackson distinguished the pre-active phase (planning: objectives, content, method) from the interactive phase (the class itself: presenting, questioning, responding); the post-active phase (evaluating and revising) is usually added as the third. Questions attribute all three to Jackson.

    Objectives: Bloom's three domains

    The objectives of teaching are usually written as changes in what learners know, feel and can do. Benjamin Bloom and colleagues (1956) set out the cognitive domain; later work added the other two.

    DomainConcernsLevels, lowest to highest
    Cognitive (Bloom, 1956)Knowing and thinkingKnowledge, comprehension, application, analysis, synthesis, evaluation
    Cognitive, revised (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001)The same, as verbsRemember, understand, apply, analyse, evaluate, create
    Affective (Krathwohl, Bloom & Masia, 1964)Feelings, values, attitudesReceiving, responding, valuing, organisation, characterisation by a value
    Psychomotor (Simpson, 1972; Dave, 1970)Physical skillsSimpson: perception, set, guided response, mechanism, complex overt response, adaptation, origination. Dave: imitation, manipulation, precision, articulation, naturalisation

    Note the change at the top of the cognitive domain: in the revision, create (once "synthesis") is the highest level, above evaluate.

    EXAMPLE 1 "Students will be able to list the steps of research" is a remember objective; "students will design a study to test a claim" is a create objective. The verb tells you the level.
    EXAMPLE 2 A teacher who wants learners to value honesty in reporting data is writing an affective objective. It is tested by what they do (do they report a result that went against them?), not by a recall question.

    Maxims of teaching

    The classic maxims say in which order to present content: known to unknown, simple to complex, concrete to abstract, particular to general, whole to part, analysis to synthesis, empirical to rational and psychological to logical (start from the learner's interest, end with the subject's logical order).

    2. Levels of Teaching: Memory, Understanding, Reflective

    The three levels describe how deeply a lesson engages the learner's mind. Each assumes the one below it.

    LevelProponentAimRoles
    Memory levelJ. F. HerbartRecall of facts; rote learningTeacher dominant, learner passive; the lowest, "thoughtless" level
    Understanding levelH. C. MorrisonGrasp of meaning, relations and principles; "memory plus insight"Teacher still leads; learner more active
    Reflective levelH. Hunt (Morris L. Bigge and Maurice P. Hunt)Problem-solving, critical and original thinkingLearner-centred; teacher creates the problem situation; the highest level
    • The Herbartian five steps (set out by Herbart's followers from his teaching) for a memory-level lesson: preparation, presentation, comparison (association), generalisation, application.
    • Morrison's teaching model for the understanding level: exploration, presentation, assimilation, organisation, recitation.
    • Hunt's reflective level works through a felt problem: the learner defines it, forms and tests hypotheses, and draws conclusions.
    EXAMPLE 1 Learners repeat the dates of the five-year plans until they can say them back: memory level. They explain why the plans moved from agriculture to industry: understanding level. They argue what India's next plan should prioritise, using evidence: reflective level.
    EXAMPLE 2 A question asks which level is most learner-centred and develops critical thinking. The answer is the reflective level; the memory level is the most teacher-centred.

    3. Characteristics and Basic Requirements of Teaching

    Characteristics of good teaching, as the paper asks about them:

    • it is interactive and two-way, with feedback in both directions;
    • it is purposeful: it has stated objectives and is planned;
    • it is diagnostic and remedial: it finds what learners do not yet know and acts on it;
    • it is democratic: it respects the learner, invites questions and allows disagreement;
    • it is a professional activity that can be observed, analysed and improved;
    • it ends in learning; teaching that changes nothing in the learner has not succeeded.

    Basic requirements are what must be in place for teaching to happen at all: a teacher with command of the subject and the skill to communicate it; learners who are ready (prior knowledge, motivation); content suited to them; a method suited to the objective; time, space and materials; and a way to evaluate what was learnt.

    EXAMPLE 1 A brilliant lecture that no student can follow has a teacher and content, but fails the requirement of learner readiness: the teacher did not start from what the learners knew.
    EXAMPLE 2 A teacher notices, from the answers to a short quiz, that half the class confuses mean and median, and teaches it again with a new example. This is the diagnostic and remedial character of teaching.

    4. Adolescent and Adult Learners

    The syllabus asks about four kinds of characteristic: academic, social, emotional and cognitive.

    Adolescent learners (about 12–19)Adult learners
    AcademicNeed structure and guidance; motivation often external (marks, approval)Self-directed; want learning they can use now; bring a lot of experience
    SocialPeer group matters greatly; seek independence from adultsRoles as workers, parents, citizens; learn well from one another
    EmotionalRapid change; identity forming; sensitive to criticismMore stable; may fear failure in front of others; resist being treated as children
    CognitiveMove into abstract thought: Piaget's formal operational stage (from about 11)Reason from experience; problem-centred; may be slower at rote tasks, better at judgement

    Two names to know:

    • Erik Erikson: adolescence is the stage of identity versus role confusion.
    • Malcolm Knowles popularised andragogy, the art and science of helping adults learn, as against pedagogy for children. His assumptions about adults: they need to know why they are learning; they have a self-concept of being responsible for themselves; their experience is a resource; they are ready to learn what their life roles need; their orientation is problem-centred; and their motivation is mainly internal.
    EXAMPLE 1 A course for working nurses on data handling starts with a real ward problem the nurses bring, not with definitions. That follows andragogy: problem-centred, using the learners' experience.
    EXAMPLE 2 A first-year undergraduate class works better in small peer groups with clear tasks and early feedback, because adolescents are peer-oriented and sensitive to public correction.

    5. Individual Differences

    Individual differences are the ways in which learners of the same age and class differ: in intelligence, aptitude, prior knowledge, pace, interests, learning style, motivation, personality and background.
    • Causes: heredity and environment (home, schooling, language, economic position), and their interaction.
    • Intelligence is not one thing on some accounts: Howard Gardner (1983) proposed multiple intelligences — linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinaesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal, and later naturalistic.
    • Learning styles, such as Neil Fleming's VARK (visual, aural, read/write, kinaesthetic), describe preferences. Research does not show that matching teaching to a preferred style improves learning, so use varied modes for everyone rather than labelling learners.
    • What the teacher does: varied methods and materials; differentiated tasks; enrichment for the fast and remedial help for the slow; self-paced and online resources; grouping that changes.
    EXAMPLE 1 In one class, some students solve a problem at once and others need a worked example. Giving the first group an extension task and the second a guided one is differentiated instruction.
    EXAMPLE 2 A question asks which practice best addresses individual differences: "the same lecture for all, at the same pace" is wrong; "varied methods, with self-paced material and remedial help" is right.

    6. Factors Affecting Teaching

    The syllabus names six, and questions often ask you to place a factor in its group.

    FactorIncludes
    TeacherSubject knowledge, communication skill, personality, attitude, enthusiasm, preparation, experience, health
    LearnerReadiness, prior knowledge, motivation, ability, interest, attention, health, family background
    Support materialTextbooks, notes, models, charts, audio-visual aids, e-content
    Instructional facilitiesClassrooms, labs, library, projectors, internet, seating, lighting
    Learning environmentClassroom climate, discipline, teacher–student relations, peer relations, freedom to ask
    InstitutionPolicies, time-table, class size, leadership, resources, academic culture
    EXAMPLE 1 A class of 150 in a hall without a microphone: class size is an institutional factor, the missing microphone an instructional facility.
    EXAMPLE 2 Students who are afraid to ask questions because they were mocked before: a learning environment factor, which the teacher can change.

    7. Teacher-centred and Learner-centred Methods

    Teacher-centredLearner-centred
    Who is activeThe teacher presents; learners receiveLearners inquire, discuss, do; the teacher guides
    ExamplesLecture, demonstration, recitationDiscussion, seminar, project, problem-solving, case study, brainstorming, role play, flipped classroom, cooperative learning
    Good forLarge classes, new information, limited timeUnderstanding and reflective aims, skills, attitudes

    Methods and their originators, often asked:

    • Project method: W. H. Kilpatrick (1918), from John Dewey's ideas; a purposeful activity in a real setting.
    • Heuristic method: H. E. Armstrong; the learner finds out for themselves.
    • Dalton plan: Helen Parkhurst; assignments done at the learner's pace.
    • Programmed instruction: B. F. Skinner; small steps, immediate feedback (linear programming).
    • Personalised System of Instruction (the Keller plan): Fred Keller; self-paced units, mastery before moving on.
    • Brainstorming: Alex Osborn; many ideas first, judgement later.
    • Case study method: associated with Harvard Law and Business Schools.
    • Flipped classroom: learners meet the content before class (video, reading) and use class time for problems and discussion.
    EXAMPLE 1 To teach the steps of a sample survey to 30 students, a teacher has them plan and run a small survey of the campus canteen. That is the project method: learner-centred and real.
    EXAMPLE 2 A question asks for the method that best develops problem-solving in a large class with little time. A short lecture followed by a problem worked in pairs (a mix) usually beats either alone; if one option must be chosen, prefer the learner-centred one that fits the aim.

    8. Off-line and On-line Methods: SWAYAM, SWAYAM Prabha, MOOCs

    Off-line methods are face-to-face, in a room at a set time. On-line methods use the internet, and may be synchronous (live: a webinar, a live class) or asynchronous (any time: recorded lectures, forums). Blended learning mixes the two.

    MOOC — a Massive Open Online Course: open to anyone, usually free to join, for very large numbers, delivered online. The term was coined in 2008 for an open online course run by George Siemens and Stephen Downes.

    SWAYAM

    • SWAYAM stands for Study Webs of Active-Learning for Young Aspiring Minds. It is the Government of India's MOOC platform, launched in July 2017, with courses from school to postgraduate level.
    • Its courses follow a four-quadrant approach: (1) e-tutorial (video lectures), (2) e-content (readable material), (3) self-assessment (quizzes, assignments), (4) a discussion forum.
    • Courses are prepared by national coordinators, among them AICTE, NPTEL, UGC, CEC, NCERT, NIOS and IGNOU.
    • Credit transfer: under the UGC's credit framework regulations for online learning through SWAYAM, a university may let students earn part of a semester's credits through SWAYAM courses; the limit was raised from 20% to 40% of the courses in a semester in 2021.

    SWAYAM Prabha

    • A group of educational DTH (direct-to-home) television channels, broadcasting 24 hours a day by satellite, for learners without good internet. It began in 2017 with 32 channels; the number has since grown, so check the current count on its site before quoting one.
    EXAMPLE 1 A student in a village with weak internet follows a statistics course on a SWAYAM Prabha channel, then takes the course's quiz on SWAYAM when connected. That is off-line and on-line used together.
    EXAMPLE 2 A question lists the four quadrants of a SWAYAM course and asks which is not one. "Classroom attendance" is not; e-tutorial, e-content, self-assessment and discussion forum are.

    9. Teaching Support Systems: Traditional, Modern, ICT-based

    SystemExamples
    TraditionalChalkboard, textbook, charts, maps, models, specimens, flannel board
    ModernOverhead and slide projectors, audio and video recordings, radio and television lessons, language labs
    ICT-basedInteractive (smart) boards, presentation software, learning management systems (Moodle and the like), MOOCs, simulations, virtual labs, e-books, video conferencing

    Edgar Dale's Cone of Experience (1946) arranges teaching aids from the most concrete at the base (direct, purposeful experiences) through contrived experiences, demonstrations, field trips, exhibits, television and film, recordings and pictures, visual symbols, to the most abstract at the top (verbal symbols). The higher an aid sits, the more it depends on words alone.

    Aids are also grouped by sense: audio (radio, recordings), visual (chart, slide, model) and audio-visual (film, television, video).

    EXAMPLE 1 A field visit to a dairy to see quality control is near the base of Dale's cone; reading a definition of quality control is at the top.
    EXAMPLE 2 A virtual lab that lets students change a sample size and watch the sampling distribution change is an ICT-based support, and closer to the concrete end than a lecture on the same idea.

    10. Evaluation: Elements and Types

    Evaluation is the systematic process of judging how far the objectives of teaching have been achieved. It is wider than measurement (assigning numbers) and assessment (collecting evidence): it adds a value judgement.

    Elements. Evaluation is tied to two other things in a triangle: the objectives, the learning experiences that serve them, and the evaluation that checks them. Change one and the others must follow.

    Types by purpose and timing:

    TypeWhenPurpose
    PlacementBefore teachingDecide where a learner should start
    FormativeDuring teachingFeedback to improve learning while it is going on
    DiagnosticWhen difficulties persistFind the causes of a learning difficulty
    SummativeAt the endJudge and certify what was achieved; grades
    • The terms formative and summative were introduced by Michael Scriven (1967), for evaluating curricula.
    • Norm-referenced evaluation compares a learner with others (rank, percentile); criterion-referenced compares with a fixed standard (did they reach 60%? can they do the task?). Robert Glaser made the distinction in 1963.
    • Continuous and comprehensive evaluation covers both scholastic and co-scholastic areas, throughout the year.
    • A good test is valid (measures what it claims to), reliable (consistent), objective (free of the marker's bias) and usable (practical to give and score).
    EXAMPLE 1 A weekly ungraded quiz whose results change the next week's lessons is formative. The end-of-semester examination is summative.
    EXAMPLE 2 "Top 6% of candidates qualify" is norm-referenced; "everyone scoring 40% or more qualifies" is criterion-referenced.

    11. Evaluation in the Choice Based Credit System

    The Choice Based Credit System (CBCS) was introduced by the UGC in Indian universities from 2015–16. Students choose from core, elective and skill courses, earn credits for each, and are graded on a ten-point scale.

    • Courses: core; elective (discipline-specific, generic, and dissertation or project); ability enhancement (compulsory courses, and skill enhancement courses).
    • A credit stands for one hour of teaching (lecture or tutorial), or two hours of practical work, a week through a semester.
    Letter gradeOA+AB+BCPFAb
    MeaningOutstandingExcellentVery goodGoodAbove averageAveragePassFailAbsent
    Grade point1098765400

    SGPA (semester grade point average) is the credit-weighted mean of the grade points: the sum of (credits × grade point) over the semester's courses, divided by the sum of the credits. CGPA is the same over all semesters so far.

    EXAMPLE 1 Three courses of 4, 4 and 2 credits with grade points 9, 7 and 10. SGPA = (4×9 + 4×7 + 2×10) / (4 + 4 + 2) = (36 + 28 + 20) / 10 = 8.4.
    EXAMPLE 2 Semester 1: 20 credits, SGPA 8.0. Semester 2: 22 credits, SGPA 7.0. CGPA = (20×8.0 + 22×7.0) / 42 = (160 + 154) / 42 = 314 / 42 ≈ 7.48. It is not the simple average 7.5, because the semesters carry different credits.

    12. Computer-based Testing and Innovations in Evaluation

    In computer-based testing (CBT) the candidate reads and answers on a computer at a test centre, and the machine records and scores the answers. UGC NET itself is held this way by the National Testing Agency.

    • For it: quick and accurate scoring; questions shuffled for each candidate; secure delivery; analysis of every item.
    • Against it: needs equipment and power at every centre; unfamiliar to some candidates; suits objective questions better than long answers.
    • Computer adaptive testing chooses each next question from the candidate's answers so far: harder after a right answer, easier after a wrong one.

    Innovations in evaluation that questions name: grading in place of marks; continuous internal assessment; open-book examinations; online and on-demand examinations; question banks; rubrics; e-portfolios; peer and self-assessment; and credit banks such as the Academic Bank of Credits, which stores the credits a student earns so they can be carried between institutions.

    EXAMPLE 1 A rubric for a project report gives separate scores for the question, the method, the analysis and the writing, each with a described standard. It makes marking more objective and tells the student where they lost marks.
    EXAMPLE 2 In an adaptive test two candidates may see different questions and still be scored on the same scale, because each question's difficulty is known in advance.
    Exam pointers for Unit I.
    • Memory — Herbart; understanding — Morrison; reflective — Hunt. Reflective is the highest and most learner-centred.
    • Revised Bloom: remember → understand → apply → analyse → evaluate → create.
    • Andragogy — Knowles; adult learning is self-directed and problem-centred.
    • Formative/summative — Scriven; norm/criterion-referenced — Glaser.
    • SWAYAM's four quadrants: e-tutorial, e-content, self-assessment, discussion forum.
    • CBCS: ten-point scale, O = 10 to P = 4; SGPA is credit-weighted.
