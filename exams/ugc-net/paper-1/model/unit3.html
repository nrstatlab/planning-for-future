    Model Questions — Unit 3: Comprehension

    UGC NET Paper I (General Paper) · 50 questions · Click "Show Answer" for the worked solution

    About this set

    Ten passages, each followed by five questions of the kinds the paper sets: the main idea, a stated detail, an inference, the meaning of a word in its context, the author's attitude and the best title.

    These are model questions written for practice at the standard of the paper; they are not questions from a past paper. Try each one before opening the answer. Every answer gives the working and a link to the section of the Unit 3 notes that teaches it.

    The questions

    Passage 1 (Questions 1–5)

    A census counts everyone; a sample survey counts a few and reasons to the rest. It is tempting to think that the census must be the more accurate of the two, since it leaves no one out. In practice the opposite is often true. A census needs an army of temporary enumerators, hurriedly trained and thinly supervised, and the errors they make in asking and recording do not shrink as the count grows; they accumulate. A survey of a few thousand households can employ a small, well-trained staff, revisit doubtful cases and check its own work.

    The survey pays for this with sampling error, the error that arises because only a part was observed. But sampling error has a virtue that the other kind lacks: when the sample is drawn by chance, its size can be calculated from the sample itself. The careful surveyor therefore knows roughly how wrong the estimate may be. The census-taker, whose errors are of the unmeasured kind, often does not.

    4. enumerators should be better paid
    Show Answer

    Answer: (B) a well-conducted sample survey can be more accurate than a census, and its error can be assessed.

    The passage argues that non-sampling errors can make a census less accurate, while a survey can control them and also measure its sampling error. Option A overstates (‘often’, not ‘always’); C contradicts the passage; D is not discussed.

    Passage 2 (Questions 6–10)

    When a teacher asks a question and a hand goes up at once, the class learns something the teacher did not intend: that questions are answered by the quick. Studies of classrooms have found that teachers typically wait less than a second after asking before they call on someone or answer themselves. When the pause is lengthened to three seconds or more, the changes are striking. Answers become longer; more students offer them; students begin to respond to one another and not only to the teacher; and those who seldom spoke begin to speak.

    The pause costs nothing and needs no equipment, yet it is hard to keep. Silence in a classroom feels like failure, and a teacher's instinct is to fill it. What the longer wait asks of the teacher is not a new technique so much as a different belief: that thinking takes time, and that a question worth asking is worth waiting for.

    Passage 3 (Questions 11–15)

    Rivers were once described as the veins of a country, and the comparison is more exact than it sounds. A river is not a pipe that carries water from one place to another; it is a living system that carries silt, nutrients, fish and seeds, floods its plain in one season and shrinks in another, and in doing so builds the very land that people farm. When a river is straightened, walled and dammed so that it behaves like a pipe, it delivers water more reliably, but it stops doing the other things. The delta that received its silt begins to sink and the sea advances; the fish that travelled upstream to breed find a wall in their way.

    None of this is an argument against dams, which have fed and lit millions of homes. It is an argument against a habit of mind that counts only what a river is made to do and never what it had been doing all along, unasked and unpaid.

    Passage 4 (Questions 16–20)

    A translation is often judged by how little of itself it shows. The ideal, on this view, is a pane of glass: the reader should look through it at the original and forget that anyone stood between. The ideal is attractive and impossible. Languages do not divide the world in the same places. One has a single word where another needs a sentence; one marks respect in every verb, another cannot mark it at all. The translator must therefore choose, at every line, what to keep and what to let go, and a choice is a kind of authorship.

    This does not mean that anything goes. A translator is like a musician playing a written score: free in phrasing and bound in notes. The good ones know which is which. What it does mean is that readers should stop asking whether a translation is faithful, as though faithfulness were one thing, and ask what it has chosen to be faithful to.

    Passage 5 (Questions 21–25)

    An average is a useful servant and a poor master. To say that the average income of a village has risen is to say something true and to conceal something that may be more important: who gained. If one family's income rises tenfold and the rest stand still, the average rises, and a report that stops at the average will describe a village that no one lives in.

    The remedy is not to distrust averages but to ask for their companions. The median tells where the middle household stands and is unmoved by a fortune at the top. A measure of spread tells how far the households lie from one another. A table by groups tells whether the landless shared in the gain. Each of these costs a line in a report. Their absence should make a reader curious, for a figure that travels alone has usually left something behind.

    Passage 6 (Questions 26–30)

    The first printed books were made to look like manuscripts. Their letters copied the hands of scribes; spaces were left for initials to be painted in by hand; some were even printed on vellum. The printers were not timid. They were selling to buyers who knew what a book looked like, and a book looked like a manuscript. It took about two generations for print to discover what it could do that the pen could not: title pages, page numbers, indexes that sent every reader to the same place, identical copies that could be cited and corrected.

    Every new medium seems to begin this way, by imitating the one it will displace. Early films were plays photographed from a seat in the stalls; early web pages were brochures. The imitation is not a failure of imagination. It is how a new thing earns the trust that later allows it to become itself.

    Passage 7 (Questions 31–35)

    It is a commonplace that correlation is not causation, and like most commonplaces it is repeated more often than it is used. Towns with more police have more crime; countries that eat more chocolate win more scientific prizes. Few are deceived by examples like these, because the absurdity is on the surface. The danger lies in the cases where a causal story is ready to hand. Children who have breakfast do better at school: of course, we say, a fed child attends. But households that give breakfast differ from those that do not in income, in routine, in the time adults have; and any of these might do the work that we have credited to the meal.

    The cure is not to stop looking for causes, which would be to stop thinking. It is to ask, of every pleasing explanation, what else would have to be true if it were right, and then to go and look.

    Passage 8 (Questions 36–40)

    Antibiotics are unlike other medicines in one respect that is easy to state and hard to take in: every use of them makes them a little less useful to everyone. A tablet for blood pressure works as well on its millionth patient as on its first. An antibiotic, each time it is used, kills the susceptible bacteria and spares the few that happen to resist it, and it is those few that multiply. Used carelessly, for colds that it cannot cure or in courses abandoned half-way, it hastens the day when it will cure nothing.

    This makes the effectiveness of antibiotics a common resource, like a pasture or a fishing ground, and it shares their weakness. The benefit of each use goes to one patient; the cost is spread, thinly and invisibly, over all. No individual has a reason to hold back. Only rules made in common, and kept, can protect what no one owns and everyone needs.

    Passage 9 (Questions 41–45)

    When the question is asked why some regions grew rich in handicrafts and others did not, the answers given are usually about skill or enterprise. A humbler answer deserves a hearing: roads. A weaver's cloth is worth what it will fetch in the market he can reach. Where a cart could travel in all seasons, a village could specialise, sell what it made best and buy the rest; where the monsoon closed the track for four months, every household had to grow its own food and make its own goods, and did both badly.

    Specialisation, in other words, is not a habit of mind that some peoples have and others lack. It is a response to the size of the market, and the size of the market is set by the cost of carrying things. The historian who wishes to explain skill would do well to begin by looking at a map.

    Answer: (D) transport and access to markets.

    The ‘humbler answer’ is roads: the market a producer can reach, set by the cost of carrying goods, determines whether a village can specialise.

    Passage 10 (Questions 46–50)

    A university library used to be judged by the number of its volumes, and the habit of counting dies hard. Yet the reader who walks in today does not lack material; she is drowning in it. A search that once returned a shelf now returns a hundred thousand records, ordered by a rule she cannot see. What she needs is not more but less: a way of telling the article that has been checked from the one that merely looks like it, the data set that is documented from the one that is orphaned, the journal that reviews from the journal that only charges.

    The librarian who can teach this is doing something older than the catalogue and more durable than any database. Collections age, formats die, subscriptions lapse. The ability to judge a source, once learnt, goes with the reader for life, and it is the one holding of a library that cannot be cancelled when the budget is cut.
