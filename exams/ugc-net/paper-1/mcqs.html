Unit 1 — Teaching Aptitude (20 MCQs)

Levels and objectives of teaching, learners, methods, SWAYAM, support systems, evaluation, CBCS.

1. Who proposed the memory level of teaching?
  1. J. F. Herbart
  2. H. C. Morrison
  3. H. Hunt
  4. B. S. Bloom
Show Answer

A. The memory level, the lowest and most teacher-centred, is Herbart's; the understanding level is Morrison's and the reflective level Hunt's.

2. The reflective level of teaching is associated with:
  1. Herbart
  2. Morrison
  3. Hunt
  4. Skinner
Show Answer

C. Hunt's reflective level is the highest: learner-centred, built on problem-solving and critical thinking.

3. In the revised taxonomy of Anderson and Krathwohl (2001), the highest level of the cognitive domain is:
  1. Evaluate
  2. Synthesis
  3. Analyse
  4. Create
Show Answer

D. The revision renamed the levels as verbs and put create (once synthesis) above evaluate. Synthesis is the old name, not a level of the revision.

4. "Valuing" is a level of which domain of educational objectives?
  1. Affective
  2. Cognitive
  3. Psychomotor
  4. Conative
Show Answer

A. The affective domain (Krathwohl, Bloom and Masia, 1964) runs receiving, responding, valuing, organisation, characterisation.

5. The phase of teaching in which the teacher decides objectives, content and method is the:
  1. Interactive phase
  2. Post-active phase
  3. Pre-active phase
  4. Evaluative phase
Show Answer

C. Planning comes before the class: the pre-active phase. The interactive phase is the class itself; the post-active phase is evaluation afterwards.

6. A teacher starts with the scores of a real cricket match before defining the arithmetic mean. Which maxim is being followed?
  1. Abstract to concrete
  2. Concrete to abstract
  3. General to particular
  4. Logical to psychological
Show Answer

B. Real scores are concrete; the definition is abstract. The maxim is concrete to abstract (and particular to general, not general to particular).

7. Andragogy, the art and science of helping adults learn, was popularised by:
  1. Malcolm Knowles
  2. Jean Piaget
  3. Erik Erikson
  4. Edgar Dale
Show Answer

A. Knowles set andragogy against pedagogy. Piaget described cognitive stages, Erikson psychosocial stages, Dale the cone of experience.

8. In Piaget's theory, abstract and hypothetical reasoning develops from about the age of eleven, in the:
  1. Sensorimotor stage
  2. Pre-operational stage
  3. Concrete operational stage
  4. Formal operational stage
Show Answer

D. Formal operations begin around eleven or twelve, which is why adolescents can handle abstract ideas that younger children cannot.

9. Which is NOT one of Knowles' assumptions about adult learners?
  1. They are self-directed
  2. Their orientation is problem-centred
  3. Their motivation is mainly external
  4. Their experience is a resource for learning
Show Answer

C. Knowles held that adults' motivation is mainly internal (self-esteem, satisfaction, quality of life). The other three are among his assumptions.

10. A class of 150 students taught together in one hall is a factor affecting teaching related mainly to:
  1. The learner
  2. Support material
  3. The teacher's personality
  4. The institution
Show Answer

D. Class size is set by the institution's policies and resources, so it is an institutional factor.

11. Which of these is a learner-centred method?
  1. Lecture
  2. Demonstration
  3. Project method
  4. Recitation
Show Answer

C. In the project method learners plan and carry out a purposeful task; the teacher guides. Lecture, demonstration and recitation are teacher-centred.

12. The project method of teaching was developed by:
  1. W. H. Kilpatrick
  2. H. E. Armstrong
  3. Helen Parkhurst
  4. B. F. Skinner
Show Answer

A. Kilpatrick set it out in 1918, drawing on Dewey. Armstrong is known for the heuristic method, Parkhurst for the Dalton plan, Skinner for programmed instruction.

13. Programmed instruction, in small steps with immediate feedback, is associated with:
  1. Fred Keller
  2. B. F. Skinner
  3. Alex Osborn
  4. Ned Flanders
Show Answer

B. Skinner's linear programming grew from his work on reinforcement. Keller devised the Personalised System of Instruction, Osborn brainstorming, Flanders interaction analysis.

14. In a flipped classroom:
  1. Learners meet the content before class and use class time for problems and discussion
  2. The teacher lectures in class and sets exercises as homework
  3. All teaching is online and there are no classes
  4. There is no assessment
Show Answer

A. The usual order is flipped: first contact with the content is at home (video, reading), and class time is for active work.

15. SWAYAM stands for:
  1. System for Web-based Advanced Youth Academic Modules
  2. Study Without Age Yet Aiming at Mastery
  3. Study Webs of Active-Learning for Young Aspiring Minds
  4. Scheme for Web Access for Young Academic Minds
Show Answer

C. SWAYAM, launched in 2017, is the Government of India's MOOC platform: Study Webs of Active-Learning for Young Aspiring Minds.

16. Which is NOT one of the four quadrants of a SWAYAM course?
  1. E-tutorial
  2. E-content
  3. Discussion forum
  4. Classroom attendance
Show Answer

D. The four quadrants are e-tutorial, e-content, self-assessment and a discussion forum. Attendance in a classroom is not part of a MOOC.

17. In Edgar Dale's Cone of Experience, the most abstract experiences, at the top of the cone, are:
  1. Direct, purposeful experiences
  2. Verbal symbols
  3. Demonstrations
  4. Field trips
Show Answer

B. Direct, purposeful experiences form the base; verbal symbols, which depend on words alone, sit at the top.

18. Evaluation carried out during teaching, to give feedback that improves learning while it is going on, is:
  1. Summative
  2. Formative
  3. Placement
  4. Norm-referenced
Show Answer

B. Formative evaluation happens during teaching and shapes it; summative evaluation judges at the end. Scriven introduced the two terms in 1967.

19. A test that compares each candidate's performance with a fixed standard of mastery, not with other candidates, is:
  1. Norm-referenced
  2. Diagnostic
  3. Placement
  4. Criterion-referenced
Show Answer

D. Criterion-referenced tests measure against a standard; norm-referenced tests rank candidates against one another (Glaser, 1963).

20. Under CBCS a student earns grade points 8, 6 and 10 in courses of 4, 3 and 3 credits. The SGPA is:
  1. 8.0
  2. 7.9
  3. 8.2
  4. 8.4
Show Answer

A. SGPA = (4×8 + 3×6 + 3×10) / (4 + 3 + 3) = (32 + 18 + 30) / 10 = 8.0. Here the simple mean of the grade points is also 8.0, but only by chance: always weight by credits.

Unit 2 — Research Aptitude (20 MCQs)

Types and methods of research, positivism, steps, sampling, referencing, ICT, ethics.

1. Action research, in which practitioners study and improve their own practice, was introduced by:
  1. Kurt Lewin
  2. Karl Popper
  3. Auguste Comte
  4. Max Weber
Show Answer

A. Lewin introduced action research in the 1940s; Stephen Corey took it into education. Popper is known for falsification, Comte for positivism, Weber for Verstehen.

2. Research carried out to solve an immediate practical problem is called:
  1. Fundamental research
  2. Applied research
  3. Conceptual research
  4. Historical research
Show Answer

B. Applied research aims at a practical solution; fundamental (pure) research aims to extend knowledge or theory.

3. Positivism as a philosophy of knowledge is associated with:
  1. Karl Popper
  2. Max Weber
  3. Barney Glaser
  4. Auguste Comte
Show Answer

D. Comte founded positivism. Popper's falsification underlies post-positivism; Weber's Verstehen, interpretivism; Glaser, grounded theory.

4. The principle that a scientific statement must be capable of being shown false is:
  1. Verification
  2. Falsification
  3. Triangulation
  4. Replication
Show Answer

B. Popper's falsification: one black swan refutes "all swans are white", but no number of white swans proves it.

5. Which method of research can directly establish a cause-and-effect relationship?
  1. Survey
  2. Case study
  3. Experimental
  4. Historical
Show Answer

C. Only the experimental method manipulates the presumed cause while controlling other variables. Surveys and case studies can show association, not cause.

6. A design with an experimental and a control group, but without random assignment to them, is:
  1. True experimental
  2. Pre-experimental
  3. Ex post facto
  4. Quasi-experimental
Show Answer

D. Random assignment is what makes a design truly experimental. A control group without it is quasi-experimental; one group without a control is pre-experimental.

7. In historical research, the process of checking whether a document is genuine is called:
  1. Internal criticism
  2. External criticism
  3. Content analysis
  4. Triangulation
Show Answer

B. External criticism tests authenticity (is it what it claims to be?); internal criticism tests the credibility of its content.

8. Grounded theory, which builds theory from the data rather than testing it, was developed by:
  1. Campbell and Stanley
  2. Lazarsfeld and Katz
  3. Glaser and Strauss
  4. McCombs and Shaw
Show Answer

C. Barney Glaser and Anselm Strauss, The Discovery of Grounded Theory (1967). Campbell and Stanley classified experimental designs.

9. A researcher studying a hidden population with no list of its members asks each participant to name others. This is:
  1. Systematic sampling
  2. Stratified sampling
  3. Snowball sampling
  4. Cluster sampling
Show Answer

C. Snowball sampling grows through referrals and suits hidden populations. The other three are probability methods that need a list or frame.

10. Which of the following is a probability sampling method?
  1. Quota sampling
  2. Purposive sampling
  3. Convenience sampling
  4. Stratified random sampling
Show Answer

D. In stratified random sampling every unit has a known chance of selection. Quota, purposive and convenience sampling are non-probability methods.

11. Arrange in the usual order: (i) review of literature (ii) data collection (iii) identifying the problem (iv) formulating hypotheses.
  1. iii, i, iv, ii
  2. i, iii, iv, ii
  3. iii, iv, i, ii
  4. iv, iii, i, ii
Show Answer

A. The problem comes first; the literature review shows what is known; hypotheses follow from both; data are collected to test them.

12. In a study of the effect of a teaching method on achievement, achievement is the:
  1. Independent variable
  2. Dependent variable
  3. Extraneous variable
  4. Intervening variable
Show Answer

B. The teaching method is the presumed cause (independent); achievement is the outcome measured (dependent).

13. A hypothesis stating that there is no difference between two groups is the:
  1. Alternative hypothesis
  2. Directional hypothesis
  3. Null hypothesis
  4. Research hypothesis
Show Answer

C. The null hypothesis states no difference or no relation; it is the one tested statistically.

14. The referencing style that cites author and page in the text, as in (Rao 45), is:
  1. MLA
  2. APA
  3. Vancouver
  4. IEEE
Show Answer

A. MLA uses author–page. APA uses author–date (Rao, 2019); Vancouver and IEEE number their references.

15. In footnotes, "ibid." means:
  1. The same source as the previous note
  2. The work already cited, at another place
  3. Compare
  4. And others
Show Answer

A. Ibid. (ibidem, in the same place) refers to the source cited immediately before. Op. cit. is the work already cited; cf. is compare; et al. is and others.

16. A researcher's six papers are cited 12, 10, 7, 5, 3 and 1 times. The researcher's h-index is:
  1. 3
  2. 4
  3. 5
  4. 6
Show Answer

B. Four papers have at least 4 citations (12, 10, 7, 5), but there are not five papers with at least 5 (the fifth has 3). So h = 4.

17. Shodhganga, maintained by INFLIBNET, is:
  1. Plagiarism-detection software
  2. A MOOC platform
  3. A list of approved journals
  4. A repository of Indian PhD theses
Show Answer

D. Shodhganga holds theses submitted to Indian universities. Plagiarism-detection software is provided through Shodh Shuddhi; SWAYAM is the MOOC platform.

18. Under the UGC plagiarism regulations of 2018, similarity of up to 10% is:
  1. Level 0
  2. Level 1
  3. Level 2
  4. Level 3
Show Answer

A. Level 0, up to 10%, is minor similarity and carries no penalty. Level 1 is above 10% to 40%, Level 2 above 40% to 60%, Level 3 above 60%.

19. In research misconduct, fabrication means:
  1. Copying another's work without credit
  2. Changing data to fit the hypothesis
  3. Publishing the same work twice
  4. Making up data or results
Show Answer

D. Fabrication is inventing data; falsification is changing them; plagiarism is copying without credit. Together they are the three serious forms of misconduct.

20. The three ethical principles of the Belmont Report (1979) are:
  1. Consent, confidentiality and anonymity
  2. Validity, reliability and objectivity
  3. Respect for persons, beneficence and justice
  4. Honesty, openness and accountability
Show Answer

C. The Belmont Report's principles are respect for persons, beneficence and justice; informed consent is how respect for persons is applied.

Passage 1 (Questions 1–5)

For most of the twentieth century, India's farms drew their water from canals and tanks; today the larger share of irrigated land is watered from wells. The change brought great gains. A farmer with a tube well need not wait for a canal's turn; she can water when the crop needs it, and yields have risen accordingly. But groundwater is a bank account that is refilled only by the rain that soaks into the soil, and in many districts more is withdrawn each year than the monsoon returns. Water tables have fallen so far in places that wells must be deepened every few years, at a cost the poorest farmers cannot meet.

The difficulty is that no single farmer gains by holding back: water she leaves in the ground may simply be pumped by a neighbour. Free or cheap electricity for pumps, introduced to help farmers, has made the problem worse by removing the one cost that might have encouraged restraint. Some villages have shown another way. By agreeing among themselves which crops to grow and how much to pump, and by building small structures that slow run-off and let rain soak in, they have stabilised their water tables. Their experience suggests that the answer lies less in new technology than in new rules, made by those who share the resource.

  1. How tube wells have raised crop yields in India
  2. The gains of groundwater irrigation, its overuse, and how shared rules may help
  3. Why free electricity for farmers should be abolished
  4. Why canals are better than wells
Show Answer

B. The first paragraph gives the gains and the overuse; the second, why it happens and what some villages did. (A) is one detail; (C) goes beyond the passage, which says cheap power worsened the problem but proposes no abolition; (D) is never argued.

2. According to the passage, why does no single farmer gain by pumping less?
  1. The water she leaves may be pumped by a neighbour
  2. Her pump costs too much to run
  3. The monsoon refills her well every year
  4. Canals supply her instead

A. The passage says so directly: "water she leaves in the ground may simply be pumped by a neighbour". (C) contradicts it: more is withdrawn than the monsoon returns.

3. In the passage, "stabilised" most nearly means:
  1. Raised sharply
  2. Measured accurately
  3. Stopped from falling further
  4. Sold to neighbours
Show Answer

C. The villages stopped the decline of their water tables. Nothing says the tables rose sharply, so (A) claims too much.

4. The author would most likely agree that:
  1. New pumping technology will solve the problem
  2. Groundwater can never be replenished
  3. Poor farmers pump the most water
  4. Cooperation among those who share a resource can protect it
Show Answer

D. The last sentence: the answer lies "in new rules, made by those who share the resource". (A) is what the author plays down; (B) contradicts "refilled only by the rain".

5. The comparison of groundwater to "a bank account" is used to:

  2. Show that farmers have become wealthy
  3. Suggest that banks should pay for wells
  4. Describe how canals were run

A. Rain is the deposit, pumping the withdrawal; when withdrawals exceed deposits, the balance (the water table) falls.

Passage 2 (Questions 6–10)

Before a scientific paper is published in a reputable journal, it is usually sent to two or three experts in the field who are not its authors. These reviewers judge whether the question is worth asking, whether the methods can answer it, and whether the conclusions follow from the results. They may recommend that the paper be accepted, revised or rejected. The system, called peer review, is often described as the gatekeeper of science.

Gatekeeper is too strong a word. Reviewers rarely see the raw data and almost never repeat the experiment; they judge the paper as it is written. They can miss honest errors, and a determined fraud may pass them altogether. Their judgement can also be coloured by the authors' reputation, which is why many journals now hide the authors' names from reviewers. None of this means that peer review is worthless: it catches many weak arguments and improves most of the papers that pass through it. But a reader should treat publication as a sign that a paper has been checked for sense, not as a guarantee that its findings are true. That guarantee, as far as science offers one, comes only when other researchers, working independently, find the same result.

  1. How to write a scientific paper
  2. The role and the limits of peer review
  3. Fraud in science
  4. Why journals hide authors' names
Show Answer

B. It describes what peer review does and then what it cannot do. Fraud and hidden names are single points within that.

7. According to the passage, reviewers usually:
  1. Repeat the experiment
  2. Examine the raw data
  3. Judge the paper as it is written
  4. Decide whether the authors are promoted
Show Answer

C. "Reviewers rarely see the raw data and almost never repeat the experiment; they judge the paper as it is written."

8. Why do many journals now hide the authors' names from reviewers?
  1. Because reviewers' judgement can be coloured by the authors' reputation
  2. To save space in the journal
  3. To prevent fraud altogether
  4. Because authors ask them to

A. The passage links the practice to reputation. It does not claim that hiding names prevents fraud; it says fraud may pass review.

9. It can be inferred that, in the author's view, the strongest evidence that a finding is true is:

  4. The reputation of the authors

B. "That guarantee … comes only when other researchers, working independently, find the same result." Publication is only "a sign that a paper has been checked for sense".

10. The author regards the phrase "gatekeeper of science" as:

  3. Irrelevant
  4. An overstatement

D. "Gatekeeper is too strong a word." The author still values peer review, so (B) is wrong.

The research suggests that their aim and the mother-tongue approach need not conflict: a strong start in the home language, with the second language introduced gradually and taught well, may be the surest route to the very fluency parents want. The difficulty is practical. It needs teachers and books in many languages, which poorer school systems find hard to provide.

Passage 4 (Questions 16–20)

Every winter, thousands of volunteers in many countries spend a few hours counting the birds they see and send their tallies to a central database. No single count is precise: volunteers differ in skill, some days are cloudy, and a rare bird may be recorded twice or missed altogether. Yet taken together, over decades, such counts have revealed changes that no team of professionals could have measured alone, such as the slow northward shift of many species' winter ranges.

The value of such citizen science depends on its design. Volunteers must follow the same simple method, count for a fixed time in a fixed place, and record not only what they saw but how long they looked. With these details, statisticians can correct for differences in effort and skill. Without them, a rise in the number of birds recorded might mean only a rise in the number of people looking. The lesson reaches beyond birds: large amounts of imperfect data can answer questions that small amounts of perfect data cannot, provided the way the data were gathered is known.

  2. Why Volunteers Make Mistakes

  4. Careers in Ornithology

A. The passage is about how many imprecise counts, gathered by a known method, become valuable. (B) and (C) are single points.

  4. Statisticians can correct for differences in effort

D. "With these details, statisticians can correct for differences in effort and skill." Recording time does not make a single count precise.

  1. That birds are increasing
  2. Why the effort behind each count must be recorded

B. Without a record of effort, more observers would look like more birds. The passage does not say the counts are useless.

  1. Dismissive

C. The author admits each count's imprecision and praises what the counts reveal together.

Unit 4 — Communication (20 MCQs)

Models, types, non-verbal cues, culture and groups, classroom talk, barriers, mass media.

1. "Who says what, in which channel, to whom, with what effect?" is the model of communication given by:
  1. Shannon and Weaver
  2. David Berlo
  3. Harold Lasswell
  4. Wilbur Schramm
Show Answer

C. Lasswell's verbal model (1948) names the five questions.

2. The concept of "noise" in a model of communication was introduced by:
  1. Aristotle
  2. Harold Lasswell
  3. Frank Dance
  4. Shannon and Weaver
Show Answer

D. Shannon and Weaver's mathematical model (1948–49) added noise, anything that distorts the signal between transmitter and receiver.

3. In Berlo's SMCR model, the letters stand for:
  1. Source, Message, Channel, Receiver
  2. Sender, Medium, Code, Response
  3. Signal, Message, Context, Reply
  4. Source, Meaning, Communication, Result
Show Answer

A. Berlo (1960): Source, Message, Channel, Receiver, each with its own elements (skills, attitudes, knowledge, culture).

4. Schramm's model of communication emphasises:
  1. The overlap of the sender's and receiver's fields of experience
  2. A one-way flow from sender to receiver
  3. The capacity of the channel
  4. Communication growing like a helix
Show Answer

A. Schramm (1954): communication works where the fields of experience overlap; his model is circular and interactive. The helix is Dance's.

5. A report sent by a subordinate to a superior is an example of:
  1. Downward communication
  2. Horizontal communication
  3. Diagonal communication
  4. Upward communication
Show Answer

D. Upward communication runs from subordinate to superior; downward, the other way; horizontal, between equals.

6. The grapevine in an organisation is an example of:
  1. Formal communication
  2. Mass communication
  3. Informal communication
  4. Intrapersonal communication
Show Answer

C. The grapevine runs outside official channels: informal communication.

7. The study of the use of space and distance in communication is called:
  1. Kinesics
  2. Haptics
  3. Proxemics
  4. Chronemics
Show Answer

C. Proxemics (Edward T. Hall). Kinesics is body movement, haptics touch, chronemics time.

8. Chronemics is the study of the use of:
  1. Touch
  2. Eye contact
  3. Voice
  4. Time
Show Answer

D. Chronemics covers punctuality, waiting and pauses: how time communicates.

9. Paralanguage refers to:
  1. How words are said: pitch, tone, volume and pace
  2. Sign language
  3. Body posture
  4. Written symbols
Show Answer

A. Paralanguage (vocalics) is the vocal part of speech other than the words themselves.

10. In Edward T. Hall's proxemics, the social distance zone is about:
  1. Up to 45 cm
  2. 45 cm to 1.2 m
  3. 1.2 m to 3.6 m
  4. Beyond 3.6 m

C. Intimate up to about 45 cm, personal about 45 cm to 1.2 m, social about 1.2 to 3.6 m, public beyond about 3.6 m.

  1. All communication
  2. Written communication
  3. Communication of feelings and attitudes when words and tone disagree
  4. Mass media

C. The finding concerned messages about feelings and attitudes with inconsistent cues. Applying it to all communication is a common misquotation.

12. The distinction between high-context and low-context cultures was made by:
  1. Geert Hofstede
  2. Bruce Tuckman
  3. Irving Janis
  4. Edward T. Hall

  4. Neither I nor II follows

B. I does not follow: the graduates who are teachers may all be outside the doctors. II follows: "Some graduates are teachers" converts to "Some teachers are graduates".

  4. Neither I nor II follows
Show Answer

A. Every mango is a fruit and no mango is an apple, so the mangoes are fruits that are not apples: I follows. II does not: there may be fruits outside the mangoes that are apples.

Conclusions: I. All pens are erasers. II. Some erasers are pens.
  1. Only I follows
  2. Only II follows
  3. Both I and II follow
  4. Neither I nor II follows
Show Answer

C. I follows (Barbara: AAA in the first figure). II follows from I by conversion: if all pens are erasers and there are pens, some erasers are pens. Both follow.

Conclusions: I. Some cats are rats. II. No cat is a rat.
  1. Only I follows
  2. Only II follows
  3. Both I and II follow
  4. Neither I nor II follows
Show Answer

D. From two particular premises no conclusion follows. The dogs that are cats and the dogs that are rats may or may not overlap, so neither I nor II must be true.

  1. Only I follows
  2. Only II follows
  3. Both I and II follow
  4. Neither I nor II follows
Show Answer

A. The pens that are boxes cannot be books, so some boxes are not books: I follows. Nothing is said of books and boxes otherwise, so II does not follow.

Show Answer

C. It affirms the consequent ("it is a fruit") and concludes the antecedent. That form is invalid: a banana is a fruit too.

  1. True
  2. False
  3. Undetermined
  4. Both true and false

C. A false makes its contradictory O true, but tells us nothing about I, which can still be true or false. Only from A true does I follow.

C. A web seminar: speakers present live, and the audience usually listens and asks questions by chat.

  4. IGNOU

C. NDLI is developed by IIT Kharagpur under the Ministry of Education. INFLIBNET runs Shodhganga, e-ShodhSindhu and e-PG Pathshala.

  1. E-content for postgraduate subjects

  3. Plagiarism-detection software
  4. Digital degree certificates
Show Answer

A. It is the UGC's e-content programme for postgraduate subjects, run through INFLIBNET. Degree certificates are in DigiLocker through NAD.

Unit 9 — People, Development and Environment (20 MCQs)

MDGs and SDGs, pollution and waste, pollutants and health, laws, the NAPCC, international agreements, disasters.

1. How many Sustainable Development Goals are there?
  1. 8
  2. 15
  3. 17
  4. 169
Show Answer

C. 17 goals (2015–2030), with 169 targets among them. 8 is the number of the earlier Millennium Development Goals.

2. The 17 Sustainable Development Goals contain how many targets?
  1. 17
  2. 100
  3. 230
  4. 169
Show Answer

D. 169 targets, set out in the 2030 Agenda adopted in September 2015.

3. The Millennium Development Goals covered the period:
  1. 1990–2000
  2. 2000–2015
  3. 2015–2030
  4. 1992–2012
Show Answer

B. The eight MDGs came from the Millennium Declaration of 2000, with 2015 as the target year; the SDGs took over for 2015–2030.

4. SDG 4 is:
  1. Zero hunger
  2. Quality education
  3. Gender equality
  4. Climate action
Show Answer

B. SDG 4 is quality education. Zero hunger is SDG 2, gender equality SDG 5, climate action SDG 13.

5. A high biochemical oxygen demand (BOD) in a river indicates:
  1. Heavy organic pollution
  2. Clean, well-oxygenated water
  3. High salinity
  4. A high fish population
Show Answer

A. BOD measures the oxygen micro-organisms need to break down organic matter; sewage and other organic waste raise it and leave less oxygen for fish.

6. Minamata disease is caused by poisoning with:
  1. Lead
  2. Cadmium
  3. Mercury
  4. Arsenic
Show Answer

C. Mercury, from industrial discharge into Minamata Bay in Japan in the 1950s. Cadmium causes itai-itai disease.

7. Itai-itai disease is caused by:
  1. Mercury
  2. Cadmium
  3. Lead
  4. Fluoride
Show Answer

B. Cadmium poisoning, first recorded in Japan, softens the bones and damages the kidneys.

8. "Blue baby syndrome" (methaemoglobinaemia) in infants is linked to drinking water with excess:
  1. Nitrate
  2. Fluoride
  3. Arsenic
  4. Lead
Show Answer

A. Nitrate, often from fertiliser run-off, reduces the blood's ability to carry oxygen in infants. Excess fluoride causes fluorosis.

9. Noise is measured in:
  1. Decibels
  2. Hertz
  3. Pascals
  4. Lux
Show Answer

A. Decibels (dB), a logarithmic scale of sound intensity. Hertz measures frequency (pitch); lux measures light.

10. India's E-Waste (Management) Rules now in force were notified in:
  1. 2011
  2. 2016
  3. 2022
  4. 2008
Show Answer

C. The E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022, based on extended producer responsibility. The solid, plastic, biomedical and hazardous waste rules date from 2016.

11. The gas that leaked in the Bhopal disaster of 1984 was:
  1. Methyl isocyanate
  2. Chlorine
  3. Sulphur dioxide
  4. Ammonia
Show Answer

A. Methyl isocyanate leaked from a pesticide plant in Bhopal in December 1984.

12. The Environment (Protection) Act was enacted in:
  1. 1972
  2. 1974
  3. 1981
  4. 1986
Show Answer

D. 1986, after the Bhopal disaster. 1972 is the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1974 the Water Act, 1981 the Air Act.

13. How many national missions does India's National Action Plan on Climate Change (2008) have?
  1. 6
  2. 7
  3. 8
  4. 10
Show Answer

C. Eight: solar, enhanced energy efficiency, sustainable habitat, water, the Himalayan ecosystem, green India, sustainable agriculture, and strategic knowledge for climate change.

14. Which of these is NOT one of the missions of the NAPCC?
  1. National Solar Mission
  2. National Water Mission
  3. National Mission for a Green India
  4. National Nuclear Energy Mission
Show Answer

D. There is no nuclear energy mission among the eight. Solar, water and green India are three of them.

15. The Montreal Protocol (1987) deals with:
  1. Substances that deplete the ozone layer
  2. Greenhouse gas emission targets
  3. Biological diversity
  4. Trade in endangered species
Show Answer

A. It phases out ozone-depleting substances such as CFCs. Greenhouse-gas targets are the business of the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement.

16. The Kyoto Protocol was adopted in:
  1. 1992
  2. 1997
  3. 2005
  4. 2015
Show Answer

B. Adopted in 1997, it came into force in 2005. 1992 is the Rio Summit; 2015 the Paris Agreement.

17. The Paris Agreement (2015) aims to hold the rise in global average temperature:
  1. Below 4°C
  2. At exactly 1°C
  3. Below 3°C, with no lower aim
  4. Well below 2°C, pursuing efforts to limit it to 1.5°C
Show Answer

D. Well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels, and pursuing 1.5°C; each country sets its own Nationally Determined Contribution.

18. The International Solar Alliance, launched by India and France in 2015, has its headquarters in:
  1. Paris
  2. Geneva
  3. Gurugram
  4. Bonn
Show Answer

C. Gurugram, in Haryana, India. It was launched at the Paris climate conference in 2015.

19. The National Disaster Management Authority, set up under the Disaster Management Act, 2005, is chaired by:
  1. The Home Minister
  2. The Prime Minister
  3. The President
  4. The Cabinet Secretary
Show Answer

B. The Prime Minister chairs the NDMA; the Chief Ministers chair the state authorities.

20. The Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction covers the period:
  1. 2005–2015
  2. 2000–2015
  3. 2020–2035
  4. 2015–2030
Show Answer

D. 2015–2030. It followed the Hyogo Framework for Action, 2005–2015.

Unit 10 — Higher Education System (20 MCQs)

Ancient centres, commissions and policies, regulators, kinds of programme, skills, value education, governance.

1. Nalanda was founded in the 5th century CE under the king:
  1. Dharmapala
  2. Kumaragupta I
  3. Ashoka
  4. Harsha
Show Answer

B. Kumaragupta I of the Gupta dynasty. Harsha later patronised it; Dharmapala founded Vikramashila.

2. Vikramashila was founded by:
  1. Dharmapala
  2. Gopala
  3. Kumaragupta I
  4. Kanishka
Show Answer

A. The Pala king Dharmapala, in the late 8th century. Gopala founded Odantapuri.

  1. Bihar
  2. Gujarat
  3. Pakistan
  4. Afghanistan

C. Takshashila (Taxila) is near Rawalpindi, Pakistan. Nalanda, Vikramashila and Odantapuri are in Bihar; Valabhi in Gujarat.

  1. D. S. Kothari
  2. A. L. Mudaliar
  3. S. Radhakrishnan
  4. K. Kasturirangan
Show Answer

C. S. Radhakrishnan. Kothari chaired the Education Commission of 1964–66, Mudaliar the Secondary Education Commission, and Kasturirangan the committee that drafted NEP 2020.

5. The Education Commission chaired by D. S. Kothari sat in:
  1. 1948–49
  2. 1952–53
  3. 1986–92
  4. 1964–66
Show Answer

D. 1964–66; its report, Education and National Development, led to the first National Policy on Education (1968).

6. The University Grants Commission became a statutory body by an Act of:
  1. 1953
  2. 1956
  3. 1964
  4. 1986
Show Answer

B. The UGC Act, 1956. The Commission had been inaugurated in 1953.

7. NEP 2020 aims to raise the gross enrolment ratio in higher education to:
  1. 50% by 2035
  2. 100% by 2030
  3. 35% by 2025
  4. 60% by 2040
Show Answer

A. NEP 2020 sets a gross enrolment ratio of 50% in higher education, vocational education included, by 2035.

8. The National Assessment and Accreditation Council (NAAC) was established in:
  1. 1986
  2. 1994
  3. 2000
  4. 2015
Show Answer

B. 1994, by the UGC, following the National Policy on Education of 1986. 2015 is the year NIRF was launched.

9. Indira Gandhi National Open University (IGNOU) was established in:
  1. 1962
  2. 1982
  3. 1985
  4. 1992
Show Answer

C. 1985. 1962 is when Delhi University began correspondence courses, and 1982 when the first state open university was founded, in Andhra Pradesh.

10. Technical education in India is regulated by:
  1. The UGC
  2. The NCTE
  3. The NAAC
  4. The AICTE
Show Answer

D. The All India Council for Technical Education. NCTE regulates teacher education; NAAC accredits; the UGC sets university standards and gives grants.

11. The National Institutional Ranking Framework (NIRF):
  1. Ranks higher education institutions each year
  2. Accredits colleges with grades
  3. Funds state universities
  4. Awards degrees
Show Answer

A. NIRF ranks institutions annually. Accreditation with grades is NAAC's work; funding of state institutions is RUSA's.

12. Education was moved from the State List to the Concurrent List by:
  1. The 42nd Amendment, 1976
  2. The 44th Amendment, 1978
  3. The 73rd Amendment, 1992
  4. The 86th Amendment, 2002
Show Answer

A. The 42nd Amendment of 1976. The 86th Amendment of 2002 made elementary education a fundamental right.

13. In a university, the body responsible for courses, examinations and academic standards is the:
  1. Executive Council
  2. Court
  3. Academic Council
  4. Finance Committee
Show Answer

C. The Academic Council. The Executive Council (or Syndicate) handles administration and finance.

14. An institution is declared a deemed-to-be university under:
  1. Section 3 of the UGC Act
  2. Article 30 of the Constitution
  3. Entry 66 of the Union List
  4. Section 12B of the UGC Act
Show Answer

A. Section 3 of the UGC Act, 1956. Section 12B concerns eligibility for central grants; Article 30, the rights of minority institutions.

15. Which of these is an example of oriental learning?

  3. A diploma at a polytechnic
  4. An Acharya degree at a Sanskrit university

D. Oriental learning keeps the classical traditions: Sanskrit, Pali, Persian, Arabic. The others are technical, management and distance programmes.

  3. A regular Ph.D. on campus
  4. A full-time LL.B.

B. Open and distance programmes break the conditions of conventional, full-time, on-campus study.

  1. Ranks universities
  2. Funds research
  3. Replaces the UGC
  4. Arranges qualifications in levels by competence
Show Answer

D. It organises qualifications in levels of knowledge, skill and aptitude, so that skills can be recognised, compared and built on.

18. The National Medical Commission replaced:
  1. AICTE
  2. NCTE
  3. The Dental Council of India
  4. The Medical Council of India
Show Answer

D. The National Medical Commission replaced the Medical Council of India in 2020.

19. The National Policy on Education of 1986 was revised in:
  1. 1990
  2. 1992
  3. 1998
  4. 2005
Show Answer

B. 1992, with a Programme of Action.

20. Environmental studies became a compulsory part of education following the Supreme Court's directions in:
