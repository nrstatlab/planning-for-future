    Practical 1 — Inverse by the Partition Method

    1. Problem

    Find the inverse of \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) by the partition method.

    2. Aim

    To invert a matrix by partitioning it into blocks and inverting the smaller pieces.

    3. Formula

    \[ A = \begin{pmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} \end{pmatrix}, \quad S = A_{22} - A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}, \] \[ B_{22} = S^{-1}, \quad B_{12} = -A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}S^{-1}, \quad B_{21} = -S^{-1}A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}, \quad B_{11} = A_{11}^{-1} + A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}S^{-1}A_{21}A_{11}^{-1} \]

    \(S\) is the Schur complement of \(A_{11}\).

    Applying it:

    1. Partition, taking \(A_{11}\) as the leading \(2 \times 2\).
    2. Invert \(A_{11}\).
    3. Form the Schur complement and its inverse.
    4. The off-diagonal blocks, then the leading block.
    5. Assemble and check \(AA^{-1} = I\).

    4. Calculation

    \[ A_{11} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A_{12} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A_{21} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A_{22} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \end{pmatrix}; \qquad A_{11}^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. \] \[ A_{11}^{-1}A_{12} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12} = \frac23, \quad S = 2 - \frac23 = \frac43, \quad S^{-1} = \frac34. \] \[ B_{12} = -\frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\times\frac34 = \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac14 \\ -\tfrac14 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B_{21} = \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac14 & -\tfrac14 \end{pmatrix} \ \text{(by symmetry)}, \] \[ B_{11} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} + \frac{1}{12}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac34 & -\tfrac14 \\ -\tfrac14 & \tfrac34 \end{pmatrix}, \]

    since \(\tfrac23 + \tfrac1{12} = \tfrac34\) and \(-\tfrac13 + \tfrac1{12} = -\tfrac14\).

    Practical 2 — Solution of Linear Equations by the Sweep-Out Method

    1. Problem

    Solve \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) by the sweep-out (Gauss–Jordan) method, where \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{b} = (7, 8, 9)'\).

    2. Aim

    To solve a system of linear equations by sweeping the augmented matrix to the identity.

    3. Formula

    \[ \left(A \mid \mathbf{b}\right) \;\longrightarrow\; \left(I \mid \mathbf{x}\right) \]

    At each step, divide the pivot row by the pivot, then subtract multiples of it from every other row so that its column becomes a unit vector. Augmenting with \(I\) instead of \(\mathbf{b}\) produces \(A^{-1}\) with identical work.

    Applying it:

    1. First pivot: \(R_1 \div 2\); \(R_2 - R_1\), \(R_3 - R_1\).
    2. Second pivot: \(R_2 \div \tfrac32\); \(R_1 - \tfrac12R_2\), \(R_3 - \tfrac12R_2\).
    3. Third pivot: \(R_3 \div \tfrac43\); \(R_1 - \tfrac13R_3\), \(R_2 - \tfrac13R_3\).
    4. Read \(\mathbf{x}\) and substitute back.

    4. Calculation

    \[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 2 & 1 & 1 & 7 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 & 8 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 & 9 \end{array}\right) \to \left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 & \tfrac72 \\ 0 & \tfrac32 & \tfrac12 & \tfrac92 \\ 0 & \tfrac12 & \tfrac32 & \tfrac{11}{2} \end{array}\right) \to \left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 0 & \tfrac13 & \tfrac53 \\ 0 & 1 & \tfrac13 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac43 & 4 \end{array}\right) \to \left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 3 \end{array}\right) \]

    Substituting back: \(2(1)+2+3 = 7\), \(1+2(2)+3 = 8\), \(1+2+2(3) = 9\). \(\checkmark\)

    CHECK IN R 
    A <- matrix(c(2,1,1, 1,2,1, 1,1,2), 3, 3, byrow = TRUE)
sweep <- function(M) {                 # Gauss-Jordan on an augmented matrix
  for (k in seq_len(nrow(M))) {
    M[k, ] <- M[k, ] / M[k, k]
    for (i in seq_len(nrow(M))[-k]) M[i, ] <- M[i, ] - M[i, k] * M[k, ]
  }
  M
}

    Practical 3 — Solution of Linear Equations by the Doolittle Method

    1. Problem

    Solve \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) with \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{b} = (7, 8, 9)'\) by the Doolittle (LU) method, and find \(\det A\).

    2. Aim

    To factorise a matrix into unit lower and upper triangular factors once, and solve by forward and back substitution.

    3. Formula

    \[ A = LU, \quad u_{ij} = a_{ij} - \sum_{k<i}\ell_{ik}u_{kj} \ (j \ge i), \quad \ell_{ji} = \frac{a_{ji} - \sum_{k<i}\ell_{jk}u_{ki}}{u_{ii}} \ (j > i); \qquad L\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{b}, \ U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{z}; \qquad \det A = \prod_i u_{ii} \]

    Applying it:

    1. The first row of \(U\) and first column of \(L\).
    2. The second row and column; then \(u_{33}\).
    3. Check \(LU = A\) on one entry.
    4. Forward substitution, then back substitution.

    4. Calculation

    \(u_{1j} = a_{1j}\): first row of \(U\) is \((2, 1, 1)\); \(\ell_{21} = \ell_{31} = \tfrac12\).

    \[ u_{22} = 2 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac32, \quad u_{23} = 1 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac12, \quad \ell_{32} = \frac{1 - \tfrac12}{\tfrac32} = \frac13, \quad u_{33} = 2 - \tfrac12 - \tfrac13\left(\tfrac12\right) = \tfrac43. \] \[ L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \tfrac12 & 1 & 0 \\ \tfrac12 & \tfrac13 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & \tfrac32 & \tfrac12 \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac43 \end{pmatrix}. \]

    Check: row 3 of \(L\) against column 3 of \(U\): \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac16 + \tfrac43 = 2 = a_{33}\). \(\checkmark\)

    \[ z_1 = 7, \quad z_2 = 8 - \tfrac72 = \tfrac92, \quad z_3 = 9 - \tfrac72 - \tfrac32 = 4; \qquad x_3 = \frac{4}{\tfrac43} = 3, \quad x_2 = \frac{\tfrac92 - \tfrac32}{\tfrac32} = 2, \quad x_1 = \frac{7 - 2 - 3}{2} = 1. \]
    CHECK IN R 
    A <- matrix(c(2,1,1, 1,2,1, 1,1,2), 3, 3, byrow = TRUE)
doolittle <- function(A) {             # Doolittle LU, written out rather than calling a library
  n <- nrow(A); L <- diag(n); U <- matrix(0, n, n)
  for (i in 1:n) {
    for (j in i:n) U[i, j] <- A[i, j] - sum(L[i, ] * U[, j])
    if (i < n) for (j in (i+1):n)
      L[j, i] <- (A[j, i] - sum(L[j, ] * U[, i])) / U[i, i]
  }
  list(L = L, U = U)
}
lu <- doolittle(A); lu$L; lu$U
max(abs(lu$L %*% lu$U - A))          # 0 -- the factorisation is exact
z <- forwardsolve(lu$L, c(7, 8, 9)); backsolve(lu$U, z)
prod(diag(lu$U))                     # det(A)

    \(\mathbf{x} = (1, 2, 3)'\) and \(\det A = 1 \times 2 \times \tfrac32 \times \tfrac43 = 4\). Factorise once and any number of right-hand sides cost only the two substitutions — which is why this, and not Gauss–Jordan, is what numerical libraries do.

    Practical 4 — Moore–Penrose Inverse by the Penrose Method

    1. Problem

    Find the Moore–Penrose inverse of \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) and verify the four Penrose conditions.

    2. Aim

    To find the Moore–Penrose inverse of a matrix of full column rank and verify that it satisfies all four Penrose conditions.

    3. Formula

    \[ \text{full column rank: } A^{+} = (A'A)^{-1}A'; \qquad \text{(i) } AA^{+}A = A, \ \text{(ii) } A^{+}AA^{+} = A^{+}, \ \text{(iii) } (AA^{+})' = AA^{+}, \ \text{(iv) } (A^{+}A)' = A^{+}A \]

    Applying it:

    1. Check the rank.
    2. Form and invert \(A'A\); multiply by \(A'\).
    3. Form \(A^{+}A\) and \(AA^{+}\); check the four conditions.

    4. Calculation

    The two columns are not multiples of one another, so \(\operatorname{rank}(A) = 2\), full column rank.

    \[ A'A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad (A'A)^{-1} = \frac13\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A^{+} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \] \[ A^{+}A = I_2, \qquad AA^{+} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \operatorname{trace}(AA^{+}) = 2. \]
    conditioncheckholds?
    (i) \(AA^{+}A = A\)\(A(A^{+}A) = A I_2 = A\)yes
    (ii) \(A^{+}AA^{+} = A^{+}\)\(I_2 A^{+} = A^{+}\)yes
    (iii) \((AA^{+})' = AA^{+}\)\(AA^{+}\) is symmetricyes
    (iv) \((A^{+}A)' = A^{+}A\)\(I_2\) is symmetricyes

    5. Result

    \(A^{+} = \frac13\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), satisfying all four conditions. \(AA^{+}\) is not \(I_3\) and could not be (its rank is 2): it is the projection onto the column space of \(A\), with trace 2 = rank. Applied to \(\mathbf{y}\), \(A^{+}\) returns the least-squares coefficients (worked also in Unit 1, Example 1.3).

    Practical 5 — Generalized Inverse of a Matrix

    1. Problem

    Find a generalized inverse of the singular matrix \(S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\), and check whether it is the Moore–Penrose inverse.

    2. Aim

    To construct a generalized inverse of a singular matrix from a non-singular submatrix, and to see that it need not be the Moore–Penrose inverse.

    3. Formula

    \[ SGS = S; \qquad G = \text{(inverse of a non-singular } r \times r \text{ submatrix, transposed back into place, zeros elsewhere)} \]

    Here \(\det S = 0\) and \(\operatorname{rank}(S) = 2\).

    Applying it:

    1. Find a non-singular \(2 \times 2\) submatrix and invert it.
    2. Place its entries back in the transposed positions, zeros elsewhere.
    3. Verify \(SGS = S\); then test the other Penrose conditions.

    4. Calculation

    Rows 1 and 3, columns 1 and 3: \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\det = 2\), inverse \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}\). Placing it back:

    \[ G = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad SG = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad (SG)S = S. \checkmark \]

    \(GSG = G\) also holds, so \(G\) is reflexive. But \((SG)' = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \ne SG\): condition (iii) fails.

    5. Result

    \(G\) is a (reflexive) generalized inverse of \(S\) but not its Moore–Penrose inverse. There are many g-inverses — a different submatrix gives a different \(G\), and every one satisfies \(SGS = S\); the Moore–Penrose inverse is the single one that is also symmetric in both products. In Unit 4 that non-uniqueness is why \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) is not unique in a rank-deficient model while \(\boldsymbol\ell'\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) for estimable \(\boldsymbol\ell\) is.

    Practical 6 — Characteristic Equation from Traces of Successive Powers

    1. Problem

    Find the characteristic equation of \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) from the traces of its powers, its roots, and its eigenvectors.

    2. Aim

    To obtain the characteristic equation of a matrix from the traces of its successive powers, and its roots and vectors.

    3. Formula

    \[ \lambda^{3} - c_1\lambda^{2} + c_2\lambda - c_3 = 0, \qquad c_1 = t_1, \quad c_2 = \frac{c_1t_1 - t_2}{2}, \quad c_3 = \frac{c_2t_1 - c_1t_2 + t_3}{3}, \qquad t_k = \operatorname{trace}(A^{k}) \]

    Checks: \(c_3 = \det A\); the roots sum to the trace and multiply to the determinant.

    Applying it:

    1. Compute \(A^{2}\), \(A^{3}\) and the three traces.
    2. The coefficients; the equation.
    3. Factorise; check.
    4. The eigenvectors from \((A - \lambda I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}\).

    4. Calculation

    \[ A^{2} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 5 & 5 \\ 5 & 6 & 5 \\ 5 & 5 & 6 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A^{3} = \begin{pmatrix} 22 & 21 & 21 \\ 21 & 22 & 21 \\ 21 & 21 & 22 \end{pmatrix}; \qquad t_1 = 6, \ t_2 = 18, \ t_3 = 66. \] \[ c_1 = 6, \quad c_2 = \frac{36 - 18}{2} = 9, \quad c_3 = \frac{54 - 108 + 66}{3} = 4; \qquad \lambda^{3} - 6\lambda^{2} + 9\lambda - 4 = (\lambda - 1)^{2}(\lambda - 4) = 0. \]

    \(c_3 = 4 = \det A\); \(4 + 1 + 1 = 6\) and \(4 \times 1 \times 1 = 4\). \(\checkmark\) For \(\lambda = 4\): \(A - 4I\) has rank 2 and \(\mathbf{x} = (1, 1, 1)'\). For \(\lambda = 1\): \(A - I\) has rank 1, the single equation \(x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 0\), and a basis \((-1, 1, 0)'\), \((-1, 0, 1)'\), both orthogonal to \((1, 1, 1)'\).

    5. Result

    The characteristic equation is \(\lambda^{3} - 6\lambda^{2} + 9\lambda - 4 = 0\), with roots 4 (vector \((1,1,1)'\)) and 1 twice (any vector with \(x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 0\)) (worked also in Unit 2, Example 2.1).

    Practical 7 — Spectral Decomposition of a Third-Order Matrix

    1. Problem

    Find the spectral decomposition of \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) (roots 4 and 1, twice, from Practical 6), and from it \(A^{-1}\) and \(A^{1/2}\).

    2. Aim

    To write a symmetric matrix as a sum of its eigenvalues times the projections onto its eigenspaces, and use it for functions of the matrix.

    3. Formula

    \[ A = \sum_j \lambda_{(j)} P_j, \qquad P_j^{2} = P_j, \quad P_jP_k = O \ (j \ne k), \quad \sum_j P_j = I, \qquad A^{m} = \sum_j \lambda_{(j)}^{m}P_j \]

    Applying it:

    1. The projection onto the \(\lambda = 4\) eigenspace, \(P_1 = \mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}'/\mathbf{v}'\mathbf{v}\).
    2. \(P_2 = I - P_1\).
    3. Check idempotence, orthogonality and the traces (the multiplicities).
    4. Reassemble \(A\); then \(m = -1\) and \(m = \tfrac12\).

    4. Calculation

    Practical 8 — Simultaneous Reduction of a Pair of Quadratic Forms

    1. Problem

    Reduce the quadratic forms \(\mathbf{x}'A\mathbf{x}\) and \(\mathbf{x}'B\mathbf{x}\) simultaneously, where \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\).

    2. Aim

    To reduce two quadratic forms, one positive definite, to sums of squares by one transformation.

    3. Formula

    \[ |A - \lambda B| = 0; \qquad \mathbf{x}'B\mathbf{x} = \sum y_i^{2}, \quad \mathbf{x}'A\mathbf{x} = \sum \lambda_i y_i^{2} \]

    \(B\) must be positive definite.

    Applying it:

    1. Check \(B\) (and \(A\)) positive definite by leading minors.
    2. Form \(|A - \lambda B|\); factor out the common term.
    3. Verify each root; write the canonical forms.

    4. Calculation

    \(B\): \(2 > 0\), \(\det B = 1 > 0\); \(A\): \(5 > 0\), \(\det A = 6 > 0\). \(\checkmark\)

    Practical 9 — Orthonormal Basis by the Gram–Schmidt Process

    1. Problem

    Construct an orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{R}^{3}\) from \(\mathbf{v}_1 = (1,1,0)'\), \(\mathbf{v}_2 = (1,0,1)'\), \(\mathbf{v}_3 = (0,1,1)'\).

    2. Aim

    To turn a set of independent vectors into an orthonormal basis of the same space.

    3. Formula

    \[ \mathbf{u}_1 = \mathbf{v}_1, \qquad \mathbf{u}_k = \mathbf{v}_k - \sum_{j<k}\frac{\mathbf{v}_k'\mathbf{u}_j}{\mathbf{u}_j'\mathbf{u}_j}\mathbf{u}_j, \qquad \mathbf{e}_k = \frac{\mathbf{u}_k}{\|\mathbf{u}_k\|} \]

    Applying it:

    1. Take \(\mathbf{u}_1 = \mathbf{v}_1\).
    2. Subtract from \(\mathbf{v}_2\) its projection on \(\mathbf{u}_1\); check orthogonality.
    3. Subtract from \(\mathbf{v}_3\) its projections on \(\mathbf{u}_1\) and \(\mathbf{u}_2\); check.
    4. Normalise.

    4. Calculation

    \[ \mathbf{u}_1 = (1,1,0)', \ \mathbf{u}_1'\mathbf{u}_1 = 2; \qquad \mathbf{u}_2 = (1,0,1)' - \tfrac12(1,1,0)' = \left(\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, 1\right)', \ \mathbf{u}_2'\mathbf{u}_2 = \tfrac32, \ \mathbf{u}_1'\mathbf{u}_2 = 0. \checkmark \] \[ \mathbf{u}_3 = (0,1,1)' - \tfrac12(1,1,0)' - \tfrac13\left(\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,1\right)' = \left(-\tfrac23, \tfrac23, \tfrac23\right)', \quad \mathbf{u}_3'\mathbf{u}_3 = \tfrac43; \quad \mathbf{u}_1'\mathbf{u}_3 = \mathbf{u}_2'\mathbf{u}_3 = 0. \checkmark \] \[ \mathbf{e}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,1,0)', \qquad \mathbf{e}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(1,-1,2)', \qquad \mathbf{e}_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(-1,1,1)'. \]
    CHECK IN R 
    gram_schmidt <- function(V) {          # columns of V in, orthonormal columns out
  E <- V
  for (k in seq_len(ncol(V))) {
    u <- V[, k]
    for (j in seq_len(k - 1)) u <- u - sum(V[, k] * E[, j]) * E[, j]
    E[, k] <- u / sqrt(sum(u^2))
  }
  E
}

    3. 3. Formula — the method named, and why it was chosen over the alternatives (partitioning when one block is already inverted, LU when several right-hand sides share a matrix, sweep-out when the inverse is wanted too), with the steps that apply it.
    4. 4. Calculation — every intermediate matrix written out, not only the final answer; an independent check (\(AA^{-1} = I\), \(LU = A\), \(\sum\lambda_i = \operatorname{trace}\), \(\prod\lambda_i = \det\), substitution back, or the same quantity computed a second way); and the R program, written without packages, with its output compared with the hand calculation.
    5. 5. Result — the conclusion in words: not "the inverse is ..." but what the numbers mean for the data.
