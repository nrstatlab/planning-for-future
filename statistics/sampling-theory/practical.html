    Practical — Sampling Theory (STS-206, Section B)

    All seven prescribed Section B experiments of STS-206: where each is worked in the units, plus the three that are not — the ratio and regression estimators in simple random sampling compared with the sample mean, and separate against combined regression estimators in stratified sampling.

    About this course. STS-206 is a conventional practical in two sections: Section A, Design and Analysis of Experiments, which belongs to Design and Analysis of Experiments (STS-203), and Section B, Sampling Theory — the seven experiments below. Both sections are examined by hand, so every experiment is worked with full arithmetic and no software.
    Before you start. Everything the BSc paper Sampling Techniques teaches is assumed and is not repeated: drawing a simple random sample with and without replacement, the sample mean and its variance, the finite population correction, stratified sampling and its allocations, and systematic sampling. Only the estimators that are new at this level are worked here.

    1. Drawing a PPS Sample — the Two Procedures

    EXPERIMENT 1 — THE MECHANICS OF SELECTION

    The estimation side of this experiment is Unit 1; what an examiner also wants to see is the draw itself, written out. Take five units with sizes \(x_i = 12, 8, 20, 5, 15\), so \(X = 60\).

    Cumulative total method, with replacement. Form the cumulative ranges

    Unit\(x_i\)CumulativeRange\(p_i = x_i/X\)
    112121–120.200000
    282013–200.133333
    3204021–400.333333
    454541–450.083333
    5156046–600.250000

    Draw a random number in \(1\)–\(60\); the unit whose range contains it is selected. With the random numbers \(37, 09, 52\) the sample is units \(3, 1, 5\). For with replacement the ranges are unchanged between draws; for without replacement the selected unit is struck out and the cumulative column is rebuilt from the remaining units, which is exactly the labour Lahiri's method avoids.

    Lahiri's method. Worked in full at Unit 1, Example 1.2: draw a unit number at random from \(1, \dots, N\) and an independent number from \(1, \dots, M\) where \(M = \max x_i\); accept the unit if the second number does not exceed its size, otherwise discard the pair and draw again. Here \(M = 20\), so the pair \((4, 9)\) is rejected because \(9 > 5\), and the pair \((2, 6)\) is accepted.

    Which estimator goes with which draw. This is the part most often got wrong in the examination, so state it in the first line of the answer:

    DrawEstimator of \(Y\)Variance estimator
    PPSWRHansen–Hurwitz, \(\frac{1}{n}\sum y_i/p_i\) \(\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum\left(\frac{y_i}{p_i} - \hat Y_{HH}\right)^{2}\)
    PPSWORHorvitz–Thompson, \(\sum y_i/\pi_i\) Yates–Grundy form, see Unit 1, section 4

    2. Ratio and Regression Estimators in Simple Random Sampling

    THE THREE ESTIMATORS SIDE BY SIDE

    All three estimate the same \(\bar Y\) from the same sample; they differ only in how much of the auxiliary information they use.

    EstimatorPoint estimateEstimated varianced.f.
    Mean per unit\(\bar y\) \(\left(\frac{1-f}{n}\right)s_y^{2}\)\(n-1\)
    Ratio\(\hat{\bar Y}_R = \frac{\bar y}{\bar x}\bar X\) \(\left(\frac{1-f}{n}\right)\frac{1}{n-1}\sum\left(y_i - \hat R x_i\right)^{2}\) \(n-1\)
    Regression\(\hat{\bar Y}_{lr} = \bar y + b\left(\bar X - \bar x\right)\) \(\left(\frac{1-f}{n}\right)\frac{1}{n-2}\sum\left(y_i - \bar y - b(x_i - \bar x)\right)^{2}\) \(n-2\)

    Why the divisors differ. The ratio estimator fits one constant, the slope through the origin, so \(n-1\) residual degrees of freedom remain; the regression estimator fits an intercept as well, so only \(n-2\) remain. In a small sample that one degree of freedom is not a technicality — the worked example below is a case where the regression estimator has the better fit and the larger estimated variance.

    EXPERIMENT 2 AND 4 — ALL THREE ON ONE SAMPLE

    Given. A population of \(N = 50\) plots. The auxiliary variable \(x\) is last season's yield, known for every plot with \(\bar X = 12\); \(y\) is this season's yield, measured on a simple random sample of \(n = 8\) plots without replacement.

    Plot1234567 8Total
    \(x\)81011131415 91292
    \(y\)202627343539 2330234

    Step 1 — the summary quantities.

    \[ \bar x = \frac{92}{8} = 11.5, \qquad \bar y = \frac{234}{8} = 29.25, \qquad f = \frac{8}{50} = 0.16, \qquad 1 - f = 0.84, \] \[ S_{xx} = \sum(x_i - \bar x)^{2} = 42, \qquad S_{yy} = \sum(y_i - \bar y)^{2} = 291.5, \qquad S_{xy} = \sum(x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y) = 110, \] \[ s_x^{2} = \frac{42}{7} = 6.000000, \qquad s_y^{2} = \frac{291.5}{7} = 41.642857, \qquad s_{xy} = \frac{110}{7} = 15.714286. \]

    The correlation, which decides in advance whether any of this is worth doing, is

    \[ r^{2} = \frac{S_{xy}^{2}}{S_{xx}S_{yy}} = \frac{110^{2}}{42 \times 291.5} = \frac{12100}{12243} = 0.988320, \qquad r = 0.994143. \]

    Step 2 — mean per unit, ignoring \(x\) entirely.

    \[ \hat{\bar Y} = 29.250000, \qquad v\!\left(\bar y\right) = \frac{0.84}{8}\times 41.642857 = 0.105 \times 41.642857 = 4.372500, \] \[ \text{s.e.} = \sqrt{4.372500} = 2.091052. \]

    Step 3 — the ratio estimator.

    \[ \hat R = \frac{\bar y}{\bar x} = \frac{29.25}{11.5} = \frac{117}{46} = 2.543478, \qquad \hat{\bar Y}_R = \frac{117}{46} \times 12 = \frac{702}{23} = 30.521739. \]

    Keep \(\hat R\) as the fraction \(117/46\) while multiplying: rounding it to six decimals first and then multiplying by \(12\) gives \(30.521736\), wrong in the last digit.

    The residuals \(e_i = y_i - \hat R x_i\), which the variance needs, are

    \(i\)1234567 8
    \(e_i\)−0.3478260.565217−0.978261 0.934783−0.6086960.8478260.108696 −0.521739
    \[ \sum e_i^{2} = 3.644612, \qquad s_e^{2} = \frac{3.644612}{7} = 0.520659, \] \[ v\!\left(\hat{\bar Y}_R\right) = \frac{0.84}{8}\times 0.520659 = 0.054669, \qquad \text{s.e.} = 0.233814. \]

    Step 4 — the regression estimator.

    \[ b = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \frac{110}{42} = \frac{55}{21} = 2.619048, \qquad \bar X - \bar x = 12 - 11.5 = 0.5, \] \[ \hat{\bar Y}_{lr} = 29.250000 + 2.619048 \times 0.5 = 29.250000 + 1.309524 = 30.559524. \]

    The residual sum of squares is obtained without listing residuals at all, by the identity

    \[ \sum\left(y_i - \bar y - b(x_i - \bar x)\right)^{2} = S_{yy} - bS_{xy} = 291.5 - 2.619048 \times 110 = 291.5 - 288.095238 = 3.404762, \] \[ s_{y \cdot x}^{2} = \frac{3.404762}{6} = 0.567460, \qquad v\!\left(\hat{\bar Y}_{lr}\right) = \frac{0.84}{8}\times 0.567460 = 0.059583, \qquad \text{s.e.} = 0.244097. \]

    Step 5 — the comparison the experiment asks for.

    EstimatorEstimate of \(\bar Y\)Estimated variance s.e.Efficiency over SRS
    Mean per unit29.2500004.3725002.091052 1.00
    Ratio30.5217390.0546690.233814 80.0
    Regression30.5595240.0595830.244097 73.4

    Each efficiency is \(v(\bar y)\) divided by the variance in its row: \(4.372500/0.054669 = 80.0\) and \(4.372500/0.059583 = 73.4\). One decimal place is all the six-figure variances support, and all an efficiency is ever quoted to.

    Step 6 — the condition for the ratio estimator to be worth using. The large-sample rule is \(\rho > C_x/(2C_y)\), where \(C\) denotes a coefficient of variation. Here

    \[ C_x = \frac{\sqrt{6.000000}}{11.5} = 0.212999, \qquad C_y = \frac{\sqrt{41.642857}}{29.25} = 0.220620, \qquad \frac{C_x}{2C_y} = 0.4827, \]

    and \(r = 0.994143\) is far above it, which the efficiency of \(80\) confirms.

    Interpretation, and the point of the comparison. Both auxiliary estimators cut the standard error by a factor near nine, because \(x\) and \(y\) are almost collinear. Between them the ratio estimator wins here, which surprises students who have been told the regression estimator is never worse. Two things explain it:

    • The fitted line is \(\hat y = -0.869048 + 2.619048x\). Its intercept is very nearly zero, so the line through the origin that the ratio estimator uses is almost the same line — the regression estimator has nothing left to gain.
    • It still pays for the intercept: its residual sum of squares, \(3.404762\), is smaller than the ratio's \(3.644612\), but it is divided by \(n - 2 = 6\) instead of \(n - 1 = 7\), and \(3.404762/6 = 0.567460\) exceeds \(3.644612/7 = 0.520659\).

    The statement "the regression estimator is at least as efficient" is about large-sample variances. With the large-sample formula \(\left(\frac{1-f}{n}\right)s_y^{2}(1 - r^{2}) = 0.105 \times 41.642857 \times 0.011680 = 0.051071\), the regression estimator is indeed the better of the two. At \(n = 8\) the degree of freedom spent on the intercept costs more than the intercept is worth. Report the estimate with its variance and say which formula produced it; an efficiency quoted without that is not checkable.

    3. Separate and Combined Regression Estimators

    THE TWO FORMS \[ \hat{\bar Y}_{lrs} = \sum_{h=1}^{L} W_h\left[\bar y_h + b_h\left(\bar X_h - \bar x_h\right)\right], \qquad \hat{\bar Y}_{lrc} = \bar y_{st} + b_c\left(\bar X - \bar x_{st}\right), \]

    with \(b_h\) the within-stratum slope and \(b_c\) a single pooled slope. The choice repeats the one made for ratio estimators in Unit 2, section 3, and for the same reasons:

    • Separate allows the slope to differ from stratum to stratum, which it usually does, but estimates \(L\) slopes from \(L\) small samples and so carries a bias of order \(\sum W_h/n_h\).
    • Combined estimates one slope from the whole sample, so its bias is of order \(1/n\), but it is wrong in every stratum if the slopes genuinely differ.
    • The working rule: separate when each \(n_h\) is large enough to estimate a slope (say \(n_h \ge 20\)) and the slopes plainly differ; combined otherwise.

    The pooled slope used below is the variance-weighted one,

    \[ b_c = \frac{\sum_h W_h^{2}\,\frac{S_{xy,h}}{n_h(n_h-1)}} {\sum_h W_h^{2}\,\frac{S_{xx,h}}{n_h(n_h-1)}}, \]

    ignoring finite population corrections, which are negligible here.

    EXPERIMENT 5 — SEPARATE AGAINST COMBINED

    Given. The two strata of Unit 2, Example 2.2: \(N_1 = 100\), \(N_2 = 200\), so \(W_1 = 1/3\) and \(W_2 = 2/3\), with known stratum means \(\bar X_1 = 5.5\) and \(\bar X_2 = 17\) and hence \(\bar X = \frac{1}{3}(5.5) + \frac{2}{3}(17) = 13.166667\).

    The sample below has the same stratum means as the one in that example, so the ratio estimates carry over unchanged — but the individual values now scatter about the line instead of lying exactly on it, which a regression needs and a ratio does not.

    Stratum\(n_h\)sample \(x\)sample \(y\) \(\bar x_h\)\(\bar y_h\)
    144, 6, 8, 211, 13, 19, 5512
    2512, 16, 20, 14, 1821, 26, 33, 22, 28 1626

    Step 1 — the within-stratum slopes. For stratum 1 the deviations are \(x - \bar x = -1, 1, 3, -3\) and \(y - \bar y = -1, 1, 7, -7\), so

    \[ S_{xx,1} = 1 + 1 + 9 + 9 = 20, \qquad S_{xy,1} = 1 + 1 + 21 + 21 = 44, \qquad b_1 = \frac{44}{20} = 2.200000. \]

    For stratum 2 the deviations are \(x - \bar x = -4, 0, 4, -2, 2\) and \(y - \bar y = -5, 0, 7, -4, 2\), so

    \[ S_{xx,2} = 16 + 0 + 16 + 4 + 4 = 40, \qquad S_{xy,2} = 20 + 0 + 28 + 8 + 4 = 60, \qquad b_2 = \frac{60}{40} = 1.500000. \]

    Step 2 — the separate estimator.

    \[ \bar X_1 - \bar x_1 = 5.5 - 5 = 0.5, \qquad \bar y_1 + b_1(0.5) = 12 + 1.1 = 13.100000, \] \[ \bar X_2 - \bar x_2 = 17 - 16 = 1, \qquad \bar y_2 + b_2(1) = 26 + 1.5 = 27.500000, \] \[ \hat{\bar Y}_{lrs} = \tfrac13(13.100000) + \tfrac23(27.500000) = 4.366667 + 18.333333 = 22.700000. \]

    Step 3 — the stratified means, which the combined estimator needs.

    \[ \bar y_{st} = \tfrac13(12) + \tfrac23(26) = \frac{64}{3} = 21.333333, \qquad \bar x_{st} = \tfrac13(5) + \tfrac23(16) = \frac{37}{3} = 12.333333. \]

    Step 4 — the pooled slope. With \(n_1(n_1-1) = 12\) and \(n_2(n_2-1) = 20\),

    \[ \text{numerator} = \left(\tfrac13\right)^{2}\frac{44}{12} + \left(\tfrac23\right)^{2}\frac{60}{20} = \frac{11}{27} + \frac{4}{3} = \frac{47}{27} = 1.740741, \] \[ \text{denominator} = \left(\tfrac13\right)^{2}\frac{20}{12} + \left(\tfrac23\right)^{2}\frac{40}{20} = \frac{5}{27} + \frac{8}{9} = \frac{29}{27} = 1.074074, \] \[ b_c = \frac{47/27}{29/27} = \frac{47}{29} = 1.620690. \]

    Step 5 — the combined estimator.

    \[ \bar X - \bar x_{st} = \frac{79}{6} - \frac{37}{3} = \frac{5}{6} = 0.833333, \] \[ \hat{\bar Y}_{lrc} = 21.333333 + \frac{47}{29}\times\frac{5}{6} = 21.333333 + 1.350575 = 22.683908. \]

    As in Step 3 of the previous experiment, the product is formed from the fractions rather than from the rounded decimals; \(1.620690 \times 0.833333\) returns \(1.350574\).

    Step 6 — the comparison, with the ratio estimators alongside.

    EstimatorEstimate of \(\bar Y\)
    Stratified mean, \(\bar y_{st}\) (no auxiliary information)21.333333
    Separate ratio22.816667
    Combined ratio22.774775
    Separate regression22.700000
    Combined regression22.683908
    \[ 22.700000 - 22.683908 = 0.016092. \]

    Interpretation. The separate and combined forms differ by \(0.016092\), about \(0.07\%\) of the estimate, even though the two slopes \(2.2\) and \(1.5\) are not close. That is the usual finding: the separate/combined choice moves the estimate very little and the bias a great deal, so it is decided by \(n_h\), not by how far apart the two numbers come out. With \(n_1 = 4\) and \(n_2 = 5\), four and five observations are nowhere near enough to estimate two slopes reliably, so the combined estimator is the defensible one here — and the answer should say so rather than quoting whichever number is larger.

    Note also that all four auxiliary estimators sit near \(22.7\) while the stratified mean alone gives \(21.333333\). The gain comes from using \(x\) at all; the choice among the four ways of using it is second order.

    How Marks Are Lost

    THE RECURRING ERRORS
    • Pairing the wrong estimator with the draw. Hansen–Hurwitz is for sampling with replacement and uses \(p_i\); Horvitz–Thompson is for without replacement and uses \(\pi_i\). They are not interchangeable, and \(\pi_i \ne np_i\) except under special designs.
    • Dividing the ratio residual sum of squares by \(n-2\). The ratio estimator fits one constant, so the divisor is \(n-1\). The regression estimator fits two, so its divisor is \(n-2\). Using the same divisor for both makes the comparison meaningless.
    • Using \(\bar x\) where \(\bar X\) belongs. The whole point is that \(\bar X\) is known for the population; writing \(\hat R\bar x\) returns \(\bar y\) and the estimator collapses.
    • Forgetting the finite population correction. At \(f = 0.16\) it removes \(16\%\) of the variance, which is not negligible.
    • Quoting an efficiency without the two variances. Write both, then the ratio; an examiner cannot give credit for a single unverifiable number.
    • Treating the cluster or two-stage variance formulae as per-unit. \(S_1^{2}\) in the two-stage formula is the mean square among PSU means; the per-unit between-cluster mean square is \(M\) times it. See Unit 4, section 1.

    What the Practical Record Should Contain

    FOR EACH EXPERIMENT
    1. The aim, and the sampling design named — with or without replacement, equal or unequal probabilities, stratified or clustered.
    2. The population quantities assumed known, and how they are known.
    3. The selection procedure, with the random numbers actually used written down.
    4. The full working: every summary total, every intermediate quantity, no step omitted.
    5. The estimate, its estimated variance, and its standard error.
    6. The comparison with the simple random sampling estimator of the same quantity, as an efficiency, with both variances shown.
    7. The conclusion in words, naming which estimator is recommended and why.
