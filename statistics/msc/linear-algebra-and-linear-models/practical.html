About this paper. STS-106 is a 2-credit practical examined in two sections — Section A conventional (by hand) and Section B using R. Its own note says the R must be written without using packages, so every program below builds the method from arithmetic rather than calling a library routine. Where R has a built-in that does the same job it is shown too, as a check on the hand-written version.

Objectives

  1. Knowing the computational procedures and also their implementation using R.
  2. Finding the inverse of a matrix using various methods.
  3. Applying transformations on matrices.
  4. Applying matrix operations to given data sets — determinant, eigenvalues, eigenvectors, transformations.
  5. Summarizing the properties of data sets based on matrix operations.

ONE MATRIX RUNS THROUGH PRACTICALS 1, 2, 3, 6 AND 7

All five use

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 8 \\ 9 \end{pmatrix}, \]

so five different methods can be checked against one another instead of each being taken on trust. The answers they must all reproduce are \(\det A = 4\), \(\mathbf{x} = (1, 2, 3)'\), and

\[ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 & -1 \\ -1 & -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Practical 1 — Inverse by the Partition Method

THE METHOD, THEN THE ARITHMETIC

The idea. Split \(A\) into blocks and invert the pieces:

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} A_{11} & A_{12} \\ A_{21} & A_{22} \end{pmatrix} \;\Longrightarrow\; A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} B_{11} & B_{12} \\ B_{21} & B_{22} \end{pmatrix}, \]

with \(S = A_{22} - A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}\) the Schur complement and

\[ B_{22} = S^{-1}, \quad B_{12} = -A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}S^{-1}, \quad B_{21} = -S^{-1}A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}, \quad B_{11} = A_{11}^{-1} + A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}S^{-1}A_{21}A_{11}^{-1}. \]

Step 1 — partition. Take \(A_{11}\) as the leading \(2\times2\):

\[ A_{11} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A_{12} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A_{21} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A_{22} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 2 — invert the small block. \(\det A_{11} = 4 - 1 = 3\), so

\[ A_{11}^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 3 — the Schur complement. First

\[ A_{11}^{-1}A_{12} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \]

then \(A_{21}\left(A_{11}^{-1}A_{12}\right) = \tfrac13(1 + 1) = \tfrac23\), so

\[ S = 2 - \tfrac23 = \tfrac43, \qquad S^{-1} = \tfrac34. \]

Step 4 — the off-diagonal blocks.

\[ B_{12} = -\frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\times \frac34 = \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac14 \\ -\tfrac14 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B_{21} = \begin{pmatrix} -\tfrac14 & -\tfrac14 \end{pmatrix}, \]

the second by symmetry of \(A\).

Step 5 — the leading block.

\[ B_{11} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} + \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\times\frac34\times \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} + \frac{1}{12}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Entry by entry: \(\tfrac23 + \tfrac1{12} = \tfrac{8+1}{12} = \tfrac34\), and \(-\tfrac13 + \tfrac1{12} = \tfrac{-4+1}{12} = -\tfrac14\).

Step 6 — assemble and check.

\[ A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} \tfrac34 & -\tfrac14 & -\tfrac14 \\ -\tfrac14 & \tfrac34 & -\tfrac14 \\ -\tfrac14 & -\tfrac14 & \tfrac34 \end{pmatrix}, \]

which is the matrix obtained in Unit 2 by Cayley–Hamilton and again by spectral decomposition. Three independent routes, one answer. \(\checkmark\)

Practical 2 — Sweep-Out (Gauss–Jordan)

SWEEPING THE AUGMENTED MATRIX TO THE IDENTITY

Step 0 — start.

\[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 2 & 1 & 1 & 7 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 & 8 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 & 9 \end{array}\right) \]

Step 1 — make the first pivot 1, then clear its column. Divide \(R_1\) by 2, then \(R_2 \to R_2 - R_1\) and \(R_3 \to R_3 - R_1\):

\[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & \tfrac12 & \tfrac12 & \tfrac72 \\ 0 & \tfrac32 & \tfrac12 & \tfrac92 \\ 0 & \tfrac12 & \tfrac32 & \tfrac{11}{2} \end{array}\right) \]

Step 2 — second pivot. Divide \(R_2\) by \(\tfrac32\), then \(R_1 \to R_1 - \tfrac12 R_2\) and \(R_3 \to R_3 - \tfrac12 R_2\):

\[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 0 & \tfrac13 & \tfrac53 \\ 0 & 1 & \tfrac13 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac43 & 4 \end{array}\right) \]

Step 3 — third pivot. Divide \(R_3\) by \(\tfrac43\) to get \((0,0,1 \mid 3)\), then \(R_1 \to R_1 - \tfrac13 R_3\) and \(R_2 \to R_2 - \tfrac13 R_3\):

\[ \left(\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 3 \end{array}\right) \]

Step 4 — read and verify. \(\mathbf{x} = (1,2,3)'\), and substituting back: \(2(1)+2+3 = 7\), \(1+2(2)+3 = 8\), \(1+2+2(3) = 9\). \(\checkmark\)

Note. Augmenting with \(I\) instead of \(\mathbf{b}\) and running the same three steps produces \(A^{-1}\) — the sweep-out method inverts and solves with identical work.

Practical 3 — The Doolittle (LU) Method

FACTORISE ONCE, SOLVE TWICE

The idea. Write \(A = LU\) with \(L\) unit lower triangular and \(U\) upper triangular. Then \(A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) becomes \(L(U\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{b}\), solved as \(L\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{b}\) forwards and then \(U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{z}\) backwards.

Step 1 — first row of \(U\), first column of \(L\). \(u_{1j} = a_{1j}\), so the first row of \(U\) is \((2, 1, 1)\); and \(\ell_{i1} = a_{i1}/u_{11}\), giving \(\ell_{21} = \tfrac12\), \(\ell_{31} = \tfrac12\).

Step 2 — second row and column.

\[ u_{22} = a_{22} - \ell_{21}u_{12} = 2 - \tfrac12(1) = \tfrac32, \qquad u_{23} = a_{23} - \ell_{21}u_{13} = 1 - \tfrac12(1) = \tfrac12, \] \[ \ell_{32} = \frac{a_{32} - \ell_{31}u_{12}}{u_{22}} = \frac{1 - \tfrac12(1)}{\tfrac32} = \frac{\tfrac12}{\tfrac32} = \frac13. \]

Step 3 — the last entry.

\[ u_{33} = a_{33} - \ell_{31}u_{13} - \ell_{32}u_{23} = 2 - \tfrac12(1) - \tfrac13\left(\tfrac12\right) = 2 - \tfrac12 - \tfrac16 = \tfrac43. \]

Step 4 — the factorisation.

\[ L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ \tfrac12 & 1 & 0 \\ \tfrac12 & \tfrac13 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & \tfrac32 & \tfrac12 \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac43 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Check \(LU = A\) on one entry: row 3 of \(L\) against column 3 of \(U\) gives \(\tfrac12(1) + \tfrac13\left(\tfrac12\right) + 1\left(\tfrac43\right) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac16 + \tfrac43 = \tfrac{3 + 1 + 8}{6} = 2 = a_{33}\). \(\checkmark\)

Step 5 — forward substitution, \(L\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{b}\).

\[ z_1 = 7, \qquad z_2 = 8 - \tfrac12(7) = \tfrac92, \qquad z_3 = 9 - \tfrac12(7) - \tfrac13\left(\tfrac92\right) = 9 - \tfrac72 - \tfrac32 = 4. \]

Step 6 — back substitution, \(U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{z}\).

\[ x_3 = \frac{4}{\tfrac43} = 3, \qquad x_2 = \frac{\tfrac92 - \tfrac12(3)}{\tfrac32} = \frac{3}{\tfrac32} = 2, \qquad x_1 = \frac{7 - 1(2) - 1(3)}{2} = \frac{2}{2} = 1. \]

Again \(\mathbf{x} = (1,2,3)'\). \(\checkmark\)

A free determinant. \(\det A = \det L \cdot \det U = 1 \times (2)\left(\tfrac32\right)\left(\tfrac43\right) = 4\), matching. Factorise once and any number of right-hand sides cost only the two substitutions — which is why this, and not Gauss–Jordan, is what numerical libraries do.

Practicals 4 and 5 — Moore–Penrose and Generalized Inverses

PRACTICAL 4 — BY THE PENROSE CONDITIONS

Worked in full at Unit 1, Example 1.3: for

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^{+} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \]

with all four conditions checked there. \(A^{+}A = I_2\) and \(AA^{+}\) is the projection onto the column space, with trace \(2 = \operatorname{rank}(A)\).

PRACTICAL 5 — A g-INVERSE OF A SINGULAR MATRIX, AND WHY IT IS NOT THE SAME THING

Given.

\[ S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det S = 0, \qquad \operatorname{rank}(S) = 2. \]

Step 1 — the standard recipe. Find any non-singular \(r \times r\) submatrix, where \(r = \operatorname{rank}\); invert it; transpose; and place the entries back in the transposed positions, with zeros elsewhere.

Step 2 — pick the submatrix. Rows 1 and 3, columns 1 and 3 give

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det = 2 \ne 0, \qquad \text{inverse } \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 3 — place it back. The entries return to positions \((1,1), (1,3), (3,1), (3,3)\), and everything else is zero:

\[ G = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 4 — verify the defining condition \(SGS = S\). First

\[ SG = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \]

and then

\[ (SG)S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = S. \checkmark \]

Step 5 — check the other Penrose conditions, and find one fails. \(GSG = G\) does hold, so \(G\) is reflexive. But

\[ (SG)' = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \ne SG, \]

so condition (iii) fails and \(G\) is not the Moore–Penrose inverse.

Interpretation. This is the practical's real lesson. A generalized inverse is easy to construct and there are many of them — a different choice of submatrix in Step 2 gives a different \(G\), and every one satisfies \(SGS = S\). The Moore–Penrose inverse is the single one that is also symmetric in both products. In Unit 4 that non-uniqueness is exactly why \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) is not unique in a rank-deficient model while \(\boldsymbol\ell'\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) for estimable \(\boldsymbol\ell\) is.

Practicals 6, 7 and 8 — Eigenstructure

ALL THREE ARE WORKED IN THE UNITS, WITH ARITHMETIC
  • Practical 6, the characteristic equation from traces of successive powers: Unit 2, Example 2.1. From \(\operatorname{trace}(A) = 6\), \(\operatorname{trace}(A^{2}) = 18\), \(\operatorname{trace}(A^{3}) = 66\) the coefficients are \(6, 9, 4\), giving \(\lambda^{3} - 6\lambda^{2} + 9\lambda - 4 = 0\) and roots \(4, 1, 1\).
  • Practical 7, spectral decomposition of a third-order matrix: Unit 2, Example 2.4. \(A = 4P_1 + P_2\) with \(P_1 = J/3\) and \(P_2 = I - J/3\), from which the inverse and the square root follow by changing one exponent.
  • Practical 8, simultaneous reduction of two quadratic forms: Unit 3, Example 3.3. \(|A - \lambda B| = (2-\lambda)(3-\lambda)\), so the pair reduces to \(y_1^{2} + y_2^{2}\) and \(2y_1^{2} + 3y_2^{2}\).
  • Practical 9, orthonormal basis by Gram–Schmidt: Unit 1, Example 1.1, with all three orthogonality checks carried out.

Practical 10 — The Variance–Covariance Matrix

Given. Five units measured on three variables:

Step 1 — one entry, in full. The \((1,1)\) entry is the variance of \(x_1\):

\[ s_{11} = \frac{(2-6)^{2} + (4-6)^{2} + (6-6)^{2} + (8-6)^{2} + (10-6)^{2}}{5-1} = \frac{16 + 4 + 0 + 4 + 16}{4} = \frac{40}{4} = 10. \]

The \((1,2)\) entry is the covariance of \(x_1\) and \(x_2\):

\[ s_{12} = \frac{(-4)(-2) + (-2)(0) + (0)(-1) + (2)(2) + (4)(1)}{4} = \frac{8 + 0 + 0 + 4 + 4}{4} = \frac{16}{4} = 4. \]

Step 3 — its characteristics.

The ratio \(1.575/57.5 = 0.0274\) says the correlations have collapsed the cloud to under \(3\%\) of the volume three uncorrelated variables with these variances would occupy.

Interpretation. \(x_1\) is strongly related to both others, but \(x_2\) and \(x_3\) are almost unrelated to each other \((r = 0.2085)\). Their apparent connection runs entirely through \(x_1\) — which is exactly what the partial correlations of Practical 13 are designed to expose.

Practical 11 — Simple Regression with Lack of Fit

REPLICATION IS WHAT MAKES A LACK-OF-FIT TEST POSSIBLE

Given. Eight observations, four distinct \(x\) values with two replicates each:

\(x\)11223344
\(y\)34678101113

Step 1 — the raw sums.

\[ n = 8, \quad \sum x = 20, \quad \sum y = 62, \quad \sum x^{2} = 60, \quad \sum xy = 183, \quad \sum y^{2} = 564. \]

Step 2 — the corrected sums of squares and products.

\[ S_{xx} = 60 - \frac{20^{2}}{8} = 60 - 50 = 10, \qquad S_{xy} = 183 - \frac{20 \times 62}{8} = 183 - 155 = 28, \] \[ S_{yy} = 564 - \frac{62^{2}}{8} = 564 - 480.5 = 83.5. \]

Step 3 — the coefficients.

\[ b_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = \frac{28}{10} = 2.8, \qquad b_0 = \bar y - b_1\bar x = \frac{62}{8} - 2.8\left(\frac{20}{8}\right) = 7.75 - 7.0 = 0.75. \]

Step 4 — the analysis of variance.

\[ SSR = b_1 S_{xy} = 2.8 \times 28 = 78.4, \qquad SSE = 83.5 - 78.4 = 5.1, \] \[ R^{2} = \frac{78.4}{83.5} = 0.938922, \qquad \bar R^{2} = 1 - (1 - 0.938922)\frac{7}{6} = 0.928743, \] \[ MSE = \frac{5.1}{6} = 0.850000. \]

Step 5 — the confidence interval for the slope.

\[ SE(b_1) = \sqrt{\frac{MSE}{S_{xx}}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.85}{10}} = \sqrt{0.085} = 0.291548, \] \[ b_1 \pm t_{6,\,0.025}\,SE(b_1) = 2.8 \pm 2.446912 \times 0.291548 = (2.086609,\ 3.513391). \]

Step 6 — pure error, from the replicates alone. At each \(x\) take the deviation of the two values from their own mean:

\(x\)valuesmeancontribution
13, 43.50.5
26, 76.50.5
38, 109.02.0
411, 1312.02.0
SS pure error5.0

with \(n - c = 8 - 4 = 4\) degrees of freedom, where \(c = 4\) is the number of distinct \(x\) values.

Step 7 — lack of fit, by subtraction.

\[ SS_{LOF} = SSE - SS_{PE} = 5.1 - 5.0 = 0.1, \qquad \text{d.f.} = c - 2 = 2, \] \[ MS_{LOF} = \frac{0.1}{2} = 0.05, \qquad MS_{PE} = \frac{5.0}{4} = 1.25, \] \[ F = \frac{0.05}{1.25} = 0.040000, \qquad P\left(F_{2,4} > 0.04\right) = 0.961169. \]

Step 8 — conclude. There is no evidence of lack of fit; a straight line is adequate.

Interpretation. Notice that without replication, \(SSE\) could not have been split and the test would be impossible — the residual would confound curvature with noise. That is the design lesson: if a model is to be tested rather than merely fitted, the design must repeat at least some \(x\) values.

Practical 12 — Multiple Regression

THE NORMAL EQUATIONS, SOLVED AS A MATRIX PROBLEM

Given. \(y\) regressed on \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) from the data of Practical 10, with \(y = 12, 18, 20, 28, 32\).

Step 1 — form \(X'X\) and \(X'\mathbf{y}\).

\[ X'X = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 30 & 15 \\ 30 & 220 & 106 \\ 15 & 106 & 55 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad X'\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 110 \\ 760 \\ 374 \end{pmatrix}. \]

(The first row is \(n\), \(\sum x_1\), \(\sum x_2\); the rest are the sums of squares and cross-products.)

Step 2 — check it is invertible. \(\det(X'X) = 720 \ne 0\), so the parameters are all estimable and the ordinary formula applies.

Step 3 — solve.

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \left(X'X\right)^{-1}X'\mathbf{y} = \begin{pmatrix} 19/3 \\ 37/18 \\ 10/9 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6.333333 \\ 2.055556 \\ 1.111111 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 4 — the analysis of variance.

\[ SST = 256.000000, \qquad SSR = 254.444444, \qquad SSE = 1.555556, \] \[ R^{2} = \frac{254.444444}{256} = 0.993924, \qquad \bar R^{2} = 1 - (1 - 0.993924)\frac{4}{2} = 0.987847, \] \[ MSE = \frac{1.555556}{5 - 3} = 0.777778, \qquad F = \frac{254.444444/2}{0.777778} = 163.571429, \] \[ P\left(F_{2,2} > 163.571429\right) = 0.006076. \]

Step 5 — standard errors, from the diagonal of \((X'X)^{-1}\).

coefficientestimate\(c_{jj}\)\(SE = \sqrt{MSE \cdot c_{jj}}\)\(t\)\(P(|t_2| > |t|)\)
\(b_1\)2.0555565/720.2324068.8446920.012543
\(b_2\)1.1111115/180.4648112.3904570.139337

Interpretation. The model as a whole is highly significant \((p = 0.0061)\) and \(x_1\) is individually significant, but \(x_2\) is not \((p = 0.1393)\) — with only two error degrees of freedom there is very little power. This is the classic pattern warning that a model may be over-fitted relative to its sample size, and it is why \(\bar R^{2}\), which penalises extra regressors, is reported beside \(R^{2}\).

Practical 13 — Simple, Partial and Multiple Correlation

WHAT PARTIALLING OUT ACTUALLY CHANGES

Step 1 — the simple correlations, on the same data:

\[ r_{01} = r(y, x_1) = 0.988212, \qquad r_{02} = r(y, x_2) = 0.869626, \qquad r_{12} = r(x_1, x_2) = 0.800000. \]

Step 2 — the partial correlations.

\[ r_{01.2} = \frac{r_{01} - r_{02}r_{12}}{\sqrt{\left(1 - r_{02}^{2}\right)\left(1 - r_{12}^{2}\right)}}. \]

Numerator: \(0.988212 - 0.869626 \times 0.800000 = 0.988212 - 0.695701 = 0.292511\). Denominator: \(\sqrt{(1 - 0.756249)(1 - 0.640000)} = \sqrt{0.243751 \times 0.360000} = \sqrt{0.087750} = 0.296227\). Hence

\[ r_{01.2} = \frac{0.292511}{0.296226} = 0.987457. \]

The same calculation with the roles exchanged gives \(r_{02.1} = 0.860663\).

Step 3 — the multiple correlation.

\[ R_{0.12} = \sqrt{\frac{r_{01}^{2} + r_{02}^{2} - 2r_{01}r_{02}r_{12}}{1 - r_{12}^{2}}} = 0.996957. \]

Step 4 — cross-check against Practical 12. \(\sqrt{R^{2}} = \sqrt{0.993924} = 0.996957\). The two routes agree to six decimals. \(\checkmark\)

Interpretation. Holding \(x_2\) fixed barely changes the relation between \(y\) and \(x_1\) \((0.9882 \to 0.9875)\), so \(x_1\) carries information of its own. Holding \(x_1\) fixed also leaves \(r(y, x_2)\) high \((0.8696 \to 0.8607)\). Contrast this with the covariance matrix of Practical 10, where \(x_2\) and \(x_3\) appeared related only through \(x_1\) — that is the case where a partial correlation collapses towards zero, and it is what the technique is for.

Practical 14 — Testing Multicollinearity

WORKED IN FULL IN THE UNIT

Unit 4, Example 4.2 computes the variance inflation factors for a design in which \(x_2\) is almost exactly \(2x_1\): \(VIF_1 = 184.2593\), \(VIF_2 = 162.6667\), \(VIF_3 = 3.7037\), against pairwise correlations \(r(x_1,x_2) = 0.995893\), \(r(x_1,x_3) = 0.800000\) and \(r(x_2,x_3) = 0.769554\).

The three standard diagnostics, in the order worth applying them:

  1. The correlation matrix. Cheap, and catches pairwise collinearity — but blind to a relation among three or more regressors.
  2. Variance inflation factors. \(VIF_j = 1/(1 - R_j^{2})\), which does catch multi-way relations. The usual warning line is \(VIF > 10\).
  3. The condition number \(\kappa = \sqrt{\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}}\) of the correlation matrix, using the eigenvalues of Unit 2. Above about 30 is usually taken as serious. It is the only one of the three that gives a single number for the design as a whole.

## Practical 3 -- Doolittle LU, written out rather than calling a library
doolittle <- function(A) {
  n <- nrow(A); L <- diag(n); U <- matrix(0, n, n)
  for (i in 1:n) {
    for (j in i:n) U[i, j] <- A[i, j] - sum(L[i, ] * U[, j])
    if (i < n) for (j in (i+1):n)
      L[j, i] <- (A[j, i] - sum(L[j, ] * U[, i])) / U[i, i]
  }
  list(L = L, U = U)
}
A <- matrix(c(2,1,1, 1,2,1, 1,1,2), 3, 3, byrow = TRUE)
lu <- doolittle(A); lu$L; lu$U
max(abs(lu$L %*% lu$U - A))          # 0 -- the factorisation is exact
prod(diag(lu$U))                     # 4 = det(A)

## Practical 6 -- characteristic equation from traces of powers
tr <- function(M) sum(diag(M))
A2 <- A %*% A; A3 <- A2 %*% A
t1 <- tr(A); t2 <- tr(A2); t3 <- tr(A3)
c1 <- t1; c2 <- (c1*t1 - t2)/2; c3 <- (c2*t1 - c1*t2 + t3)/3
c(c1, c2, c3)                        # 6 9 4
polyroot(c(-c3, c2, -c1, 1))         # 1, 1, 4

## Practical 5 -- a g-inverse from a non-singular submatrix
ginv_sub <- function(S, rows, cols) {
  G <- matrix(0, ncol(S), nrow(S))
  G[cols, rows] <- solve(S[rows, cols])
  G
}
S <- matrix(c(1,1,0, 1,1,0, 0,0,2), 3, 3, byrow = TRUE)
G <- ginv_sub(S, c(1,3), c(1,3))
max(abs(S %*% G %*% S - S))          # 0 -- condition (i) holds
isTRUE(all.equal(t(S %*% G), S %*% G))   # FALSE -- so NOT Moore-Penrose

## Practical 14 -- VIF without the car package
vif_manual <- function(X) {
  sapply(seq_len(ncol(X)), function(j)
    1 / (1 - summary(lm(X[, j] ~ X[, -j]))$r.squared))
}
Z <- cbind(c(1,2,3,4,5), c(2,4,6,8,11), c(1,3,2,5,4))
vif_manual(Z)                        # 184.2593 162.6667 3.7037

What the Practical Record Should Contain

FOR EACH EXPERIMENT
  1. The aim and the data, as given.
  2. The method named, and why it was chosen over the alternatives — partitioning when one block is already inverted, LU when several right-hand sides share a matrix, sweep-out when the inverse is wanted too.
  3. Every intermediate matrix written out, not only the final answer.
  4. An independent check. \(A A^{-1} = I\), \(LU = A\), \(\sum\lambda_i = \operatorname{trace}\), \(\prod\lambda_i = \det\), substitution of the solution back into the equations, or the same quantity computed a second way.
  5. The R program, written without packages, and its output compared with the hand calculation.
  6. The conclusion in words — not "the inverse is ..." but what the numbers mean for the data.
