About this paper. STS-205 is a 2-credit conventional practical in two sections: Section A, Estimation Theory, which belongs to Estimation Theory (STS-201) and is worked with that paper; and Section B, Multivariate Analysis — the thirteen experiments below. Both sections are examined by hand, so every experiment is worked with full arithmetic; R is shown only as a check.

Practical 2 — Writing the Density, and Reading It Backwards

PART (a) — FROM PARAMETERS TO DENSITY

Given. \(\boldsymbol\mu = (5, 8)'\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma} = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 4\\ 4 & 5\end{pmatrix}\), the maximum likelihood estimates of Example 1.3. Asked. Write the bivariate normal density.

Step 1 — the determinant. \(|\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}| = 25 - 16 = 9\), so \(|\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}|^{1/2} = 3\).

Step 2 — the inverse. For a \(2 \times 2\) matrix, swap the diagonal, negate the off-diagonal, divide by the determinant:

\[ \hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}^{-1} = \frac{1}{9}\begin{pmatrix}5 & -4\\ -4 & 5\end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 3 — the normalising constant. With \(p = 2\),

\[ \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{p/2}|\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}|^{1/2}} = \frac{1}{2\pi \times 3} = \frac{1}{18.849556} = 0.053052. \]

Step 4 — expand the quadratic form. Writing \(u = x_1 - 5\) and \(v = x_2 - 8\),

\[ (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)'\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu) = \frac{1}{9}\left(5u^{2} - 8uv + 5v^{2}\right), \]

the cross-term coefficient being \(2 \times (-4) = -8\) because the matrix contributes \(-4\) twice, once above and once below the diagonal.

Step 5 — assemble.

\[ f(x_1, x_2) = 0.053052\, \exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{18}\left[5(x_1-5)^{2} - 8(x_1-5)(x_2-8) + 5(x_2-8)^{2}\right]\right\}, \]

the \(18\) in the denominator being \(2 \times 9\), from the \(-\tfrac12\) and the \(\tfrac19\).

Step 6 — a check at the centre. At \(\mathbf{x} = \boldsymbol\mu\) the exponent is zero, so \(f(5, 8) = 0.053052\), the largest value the density takes. \(\checkmark\)

PART (b) — FROM DENSITY TO PARAMETERS

Given. A bivariate density of the form

\[ f(x_1, x_2) = k\,\exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{12}\left[2(x_1-3)^{2} - 4(x_1-3)(x_2-4) + 5(x_2-4)^{2}\right]\right\}. \]

Asked. \(\boldsymbol\mu\), \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\), \(\rho\), and the constant \(k\).

Step 1 — read the mean. The exponent is a quadratic form in \(x_1 - 3\) and \(x_2 - 4\), and the multivariate normal density is a quadratic form in \(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol\mu\). Therefore

\[ \boldsymbol\mu = \begin{pmatrix}3\\4\end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 2 — recover \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\). The density carries a factor \(-\tfrac12\) in front of the quadratic form, so writing \(-\tfrac{1}{12} = -\tfrac12 \times \tfrac16\),

\[ \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix}2 & -2\\ -2 & 5\end{pmatrix}. \]

The off-diagonal entry is \(-2\) and not \(-4\), because the printed cross term \(-4(x_1-3)(x_2-4)\) is the sum of the \((1,2)\) and \((2,1)\) contributions, which are equal.

Step 3 — invert to get \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\). The determinant of \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\) is

\[ \left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^{2}\left(2 \times 5 - (-2)^{2}\right) = \frac{10 - 4}{36} = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}, \]

so \(|\boldsymbol\Sigma| = 6\), and inverting the \(2 \times 2\) matrix,

\[ \boldsymbol\Sigma = 6 \times \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix}5 & 2\\ 2 & 2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 2\\ 2 & 2\end{pmatrix}. \]

Step 4 — verify.

\[ \boldsymbol\Sigma\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix}5 & 2\\ 2 & 2\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}2 & -2\\ -2 & 5\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix}10 - 4 & -10 + 10\\ 4 - 4 & -4 + 10\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}. \checkmark \]

Step 5 — the correlation.

\[ \rho = \frac{2}{\sqrt{5 \times 2}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{10}} = \frac{2}{3.162278} = 0.632456. \]

Step 6 — the constant.

\[ k = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt 6} = \frac{1}{6.283185 \times 2.449490} = \frac{1}{15.390598} = 0.064975. \]

Interpretation. This is the matrix analysed in Unit 4, Example 4.1, whose principal components were found exactly, with \(\lambda_1 = 6\) and \(\lambda_2 = 1\). So the density written here has contours whose long axis points along \((2, 1)'\) and is \(\sqrt 6 = 2.449\) times the short one. Reading a density backwards is worth practising for exactly this reason: every geometric fact about the distribution is in those four numbers.

Practical 4 — Mahalanobis \(D^{2}\) for One Sample

THE SAME DATA AS THE ONE-SAMPLE T-SQUARED

Given. \(n = 10\), \(p = 2\), \(\bar{\mathbf{x}} = (7, 12)'\), \(\mathbf{S} = \begin{pmatrix}4 & 3\\ 3 & 9\end{pmatrix}\), and \(\boldsymbol\mu_0 = (5, 9)'\) — the data of Unit 3, Example 3.1.

Step 1 — the deviation vector. \(\mathbf{d} = (2, 3)'\).

Step 2 — the inverse. \(|\mathbf{S}| = 27\), so \(\mathbf{S}^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{27}\begin{pmatrix}9 & -3\\ -3 & 4\end{pmatrix}\).

Step 3 — the distance.

\[ D^{2} = \mathbf{d}'\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{d} = \frac{9(4) - 6(2)(3) + 4(9)}{27} = \frac{36 - 36 + 36}{27} = \frac{4}{3} = 1.333333, \] \[ D = \sqrt{1.333333} = 1.154701. \]

Step 4 — relate it to \(T^{2}\).

\[ T^{2} = n\,D^{2} = 10 \times \frac{4}{3} = \frac{40}{3} = 13.333333, \]

which is the statistic of Example 3.1, so the test and its conclusion are already there: \(F = 5.925926\) on \((2, 8)\) degrees of freedom, \(p = 0.026373\).

Step 5 — compare with the Euclidean distance. Ignoring the dispersion matrix entirely,

\[ \|\mathbf{d}\| = \sqrt{2^{2} + 3^{2}} = \sqrt{13} = 3.605551. \]

Interpretation. The Euclidean distance \(3.606\) and the Mahalanobis distance \(1.155\) are not comparable numbers, and the difference is the whole point. The Euclidean figure changes if \(x_1\) is rescaled; the Mahalanobis figure does not. And \(D^{2}\) is small here because the shift \((2, 3)'\) runs along the direction in which the variables already co-vary — the cancellation \(36 - 36\) in Step 3 is that fact, arithmetically. A shift of the same Euclidean size in the opposite direction, say \((2, -3)'\), would give

\[ \frac{36 + 36 + 36}{27} = \frac{108}{27} = 4.000000, \]

three times as far in the metric that matters.

Working These by Hand in an Examination

  • Invert \(2 \times 2\) matrices by the swap-negate-divide rule and keep the determinant outside as a fraction. Carrying \(\tfrac{1}{27}\) through to the end is far safer than converting to decimals early, and it makes the final cancellation visible.
  • Check every inverse by multiplying back to \(\mathbf{I}\). It costs four multiplications and catches the most common arithmetic slip in the paper.
  • Check every eigenvalue set against the trace and the determinant: they must sum to \(\operatorname{tr}\) and multiply to \(\det\). Two checks, no extra work.
  • Check a discriminant function by confirming that \(\bar y_1 - \bar y_2 = D^{2}\); if it does not, the coefficient vector is wrong.
  • Check a scaling solution by rebuilding one or two distances from the recovered coordinates.
  • State the assumption you are relying on. Hotelling's \(T^{2}\) needs multivariate normality; the two-sample version and discriminant analysis need equal dispersion matrices as well. Writing that down is part of the answer, not an aside.

What the Practical Record Should Contain

FOR EACH EXPERIMENT
  1. The aim, in one line.
  2. The data, written out as a matrix with its dimensions stated.
  3. The formula being used, with the null hypothesis where there is one.
  4. Every intermediate matrix — the inverse, the pooled matrix, the eigenvalues — not only the final number.
  5. The arithmetic check that applies to that method, from the list above.
  6. The conclusion in words, naming the assumption it rests on.
