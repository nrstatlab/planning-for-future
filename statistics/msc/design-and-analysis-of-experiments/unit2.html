Where this unit starts — and it starts a long way in. Factorial experiments are already taught at undergraduate level: Design and Analysis of Experiments, Unit 1, section 4 covers all of the following, and none of it is written again here:
  • what a factorial experiment is, and why it beats varying one factor at a time;
  • the \(2^{k}\) notation \((1),\ a,\ b,\ ab\), a letter present meaning that factor at its high level;
  • effects as contrasts, with the \(2^{2}\) formulae and \(SS = (\text{contrast})^{2}/(2^{k}r)\);
  • Yates's algorithm, stated and worked on a \(2^{2}\);
  • what an interaction means, the interaction plot, and the rule that main effects must not be read in isolation when one is present.
Read that section first. This unit begins where it stops, with four things it does not do: run Yates's algorithm through the three passes of a \(2^{3}\) (it says three passes work, and leaves it there), give the algebraic form of the contrasts, establish the variance every effect shares, and handle three levels per factor instead of two. The layout is still a randomised block or completely randomised design, from Unit 2 and Unit 3 of the same course, and the analysis of variance is the one in Unit 1 here.

1. The Contrasts, in Algebraic Form

WRITING DOWN ALL \(2^{k}-1\) CONTRASTS AT ONCE

The undergraduate section gives the \(2^{2}\) contrasts one at a time. Writing them out that way for \(k = 4\) means fifteen contrasts of sixteen terms each, and the sign errors are certain. There is a generating form that produces every one of them mechanically. For \(2^{3}\),

\[ [A] = (a-1)(b+1)(c+1), \qquad [AB] = (a-1)(b-1)(c+1), \qquad [ABC] = (a-1)(b-1)(c-1), \]

expanded formally, with \(1\) standing for the combination \((1)\) and each product of letters standing for the combination containing them. The rule: a factor in the effect contributes \((\text{letter} - 1)\), a factor not in it contributes \((\text{letter} + 1)\). Expanding \([AB]\) gives

\[ (a-1)(b-1)(c+1) = abc + ab + c + (1) - ac - bc - a - b, \]

which is the \(AB\) contrast with its eight signs, obtained without thinking about any of them. Equivalently, and more usefully at the desk: a combination enters with \(+\) when it shares an even number of letters with the effect, and with \(-\) when it shares an odd number.

The conversion to an effect and a sum of squares is the undergraduate one with \(r\) replicates carried through:

\[ \text{effect} = \frac{[\,\cdot\,]}{r\,2^{k-1}}, \qquad SS = \frac{[\,\cdot\,]^{2}}{r\,2^{k}}, \]

each on one degree of freedom, the \(SS\) divisor being \(\sum c_i^{2}\) for a contrast whose \(r2^{k}\) coefficients are all \(\pm 1\).

YATES'S ALGORITHM THROUGH THREE PASSES

The algorithm itself is in the undergraduate section, worked on a \(2^{2}\), which ends with the remark that three passes handle a \(2^{3}\). Example 2.1 below is that run, because the pattern of which column holds which effect is not obvious until it has been seen once.

Two checks that cost nothing, and that the two-pass case is too small to make the point of:

  • the first entry of the final column must be the grand total;
  • the sum of squares of the remaining \(2^{k}-1\) entries, divided by \(r2^{k}\), must equal the treatment sum of squares computed the ordinary way, \(\sum_i T_i^{2}/r - \text{CF}\).

Run both. A single transposed pair in the standard order passes unnoticed otherwise, and it corrupts every effect from that pass onward.

The pattern — large main effects, one two-factor interaction, negligible three-factor interaction — is the usual one, and it is what makes the fractional designs of Unit 3 worth having: if three-factor interactions are reliably negligible, the runs spent estimating them are wasted.

Read the AB interaction from the data rather than the table. The effect of A at low B is \(\left[(a - (1)) + (ac - c)\right]/(2r) = \left[(32-20)+(38-24)\right]/4 = 26/4 = 6.5\); at high B it is \(\left[(ab - b)+(abc - bc)\right]/4 = \left[(44-26)+(52-30)\right]/4 = 40/4 = 10\). The difference is \(3.5\), and half of it, \(1.75\), is exactly the AB effect computed above. That is what an interaction effect is: half the change in one factor's effect when the other factor moves.

The count is \(\binom{k}{j}\) effects with \(j\) letters, and they sum to \(2^{k} - 1\), which is the treatment degrees of freedom — as it must be. Every entry in the table is one degree of freedom, and every one is estimated by a contrast of all \(r2^{k}\) observations, so all effects are estimated with the same precision:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\text{any effect}) = \frac{\sigma^{2}}{r\,2^{k-2}}. \]

Why. An effect is \(\left[\,\cdot\,\right]/(r2^{k-1})\) and the contrast is a sum of \(r2^{k}\) terms each with coefficient \(\pm 1\), so its variance is \(r2^{k}\sigma^{2}\); dividing by \((r2^{k-1})^{2}\) gives \(r2^{k}\sigma^{2}/(r^{2}2^{2k-2}) = \sigma^{2}/(r2^{k-2})\).

The arithmetic grows, the structure does not. At \(k = 4\) there are fifteen contrasts and Yates's algorithm takes four passes, but nothing conceptual is added. What does change is the balance of the design: eleven of the fifteen effects involve three or four factors, and those are usually the ones worth sacrificing — which is the subject of confounding and fractional replication in Unit 3.

3. The \(3^{2}\) Factorial Experiment

WHY THREE LEVELS, AND WHAT IT COSTS

Two levels can only fit a straight line between them. Three levels allow a curvature term, which matters whenever the response has an optimum in the range studied — a temperature that is too low and too high, a dose that is too small and too large. The cost is runs: \(3^{2} = 9\) against \(2^{2} = 4\).

With three levels each factor's \(2\) degrees of freedom split into a linear and a quadratic component, using the orthogonal polynomial coefficients for equally spaced levels:

\[ \text{linear } (-1, 0, +1), \qquad \text{quadratic } (+1, -2, +1), \]

which are orthogonal to each other and to the mean. The interaction's \(4\) degrees of freedom then split into four single-degree components, obtained by multiplying the coefficients:

\[ A_LB_L, \qquad A_LB_Q, \qquad A_QB_L, \qquad A_QB_Q. \]

For a contrast \(C = \sum_{ij} c_i d_j T_{ij}\) on cell totals \(T_{ij}\) over \(r\) replicates,

\[ SS = \frac{C^{2}}{r\left(\sum_i c_i^{2}\right)\left(\sum_j d_j^{2}\right)}. \]

The two divisors are \(2\) for a linear coefficient set and \(6\) for a quadratic one.

Each cell holds two observations differing by 2, so the within-cell sum of squares is \(2\) per cell and \(SS_E = 9 \times 2 = 18\) on \(9(r-1) = 9\) degrees of freedom, \(MS_E = 2\).

Step 1 — the ordinary analysis of variance. With \(\text{CF} = 180^{2}/18 = 1800\) and a raw sum of squares of \(1974\),

\[ SS_A = \frac{42^{2}+64^{2}+74^{2}}{6} - 1800 = \frac{11336}{6} - 1800 = 89.333333, \] \[ SS_B = \frac{46^{2}+60^{2}+74^{2}}{6} - 1800 = \frac{11192}{6} - 1800 = 65.333333, \] \[ SS_{\text{cells}} = \frac{3912}{2} - 1800 = 156, \qquad SS_{AB} = 156 - 89.333333 - 65.333333 = 1.333333, \] \[ SS_{\text{total}} = 1974 - 1800 = 174, \qquad SS_E = 174 - 156 = 18. \]

Step 2 — split A. Apply the coefficients to the row totals \(42, 64, 74\):

\[ C_{A_L} = -42 + 0 + 74 = 32, \qquad SS_{A_L} = \frac{32^{2}}{2 \times 2 \times 3} = \frac{1024}{12} = 85.333333, \] \[ C_{A_Q} = 42 - 2(64) + 74 = -12, \qquad SS_{A_Q} = \frac{(-12)^{2}}{2 \times 6 \times 3} = \frac{144}{36} = 4. \]

The divisors are \(r \sum c_i^{2} \times 3\), the last factor being the three levels of B that each row total spans. The two components add to \(89.333333 = SS_A\). \(\checkmark\)

Step 3 — split B. On the column totals \(46, 60, 74\):

\[ C_{B_L} = -46 + 74 = 28, \qquad SS_{B_L} = \frac{784}{12} = 65.333333, \] \[ C_{B_Q} = 46 - 120 + 74 = 0, \qquad SS_{B_Q} = 0. \]

B is exactly linear: its three totals are equally spaced, \(46, 60, 74\), stepping by 14 each time.

\[ C_{A_QB_Q} = 10 - 28 + 18 - 32 + 88 - 52 + 20 - 48 + 30 = 6, \] \[ SS_{A_QB_Q} = \frac{6^{2}}{2 \times 6 \times 6} = \frac{36}{72} = 0.5. \]

Had \(A_Q\) been large, the reading would have been that A has an interior optimum, and the natural next step would be the response surface methods of Unit 4 rather than more factorial runs.

The split also answers a question the \(2\)-degree-of-freedom \(F\) for A cannot. \(F_A = (89.333333/2)/2 = 22.333333\) is significant, but that test spreads its power over both components. Testing \(A_L\) alone gives \(F = 42.666667\) on one degree of freedom — nearly twice as sensitive, because the test is aimed at the alternative that actually holds.

4. Interactions of Three Factors and More

WHAT TWO FACTORS DO NOT TEACH YOU

How to read a two-factor interaction — as the failure of the lines to be parallel, and as the rule that main effects must not then be reported alone — is already covered, with its plot. Example 2.1 checked it arithmetically on this data set: the effect of A was \(6.5\) at low B and \(10\) at high B, and half the difference is the \(1.75\) the contrast gave.

Three factors bring something that two cannot. \(ABC\) measures how the AB interaction itself changes between the levels of C — half the difference between \(AB\) computed at high C and \(AB\) computed at low C. It is a difference of differences of differences, and that is exactly why it is hard to explain to anyone and easy to mistrust.

Two practical consequences, and the whole of the next unit rests on them.

  • A large three-factor interaction usually means the wrong scale. An effect that is multiplicative on the original scale is additive on the log scale, and interactions of every order shrink or vanish under the transformation. Try that before trying to interpret the term.
  • High-order interactions are usually negligible — which is an empirical observation, not a theorem, and it is the assumption that makes confounding and fractional replication worth doing in Unit 3. At \(k = 4\), eleven of the fifteen effects involve three or four factors; if those eleven are noise, eleven of the fifteen degrees of freedom are available to be spent on something else.

Key Take-aways

  • The generating form \((a\pm1)(b\pm1)(c\pm1)\) — minus for a letter in the effect, plus for one not in it — writes down any contrast without reasoning about individual signs. At the desk: \(+\) for an even number of shared letters.
  • Every effect has the same variance, \(\sigma^{2}/(r2^{k-2})\), because every one is a contrast of all \(r2^{k}\) observations.
  • After Yates's algorithm, run both checks: the grand total in the first cell, and the final column reproducing \(\sum_i T_i^{2}/r - \text{CF}\).
  • In \(3^{2}\), each main effect splits into linear and quadratic components and the interaction into four, using \((-1,0,1)\) and \((1,-2,1)\); the components add back to the whole, which is the arithmetic check.
  • \(ABC\) is half the change in the \(AB\) interaction between the levels of C. A large one usually means the wrong scale; their general smallness is what the next unit spends.
