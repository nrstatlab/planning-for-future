    1. Life Tables

    DEFINITION

    Life tables, also called mortality tables, give the life history of a hypothetical group, or cohort, of people as it is gradually diminished by deaths. They give the essential facts about the cohort at each age: the probabilities of living and of dying, and the number of survivors. A life table summarises the mortality experience of a population during a given period; it is constructed from census data and death-registration data.

    Notation and Terminology

    1. \(l_0\): the cohort or radix, the assumed number of births with which the table starts.
    2. \(l_x\): the number of persons living at exact age \(x\) out of the \(l_0\) born.
    3. \(d_x\): the number of the \(l_x\) persons reaching age \(x\) who die before reaching age \(x+1\); \(d_x = l_x - l_{x+1}\).
    4. \({}_{n}p_x\): the probability that a person aged \(x\) survives to age \(x+n\); \({}_{n}p_x = l_{x+n}/l_x\).
    5. \(p_x\): the probability that a person aged \(x\) survives one year; \(p_x = l_{x+1}/l_x\).
    6. \(q_x = 1 - p_x\): the probability that a person aged \(x\) dies within one year: \[ q_x = 1 - \frac{l_{x+1}}{l_x} = \frac{l_x - l_{x+1}}{l_x} = \frac{d_x}{l_x}. \]
    7. \(L_x\): the number of years lived in aggregate by the cohort between ages \(x\) and \(x+1\); \(L_x = \dfrac{l_x + l_{x+1}}{2}\) (see the note below).
    8. \(T_x\): the total number of years lived by the cohort after age \(x\); \(T_x = L_x + L_{x+1} + L_{x+2} + \cdots\).
    9. \(e^0_x\): the complete expectation of life at age \(x\), the average number of years a person of age \(x\) can expect to live under the prevailing mortality: \[ e^0_x = \frac{T_x}{l_x} = \frac{L_x + L_{x+1} + \cdots}{l_x}, \] or, approximately, \(e^0_x = e_x + \tfrac12\) (Result 6 below).
    10. \(e_x\): the curtate expectation of life, the average number of complete years lived after age \(x\) by each of the \(l_x\) persons: \(e_x = \sum_{n \ge 1} l_{x+n}/l_x\).
    NOTE — WHY \(L_x = (l_x + l_{x+1})/2\)

    Assume the \(d_x\) deaths are spread uniformly over the year of age. Then for \(0 \le t \le 1\), \(l_{x+t} = l_x - t\,d_x\), and

    \[ \begin{aligned} L_x &= \int_0^1 l_{x+t}\,dt = \int_0^1 (l_x - t\,d_x)\,dt \\ &= l_x\Big[t\Big]_0^1 - d_x\Big[\frac{t^2}{2}\Big]_0^1 = l_x - \frac{d_x}{2} \\ &= l_x - \frac{l_x - l_{x+1}}{2} = \frac{l_x + l_{x+1}}{2}. \end{aligned} \]

    Observe also that \(l_{x+\frac12} = l_x - \tfrac12 d_x = L_x\): the years lived in the year of age equal the number alive at its middle.

    Columns of a Life Table

    ColumnSymbolDescription
    Age\(x\)Exact age
    Survivors\(l_x\)Number alive at exact age \(x\) (starts at \(l_0\), the radix, usually 100 000)
    Deaths\(d_x\)Deaths between ages \(x\) and \(x+1\); \(d_x = l_x - l_{x+1}\)
    Probability of dying\(q_x\)\(q_x = d_x / l_x\)
    Probability of surviving\(p_x\)\(p_x = l_{x+1}/l_x = 1 - q_x\)
    Person-years lived\(L_x\)Total person-years lived by the cohort between ages \(x\) and \(x+1\): \(L_x \approx (l_x + l_{x+1})/2\)
    Total person-years lived above x\(T_x\)\(T_x = \sum_{y \ge x} L_y\)
    Complete expectation of life at age x\(e^0_x\)\(e^0_x = T_x / l_x\)

    Proofs of Key Relations

    Each relation below is derived from the definitions in the table above, one step at a time.

    RELATION 1 — \(p_x + q_x = 1\)

    Consider a person exactly aged \(x\). Over the next year exactly one of two things happens: the person survives to age \(x+1\), or the person dies before age \(x+1\). These two events are mutually exclusive and together cover every possibility, so their probabilities must add to \(1\). Writing \(p_x\) for the survival probability and \(q_x\) for the death probability,

    \[ p_x + q_x = 1, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad p_x = 1 - q_x. \]
    RELATION 2 — \(d_x = l_x - l_{x+1}\)

    Of the \(l_x\) people alive at exact age \(x\), those still alive one year later number \(l_{x+1}\). The people who are no longer alive are precisely the deaths between ages \(x\) and \(x+1\), which is what \(d_x\) counts. Subtracting the survivors from the starting group,

    \[ d_x = l_x - l_{x+1}. \]
    RELATION 3 — \(l_{x+1} = l_x\,p_x = l_x(1-q_x)\)

    Step 1. By definition the survival probability is the fraction of the age-\(x\) group that reaches age \(x+1\): \(p_x = l_{x+1}/l_x\). Multiplying both sides by \(l_x\),

    \[ l_{x+1} = l_x\,p_x. \]

    Step 2. Substituting \(p_x = 1-q_x\) from Relation 1,

    \[ l_{x+1} = l_x(1-q_x). \]
    RELATION 4 — \(T_x = T_{x+1} + L_x\)

    By definition \(T_x = \sum_{y \ge x} L_y\) is the total person-years still to be lived by the group from age \(x\) onward. Split that sum by peeling off the first term:

    \[ T_x = \sum_{y \ge x} L_y = L_x + \sum_{y \ge x+1} L_y. \]

    The remaining sum is exactly \(T_{x+1}\), so

    \[ T_x = L_x + T_{x+1}. \]

    In words: the years lived from age \(x\) on = the years lived during the single year \([x, x+1]\) (that is \(L_x\)) plus all the years lived from age \(x+1\) on (that is \(T_{x+1}\)). This is why the \(T_x\) column is built up from the bottom of the table.

    RELATION 5 — \(e^0_x = T_x / l_x\)

    \(T_x\) is the total number of future years that will be lived, added up over all \(l_x\) people who are alive at age \(x\). The average number of future years per person is that total shared equally among the \(l_x\) survivors:

    \[ e^0_x = \frac{\text{total future person-years}}{\text{number of survivors}} = \frac{T_x}{l_x}, \]

    which is the (complete) expectation of life at age \(x\).

    CHECK (from Example 1 below)

    With \(l_{30}=95\,000,\ l_{31}=94\,500\): Relation 2 gives \(d_{30}=500\); then \(q_{30}=500/95\,000=0.00526\) and \(p_{30}=1-0.00526=0.99474\) (Relation 1); and Relation 3 recovers \(l_{31}=95\,000\times0.99474=94\,500\). ✓

    Results on Life-Table Functions

    RESULT 1 — \({}_{n}p_x = p_x\,p_{x+1}\cdots p_{x+n-1}\)

    Proof. Multiply and divide by the survivors at the intermediate ages:

    \[ \begin{aligned} {}_{n}p_x = \frac{l_{x+n}}{l_x} &= \frac{l_{x+1}}{l_x}\cdot\frac{l_{x+2}}{l_{x+1}}\cdots\frac{l_{x+n}}{l_{x+n-1}} \\ &= p_x\,p_{x+1}\cdots p_{x+n-1}. \end{aligned} \]
    RESULT 2 — \({}_{n-1|}q_x = d_{x+n-1}/l_x\)

    Let \({}_{n-1|}q_x\) be the probability that a person aged \(x\) dies in the \(n\)th year, that is, between ages \(x+n-1\) and \(x+n\).

    Proof. The person must survive \(n-1\) years and then, aged \(x+n-1\), die within a year:

    \[ \begin{aligned} {}_{n-1|}q_x &= {}_{n-1}p_x\cdot q_{x+n-1} \\ &= \frac{l_{x+n-1}}{l_x}\cdot\frac{d_{x+n-1}}{l_{x+n-1}} = \frac{d_{x+n-1}}{l_x}. \end{aligned} \]
    RESULT 3 — \(l_x = d_x + d_{x+1} + \cdots + d_{\omega-1}\)

    Let \(\omega\) be the last age in the table, so that \(l_\omega = 0\).

    Proof. Since \(d_y = l_y - l_{y+1}\), the sum telescopes:

    \[ \begin{aligned} &d_x + d_{x+1} + \cdots + d_{\omega-1} \\ &\quad= (l_x - l_{x+1}) + (l_{x+1} - l_{x+2}) \\ &\qquad + \cdots + (l_{\omega-1} - l_\omega) \\ &\quad= l_x - l_\omega = l_x. \end{aligned} \]
    RESULT 4 — \(T_x = \tfrac12 l_x + l_{x+1} + l_{x+2} + \cdots\)

    Proof. Put \(L_y = \tfrac12(l_y + l_{y+1})\) in \(T_x = L_x + L_{x+1} + L_{x+2} + \cdots\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} T_x &= \tfrac12(l_x + l_{x+1}) + \tfrac12(l_{x+1} + l_{x+2}) + \cdots \\ &= \tfrac12 l_x + l_{x+1} + l_{x+2} + \cdots \end{aligned} \]
    RESULT 5 — \(e_x = (l_{x+1} + l_{x+2} + \cdots)/l_x\)

    Proof. Of the \(l_x\) persons aged \(x\), the \(d_x\) who die in the first year live 0 complete years; the \(d_{x+1}\) who die in the second year live 1 complete year; in general the \(d_{x+i}\) who die in the \((i+1)\)th year live \(i\) complete years. So

    \[ \begin{aligned} e_x &= \frac{0\cdot d_x + 1\cdot d_{x+1} + 2\cdot d_{x+2} + \cdots}{l_x} \\ &= \frac{(l_{x+1} - l_{x+2}) + 2(l_{x+2} - l_{x+3}) + \cdots}{l_x} \\ &= \frac{l_{x+1} + l_{x+2} + l_{x+3} + \cdots}{l_x}, \end{aligned} \]

    since each \(l_{x+k}\) appears once with coefficient \(k\) and once with coefficient \(-(k-1)\).

    RESULT 6 — \(e^0_x = e_x + \tfrac12\)

    Proof. By Result 5 and then Result 4,

    \[ \begin{aligned} e_x + \tfrac12 &= \frac{l_{x+1} + l_{x+2} + \cdots}{l_x} + \frac12 \\ &= \frac{\tfrac12 l_x + l_{x+1} + l_{x+2} + \cdots}{l_x} \\ &= \frac{T_x}{l_x} = e^0_x. \end{aligned} \]

    Result 4 rests on \(L_x = \tfrac12(l_x + l_{x+1})\), so this holds exactly when deaths are uniform over each year of age and approximately otherwise.

    RESULT 7 — \(p_x = \dfrac{e_x}{1 + e_{x+1}}\)

    Proof. By Result 5,

    \[ e_x\,l_x = l_{x+1} + l_{x+2} + l_{x+3} + \cdots \qquad (1) \] \[ e_{x+1}\,l_{x+1} = l_{x+2} + l_{x+3} + \cdots \qquad (2) \]

    Subtracting (2) from (1), \(e_x l_x - e_{x+1} l_{x+1} = l_{x+1}\), so \(e_x l_x = (1 + e_{x+1})\,l_{x+1}\), and

    \[ p_x = \frac{l_{x+1}}{l_x} = \frac{e_x}{1 + e_{x+1}}. \]

    Central Mortality Rate and Force of Mortality

    CENTRAL MORTALITY RATE

    The central mortality rate at age \(x\) measures the risk of death of a person whose exact age is not known but lies between \(x\) and \(x+1\): the deaths in the year of age divided by the years lived in it,

    \[ m_x = \frac{d_x}{L_x}. \]

    It is a rate per year lived; the corresponding probability is \(q_x\).

    FORCE OF MORTALITY

    Treat \(l_x\) as a continuous function of \(x\). The rate at which \(l_x\) decreases at age \(x\) is

    \[ \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{l_x - l_{x+t}}{t} = -\lim_{t \to 0}\frac{l_{x+t} - l_x}{t} = -\frac{dl_x}{dx}. \]

    The force of mortality \(\mu_x\) is this instantaneous rate of decrease measured relative to \(l_x\):

    \[ \mu_x = -\frac{1}{l_x}\,\frac{dl_x}{dx} = -\frac{d}{dx}\,\log l_x. \]
    RESULT 1 — \(q_x = \dfrac{2m_x}{2 + m_x}\)

    Proof. Using \(L_x = l_x - \tfrac12 d_x\),

    \[ \begin{aligned} m_x = \frac{d_x}{L_x} &= \frac{d_x}{l_x - \tfrac12 d_x} = \frac{2d_x}{2l_x - d_x} \\ &= \frac{2\,d_x/l_x}{2 - d_x/l_x} = \frac{2q_x}{2 - q_x}. \end{aligned} \]

    Hence \(2m_x - m_x q_x = 2q_x\), so \(2m_x = q_x(2 + m_x)\), and

    \[ q_x = \frac{2m_x}{2 + m_x}. \]
    RESULT 2 — \(\mu_{x+\frac12} = m_x\) (approximately)

    Proof. \(L_x = \int_0^1 l_{x+t}\,dt\). Differentiate with respect to \(x\) under the integral sign; since \(l_{x+t}\) depends on \(x\) and \(t\) only through \(x+t\), \(\frac{d}{dx}\,l_{x+t} = \frac{d}{dt}\,l_{x+t}\), and

    \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{dL_x}{dx} &= \int_0^1 \frac{d}{dx}\,l_{x+t}\,dt = \int_0^1 \frac{d}{dt}\,l_{x+t}\,dt \\ &= \Big[l_{x+t}\Big]_0^1 = l_{x+1} - l_x = -d_x. \end{aligned} \]

    Dividing by \(L_x\),

    \[ m_x = \frac{d_x}{L_x} = -\frac{1}{L_x}\,\frac{dL_x}{dx}. \]

    Replacing \(L_x\) by \(l_{x+\frac12}\) (its value under uniform deaths, from the note above),

    \[ m_x \approx -\frac{1}{l_{x+\frac12}}\,\frac{d}{dx}\,l_{x+\frac12} = \mu_{x+\frac12}. \]

    When deaths are uniform over the year of age the two are equal exactly: \(\mu_{x+\frac12} = d_x/l_{x+\frac12} = d_x/L_x = m_x\).

    Construction of a Life Table

    The construction is described under three heads: (1) assumptions, (2) description and (3) construction.

    1. Assumptions.

    1. The cohort is closed: there is no emigration or immigration.
    2. Individuals die at each age according to a predetermined schedule, which is fixed and does not change.
    3. The cohort starts from a standard number of births, such as 10 000 or 100 000, called the radix.
    4. Deaths are uniformly distributed over each year of age, from \(x\) to \(x+1\) (birthday to birthday).

    2. Description. A complete life table has eleven columns, related as follows.

    ColumnSymbolRelation
    1\(x\)age: \(0, 1, 2, \ldots\)
    2\(l_x\)persons living at exact age \(x\)
    3\(d_x\)\(d_x = l_x - l_{x+1}\)
    4\(q_x\)\(q_x = d_x/l_x\)
    5\(p_x\)\(p_x = l_{x+1}/l_x = 1 - q_x\)
    6\(m_x\)\(m_x = 2q_x/(2 - q_x)\)
    7\(\mu_x\)\(\mu_{x+\frac12} = m_x\)
    8\(L_x\)\(L_x = (l_x + l_{x+1})/2 = l_x - d_x/2\)
    9\(T_x\)\(T_x = L_x + L_{x+1} + \cdots\), or \(T_{x+1} = T_x - L_x\)
    10\(e^0_x\)\(e^0_x = T_x/l_x\)
    11\(e_x\)\(e_x = e^0_x - \tfrac12\)

    3. Construction. The first figure needed is the radix \(l_0\). A complete life table can then be built from the single column \(q_x\) \((x = 0, 1, 2, \ldots)\), which is therefore called the pivotal column:

    1. \(d_0 = l_0\,q_0\) and \(l_1 = l_0 - d_0\); then \(d_1 = l_1\,q_1\) and \(l_2 = l_1 - d_1\); and so on down the table.
    2. \(L_x = (l_x + l_{x+1})/2\) for each \(x\).
    3. \(T_x = L_x + L_{x+1} + \cdots\), accumulated from the oldest age upwards.
    4. \(e^0_x = T_x/l_x\), and \(p_x\), \(m_x\), \(e_x\) from the relations in the table.

    When the observed data are central death rates \(m_x\) (as rates per person, not per 1 000), the pivotal column comes from \(q_x = 2m_x/(2 + m_x)\).

    Uses of Life Tables

    1. To describe the course of life in a population: survival and death at each age.
    2. To fix premium rates in life insurance.
    3. To find the growth rate of a population (through the net reproduction rate, Section 3).
    4. To project the population by age and sex.
    5. To compare the mortality of different groups of people.
    6. To find the expectation of life at each age.
    7. To work out retirement benefits in government and private service.
    8. To estimate the size of the future labour force.
    9. In demographic and public-health studies.
    EXAMPLE 1 (Partial life table)

    For ages 30 to 32: \(l_{30} = 95\,000,\; l_{31} = 94\,500,\; l_{32} = 94\,000\).

    \(d_{30} = 500\), \(q_{30} = 500/95\,000 = 0.00526\), \(p_{30} = 0.99474\).

    \(L_{30} = (95\,000 + 94\,500)/2 = 94\,750\).

    EXAMPLE 2 (Life expectancy)

    If \(T_{30} = 4\,500\,000\) and \(l_{30} = 95\,000\), then \(e^0_{30} = 4\,500\,000 / 95\,000 = 47.37\) years (expected additional years of life for a 30-year-old).

    Abridged Life Tables

    DEFINITION

    A complete life table gives its functions for every single year of age. An abridged life table gives them either (i) at integral ages some distance apart, usually 5 or 10 years, or (ii) for age groups of width 5 or 10 years.

    Its columns, for the age interval \(x\) to \(x+n\):

    1. \(x\) to \(x+n\): the interval between two exact ages.
    2. \(l_x\): the number of persons living at the beginning of the interval, out of \(l_0\) born.
    3. \({}_{n}q_x\): the probability of dying between ages \(x\) and \(x+n\); \({}_{n}q_x = 1 - {}_{n}p_x = 1 - l_{x+n}/l_x\).
    4. \({}_{n}d_x\): the number of deaths in the interval; since \({}_{n}q_x = {}_{n}d_x/l_x\), \({}_{n}d_x = l_x\,{}_{n}q_x\).
    5. \({}_{n}L_x\): the stationary population of the age group \(x\) to \(x+n\), the years lived in it; \({}_{n}L_x = \int_0^n l_{x+t}\,dt\).
    6. \(T_x\): the number of years lived after age \(x\); \(T_x = \int_0^\infty l_{x+t}\,dt\).
    7. \(e^0_x = T_x/l_x\).
    8. \({}_{n}m_x\): the age-specific (central) mortality rate. In the same way as Result 1 above, \({}_{n}q_x = \dfrac{2n\,{}_{n}m_x}{2 + n\,{}_{n}m_x}\), so \({}_{n}m_x = \dfrac{2\,{}_{n}q_x}{2n - n\,{}_{n}q_x}\).

    Methods of constructing abridged life tables include (1) Reed and Merrell's method, (2) Greville's method and (3) King's method.

    2. Measures of Fertility

    Fertility is the actual production of children, and through it a population grows. It is measured by (1) the crude birth rate, (2) the general fertility rate, (3) specific fertility rates, (4) age-specific fertility rates and (5) the total fertility rate.

    2.1 Crude Birth Rate (CBR)

    \[ \text{CBR} \;=\; \dfrac{B}{P} \times 1000, \]

    where \(B\) = live births in the year, \(P\) = mid-year population.

    The crude birth rate is the simplest measure of fertility: it relates the live births to the total population. If \(B^t\) is the number of live births in a region during the period \(t\) and \(P^t\) its population, \(\text{CBR} = \dfrac{B^t}{P^t}\times k\) with \(k = 1000\).

    Merits.

    1. It is simple, easy to compute and easily understood.
    2. It is based on the total population, so no details of its sections are needed.

    Demerits.

    1. It ignores the age and sex distribution of the population.
    2. It is not a probability ratio: the whole population is not exposed to the risk of giving birth, only the women in the child-bearing ages 15–49.
    3. It is not suitable for comparing the fertility of different countries or regions.
    4. It treats the whole population alike, as if women of every age were equally fertile.

    Note. The crude birth rate usually lies between 10 and 55 per thousand.

    2.2 General Fertility Rate (GFR)

    \[ \text{GFR} \;=\; \dfrac{B}{P^{\text{f}}_{15-49}} \times 1000, \]

    where the denominator is the mid-year female population aged 15–49 (reproductive ages).

    In general form,

    \[ \text{GFR} = \frac{B^t}{\sum_{x=\lambda_1}^{\lambda_2} {}^{f}P_x}\times k, \]

    where \({}^{f}P_x\) is the female population aged \(x\), \(\lambda_1 = 15\) and \(\lambda_2 = 49\) are the limits of the reproductive ages, and \(k = 1000\).

    Merits.

    1. Its denominator is the women exposed to the risk of child-bearing, so it is much closer to a probability ratio than the crude birth rate.
    2. It takes account of the sex distribution, and of the age distribution to some extent.
    3. It shows how far the women of reproductive age add to the population through live births.

    Demerit. It is not suitable for comparisons between periods or regions, because it overlooks the age composition of the women within the ages 15–49.

    Specific Fertility Rate (SFR)

    Fertility is affected by factors such as age, marriage, migration and region. The fertility rate computed for a section of the women defined by such a factor is a specific fertility rate:

    \[ \text{SFR} = \frac{\text{births to women of the section}}{\text{female population of the section}}\times k, \qquad k = 1000. \]

    2.3 Age-Specific Fertility Rate (ASFR)

    \[ \text{ASFR}_x \;=\; \dfrac{B_x}{P^{\text{f}}_x} \times 1000, \]

    where \(B_x\) = births to women in age group \(x\), \(P^{\text{f}}_x\) = mid-year female population in age group \(x\).

    Age-specific fertility rates are the specific fertility rates for the age groups within the reproductive ages. With \({}_{n}B_x\) the births in the period to women aged \(x\) to \(x+n\) (at least \(x\) and under \(x+n\)) and \({}^{f}_{\,n}P_x\) the average female population of that age group,

    \[ {}_{n}i_x = \frac{{}_{n}B_x}{{}^{f}_{\,n}P_x}\times k; \qquad\text{for } n = 1,\quad i_x = \frac{B_x}{{}^{f}P_x}\times k. \]

    Merits.

    1. Its denominator is the women of that age, the ones exposed to the risk, so it is close to a probability ratio.
    2. It takes account of the age composition of the women, and is suitable for comparing fertility between age groups.

    Demerit. Comparing two regions, or one region at two times, is not easy with a whole set of rates: the rate may be higher in some age groups in one region and lower in others.

    2.4 Total Fertility Rate (TFR)

    \[ \text{TFR} \;=\; \frac{n}{1000}\sum_{x=15}^{49} \text{ASFR}_x, \]

    where the ASFRs are per 1 000 women and \(n\) is the width of the age group (5 if data grouped in 5-year bands). Equivalent: TFR = average number of children a woman would have, surviving the full reproductive span, with current ASFRs.

    The total fertility rate combines the age-specific fertility rates into a single figure:

    \[ \text{TFR} = \sum_{x=\lambda_1}^{\lambda_2} i_x = \sum_{x=\lambda_1}^{\lambda_2}\frac{B_x}{{}^{f}P_x}\times k, \]

    where \(i_x\) is the annual age-specific fertility rate for ages \((x,\,x+1)\), \(B_x\) the births to women of that age, \({}^{f}P_x\) the female population of that age, and \(\lambda_1 = 15\), \(\lambda_2 = 49\).

    Note. For age groups of width \(n\), \(\text{TFR} = \sum n\,({}_{n}i_x)\); for quinquennial groups, \(\text{TFR} = 5\sum {}_{5}i_x\). With \(k = 1000\) the TFR is per thousand women; divided by 1000 it is the number of children per woman, as in the formula above.

    Replacement-level TFR ≈ 2.1 in low-mortality settings.

    EXAMPLE 1

    City population 1 million; live births 18 000 in the year. CBR = 18 per 1 000.

    Female population 15–49 = 250 000. GFR = 18 000/250 000 × 1 000 = 72 per 1 000 women.

    EXAMPLE 2 (TFR)

    Suppose ASFRs (per 1 000) by 5-year age group are:

    Age15–1920–2425–2930–3435–3940–4445–49
    ASFR401201408040102

    Sum = 432. TFR = 432 × 5 / 1 000 = 2.16 children per woman — close to replacement.

    3. Measures of Population Growth

    To judge whether a population is increasing, decreasing or stationary, fertility rates alone are not enough, because (1) they ignore the sex of the newborn, though only the girls born become the mothers of the next generation, and (2) they ignore the mortality of the newborn: some of the girls born die before reaching the child-bearing ages. Growth is measured by (1) the crude rate of natural increase, (2) Pearl's vital index, (3) the gross reproduction rate and (4) the net reproduction rate.

    Crude Rate of Natural Increase

    \[ \text{Crude rate of natural increase} = \text{CBR} - \text{CDR} \quad\text{(per thousand)}. \]

    It gives the net increase (or, if negative, the decrease) of the population through births and deaths during the period, per thousand of the population.

    3.1 Pearl's Vital Index

    DEFINITION

    Pearl's vital index measures the ratio of births to deaths:

    \[ \text{Vital Index} \;=\; \dfrac{B}{D} \times 100. \]

    If > 100, population is growing; if < 100, declining.

    The index is named after Raymond Pearl. Since \(\text{CBR}/\text{CDR} = (B/P)/(D/P) = B/D\), it is also \(\dfrac{\text{CBR}}{\text{CDR}}\times 100\).

    NOTES
    1. The crude rate of natural increase and the vital index are both simple to compute.
    2. A vital index above 100 means that births exceed deaths, so the population is increasing; below 100, the population is not holding its own.
    3. Neither shows the trend of growth.
    4. Both suffer from the drawbacks of the CBR and the CDR, and are not suitable for comparisons.
    EXAMPLE

    Births = 18 000, Deaths = 8 000. Vital Index = 18 000/8 000 × 100 = 225 → population growing rapidly.

    3.2 Gross Reproduction Rate (GRR)

    DEFINITION

    GRR is the average number of female babies that a cohort of women would bear during their reproductive lifetime if they experienced current ASFRs.

    \[ \text{GRR} \;=\; \frac{n}{1000}\sum_{x} \text{ASFR}_x \cdot f, \]

    where \(f\) is the proportion of female births (≈ 0.487 worldwide), \(n\) is the age-group width.

    Often computed simply as: \(\text{GRR} \approx \text{TFR} \times f\).

    GRR from Female Births: Formula, Notes, Merits

    Only the female births are potential mothers, so the gross reproduction rate is the sum, over each year of the reproductive period, of the age-specific fertility rates computed from female births:

    \[ \text{GRR} = \sum_{x=\lambda_1}^{\lambda_2} \frac{{}^{f}B_x}{{}^{f}P_x}\times k = \sum_{x=\lambda_1}^{\lambda_2} {}^{f}i_x, \]

    where \({}^{f}B_x\) is the number of female births to women aged \(x\), \({}^{f}P_x\) the female population aged \(x\) to \(x+1\), \(\lambda_1 = 15\) and \(\lambda_2 = 49\). For age groups of width \(n\),

    \[ \text{GRR} = \sum n\,({}^{f}_{\,n}i_x) = \sum n\,\frac{{}^{f}_{\,n}B_x}{{}^{f}_{\,n}P_x}\times k, \]

    where \({}^{f}_{\,n}i_x\) is the age-specific fertility rate from female births in \((x,\,x+n)\); for quinquennial groups \(\text{GRR} = 5\sum {}^{f}_{\,5}i_x\).

    NOTES
    1. \(\text{GRR} = \dfrac{\text{female births}}{\text{total births}}\times\text{TFR}\).
    2. The GRR is a number of daughters per woman (per thousand women when \(k = 1000\)), not a rate per year; in practice it lies between 0 and about 5.

    Merits.

    1. It is useful for comparing fertility in different regions and periods.
    2. It shows the rate at which mothers would be replaced by daughters if none of the daughters died before the end of the reproductive ages.

    Demerit. It ignores the fact that some of the female babies die before reaching the reproductive ages.

    3.3 Net Reproduction Rate (NRR)

    DEFINITION

    NRR allows for mortality of women before they complete their reproductive years. It is the average number of daughters that a woman would have, allowing for the chance she may die before age 49.

    \[ \text{NRR} \;=\; \frac{n}{1000}\sum_{x} \text{ASFR}_x \cdot f \cdot \dfrac{L_x}{l_0}, \]

    where \(L_x/l_0\) is the life-table person-years per year of age in the group divided by the radix — in effect the chance of surviving to the middle of the age group.

    NRR from a Female Life Table

    The net reproduction rate is the GRR adjusted for mortality. In Benjamin's description, it measures the extent to which mothers produce female infants who survive to replace them: how far a generation of girl babies survives to reproduce itself as it passes through the child-bearing ages.

    Step 1. From the female age-specific death rates \({}^{f}m_x\), construct a female life table. In it, \({}^{f}_{\,n}L_x\) is the number of years lived in the age group \((x,\,x+n)\) by a cohort of \({}^{f}l_0\) girls born.

    Step 2. The survival rate of the age group is

    \[ {}_{n}\pi_x = \frac{{}^{f}_{\,n}L_x}{n\;{}^{f}l_0}, \]

    the average proportion of the girls born who are alive in the age group \((x,\,x+n)\).

    Step 3. Out of \(k\) newborn girls, about \(k\,{}_{n}\pi_x\) are alive in the age group \((x,\,x+n)\), \(k\,{}_{n}\pi_{x+n}\) in \((x+n,\,x+2n)\), and so on. Multiplying each female age-specific fertility rate by the corresponding survival rate and adding,

    \[ \text{NRR} = k\sum n\,\frac{{}^{f}_{\,n}B_x}{{}^{f}_{\,n}P_x}\;{}_{n}\pi_x = \sum n\;{}^{f}_{\,n}i_x\;{}_{n}\pi_x. \]
    NOTES
    1. Usually \(\text{NRR} \le \text{GRR}\); they are equal only if all the girls born survive to the end of the reproductive ages.
    2. Like the GRR, the NRR is a number of daughters per woman, not a rate per year; in practice it lies between 0 and about 5.
    3. Even the NRR takes no account of non-marriage and widowhood.
    4. \(\text{NRR} = 1\) means that a generation of newborn girls exactly replaces itself in the next generation.
    5. The NRR is used in population projections.

    Interpretation

    • NRR = 1 ⇒ population just replacing itself.
    • NRR > 1 ⇒ population growing.
    • NRR < 1 ⇒ population declining.
    • NRR ≤ GRR (mortality reduces NRR).
    EXAMPLE 1 (GRR)

    From Example 2 of fertility: TFR = 2.16. Female-births fraction = 0.487. GRR = 2.16 × 0.487 = 1.052 daughters per woman.

    EXAMPLE 2 (NRR)

    Continuing: suppose the average survival probability from birth to mid-reproductive age is 0.92.

    NRR = GRR × 0.92 = 1.052 × 0.92 ≈ 0.968. Slightly below 1 → population just below replacement; long-term decline expected.

    Key Take-aways

    • Life table summarises mortality experience; columns: \(l_x, d_x, q_x, p_x, m_x, \mu_x, L_x, T_x, e^0_x, e_x\); the whole table can be built from the radix and the pivotal column \(q_x\).
    • Complete expectation of life \(e^0_x = T_x / l_x\); curtate \(e_x = \sum_{n\ge1} l_{x+n}/l_x\); \(e^0_x \approx e_x + \tfrac12\); \(p_x = e_x/(1 + e_{x+1})\); \({}_{n}p_x = p_x p_{x+1}\cdots p_{x+n-1}\).
    • Central mortality rate \(m_x = d_x/L_x\); \(q_x = 2m_x/(2 + m_x)\); force of mortality \(\mu_x = -\frac{1}{l_x}\frac{dl_x}{dx}\), with \(\mu_{x+\frac12} \approx m_x\).
    • Fertility measures, from crudest to most refined: CBR (whole population in the denominator); GFR (women aged 15–49 only); ASFR (one age group at a time, most detailed); TFR (the ASFRs summed into one figure).
    • Population growth: Pearl's Vital Index = B/D × 100; GRR (daughters only); NRR includes mortality.
    • NRR \(= \sum n\,{}^{f}_{\,n}i_x\,{}_{n}\pi_x\) with survival rates \({}_{n}\pi_x = {}_{n}L_x/(n\,l_0)\); NRR = 1 ↔ replacement-level fertility.
