    Unit 4 — Vital Statistics & Measures of Mortality

    Definition, sources and uses of vital statistics; measures of mortality — Crude Death Rate, Specific Death Rate, Standardised Death Rate (direct & indirect methods, with the adjustment factor); merits and demerits of each, and worked problems.

    1. Definition of Vital Statistics

    DEFINITION

    Vital statistics are the data and methods used to study the dynamic phenomena of human population such as births, deaths, marriages, divorces, fertility, mortality and migration.

    Spiegelman: "The branch of biometry which deals with the data and the laws of human mortality, morbidity and demography."

    The science behind it is demography.

    Uses of Vital Statistics

    1. Public health policy — identify hot-spots of mortality, plan hospitals.
    2. Population projections for socio-economic planning.
    3. Insurance and pension fund actuarial calculations.
    4. Measure success of welfare schemes (immunisation, sanitation).
    5. Estimate life expectancy, fertility patterns, dependency ratios.
    6. Compare standards of living across regions or countries.

    2. Sources of Vital Statistics

    1. Registration method (Civil Registration System, CRS) — births & deaths registered with the local registrar. Most accurate when comprehensive (e.g., UK, USA). India's Registration of Births and Deaths Act, 1969 mandates registration.
    2. Census method — decennial population enumeration; provides snapshot data on demographic characteristics.
    3. Sample surveys — NSSO & SRS (Sample Registration System, India) provide periodic vital rate estimates from large samples.
    4. Hospital records — partial coverage; suffer selection bias.
    5. Analytical / indirect methods — when direct data are missing, estimates from related indicators.

    In India the primary source for vital rates is the Sample Registration System (SRS) run by the Office of the Registrar General & Census Commissioner.

    3. Measures of Mortality

    3.1 Crude Death Rate (CDR)

    DEFINITION

    The number of deaths per 1 000 population in a given calendar year.

    \[ \text{CDR} \;=\; \dfrac{D}{P} \times 1000, \]

    where \(D\) = total deaths in the year, \(P\) = mid-year population.

    Merits: easy to compute, single number, comparable across regions.
    Demerits: ignores age structure — populations with many elderly will show high CDR even if mortality at each age is low.

    EXAMPLE 1

    City A had 12 500 deaths in a year; mid-year population was 1 250 000. CDR = (12 500/1 250 000) × 1000 = 10 per 1 000.

    EXAMPLE 2

    A crude death rate mixes mortality with age structure. A country with a young population can have a lower CDR than a country with an old one, even when its people die younger at every age. This is why CDRs of different populations are compared only after standardisation (Section 3.3).

    The Crude Death Rate in Detail

    Mortality is measured by four kinds of rate: (1) the crude death rate, (2) specific death rates, (3) age-specific death rates and (4) standardised death rates. The crude death rate is the simplest of them.

    DEFINITION

    The crude death rate is the number of deaths from all causes per \(k\) persons of the population of a region during a given period. The annual crude death rate is

    \[ m = \frac{\text{annual deaths}}{\text{annual mean population}} \times k, \qquad k = 1000. \]

    Merits.

    1. It is simple and easy to compute.
    2. It serves as an index of the demographic and health problems of a region.
    3. It has wide applications in vital statistics.
    4. Divided by \(k\), it is approximately the probability that a person of the given population dies during the given period (approximately, because its denominator is the mean population of the period, not the number alive at its start).

    Demerits.

    1. It ignores the age and sex distribution of the population, although mortality differs from age to age: the risk of death is higher in old age, for example.
    2. It is not suitable for comparing the mortality of two places, or of one place at two periods, unless (i) the populations have more or less the same age and sex distribution and (ii) the periods are not too far apart.
    CDR BY SEX \[ \text{CDR (males)} = \frac{{}^{m}D}{{}^{m}P}\times 1000, \] \[ \text{CDR (females)} = \frac{{}^{f}D}{{}^{f}P}\times 1000, \]

    where \({}^{m}D,\ {}^{f}D\) are the male and female deaths and \({}^{m}P,\ {}^{f}P\) the male and female populations.

    NOTES
    1. The crude death rate usually lies between 8 and 30 per thousand.
    2. The crude death rate of females is generally lower than that of males.

    3.2 Specific Death Rate (SDR)

    DEFINITION

    The death rate for a specific category — age, sex, cause of death, occupation, etc. Most common is the Age-Specific Death Rate (ASDR).

    \[ \text{ASDR}_x \;=\; \dfrac{D_x}{P_x} \times 1000, \]

    where \(D_x\) = deaths in age group \(x\), \(P_x\) = mid-year population of age group \(x\).

    Other specific rates: sex-specific death rate, cause-specific death rate, infant mortality rate, etc.

    EXAMPLE 1 (Age-specific)

    In age group 50–59: deaths 800, population 80 000. ASDR = (800/80 000) × 1000 = 10 per 1 000.

    EXAMPLE 2 (Infant mortality rate, IMR)

    Infant deaths = 4 500 in a year; live births = 150 000. IMR = (4 500/150 000) × 1000 = 30 per 1 000 live births.

    Specific and Age-Specific Death Rates in Detail

    The crude death rate is unsuitable when the pattern of mortality differs from one section of the population to another. Mortality depends on factors such as age, sex, occupation and social status. In studies of child health, for instance, separate rates are needed for children under 1, aged 1–4 and aged 5–9.

    DEFINITION

    The specific death rate of a section of the population, for a given period, is

    \[ \text{SDR} = \frac{\text{deaths in the section}}{\text{population of the section}}\times 1000. \]

    Specific death rates are computed particularly for age and sex; they are useful in the construction of life tables.

    AGE-SPECIFIC DEATH RATE

    Let \({}_{n}D_x\) be the number of deaths in the age group \((x,\,x+n)\) and \({}_{n}P_x\) the population of that age group. Then

    \[ {}_{n}m_x = \frac{{}_{n}D_x}{{}_{n}P_x}\times 1000. \]

    For males and for females separately,

    \[ {}^{m}_{\,n}m_x = \frac{{}^{m}_{\,n}D_x}{{}^{m}_{\,n}P_x}\times 1000, \] \[ {}^{f}_{\,n}m_x = \frac{{}^{f}_{\,n}D_x}{{}^{f}_{\,n}P_x}\times 1000. \]

    Merits.

    1. Age- and sex-specific death rates overcome the drawback of the crude death rate.
    2. They are suitable for comparing the relative mortality of a section of the population in different regions.
    3. They are needed for the net reproduction rate and for the construction of life tables.

    Demerits.

    1. They are not useful for an overall comparison of mortality in two regions: the rate may be higher for one age group in one region and for another age group in the other.
    2. Age- and sex-specific rates still ignore social, occupational and topographical factors.

    3.3 Standardised Death Rate (SDRstd)

    PURPOSE

    Used to compare mortality across populations with different age structures. Two methods: Direct and Indirect.

    (a) Direct Method

    Apply the ASDRs of the population being studied to a standard population:

    \[ \text{SDR}_{\text{direct}} \;=\; \dfrac{\sum P_x^{\text{std}} \cdot m_x}{\sum P_x^{\text{std}}}, \]

    where \(P_x^{\text{std}}\) = standard-population age distribution, \(m_x\) = ASDR of the study population for age group \(x\).

    The result is what the death rate would have been if the study population had the same age structure as the standard.

    (b) Indirect Method

    Used when age-specific death rates of the study population are not available, but its age distribution and total deaths are.

    \[ \text{Expected deaths} \;=\; \sum P_x \cdot M_x^{\text{std}}, \]

    where \(M_x^{\text{std}}\) = ASDRs of the standard population, \(P_x\) = age structure of the study population.

    \[ \text{SMR} \;=\; \dfrac{\text{Actual deaths}}{\text{Expected deaths}}, \qquad \text{SDR}_{\text{indirect}} \;=\; \text{SMR} \times \text{CDR}^{\text{std}}. \]

    SMR = Standardised Mortality Ratio. If SMR > 1, study population has higher mortality than standard.

    Crude Rates as Weighted Means of Age-Specific Rates

    Let two regions \(A\) and \(B\) have populations \(P_x^a,\ P_x^b\), deaths \(D_x^a,\ D_x^b\) and age-specific death rates \(m_x^a,\ m_x^b\) in the age group \((x,\,x+1)\); the argument is the same for any age groups. Their crude death rates are

    \[ m^a = \frac{D^a}{P^a}\times 1000, \qquad m^b = \frac{D^b}{P^b}\times 1000. \]

    Step 1. Since \(m_x^a = \dfrac{D_x^a}{P_x^a}\times 1000\), the deaths in the age group satisfy \(1000\,D_x^a = m_x^a P_x^a\).

    Step 2. Adding over the age groups, \(1000\,D^a = 1000\sum D_x^a = \sum m_x^a P_x^a\).

    Step 3. Dividing by \(P^a = \sum P_x^a\),

    \[ m^a = \frac{\sum m_x^a P_x^a}{\sum P_x^a}, \]

    and in the same way

    \[ m^b = \frac{\sum m_x^b P_x^b}{\sum P_x^b}. \]

    So each crude death rate is a weighted arithmetic mean of the age-specific death rates, the weights being the populations of the age groups. Even if the age-specific rates are equal, \(m_x^a = m_x^b\) for every \(x\), the age distributions are in general not the same, \(P_x^a/\sum P_x^a \ne P_x^b/\sum P_x^b\), and so the crude rates differ: \(m^a \ne m^b\).

    This drawback is removed by using the same set of weights for both regions. The rates so obtained are called standardised (or adjusted) death rates. There are two methods: (1) the direct method and (2) the indirect method.

    Direct Method: Formula, Merits and Demerits

    In the direct method the standardised death rate of a region is the weighted arithmetic mean of its age-specific death rates, the weights being the age-group populations of another region chosen as the standard population. With \(P_x^S\) the number of persons in age group \(x\) of the standard population,

    \[ (\text{STDR})_A = \frac{\sum m_x^a P_x^S}{\sum P_x^S}, \] \[ (\text{STDR})_B = \frac{\sum m_x^b P_x^S}{\sum P_x^S}. \]

    Merits.

    1. It is simple and easy to compute.
    2. The age-adjusted rates are comparable, since they eliminate the difference caused by the different age distributions of \(A\) and \(B\).

    Demerit. The result depends on the choice of the standard population: another standard changes the adjusted rates and may change their relative positions.

    Indirect Method: the Adjustment Factor

    The direct method needs the population and the age-specific death rate of every age group. Sometimes the age-specific death rates of a region are not known, though its total deaths, and so its crude death rate, are. The indirect method is then used: the crude death rate of region \(A\) is multiplied by an adjustment factor \(C\), which allows for the relative mortality proneness of its age distribution, so that

    \[ \text{CDR}_A \times C = (\text{STDR})_A. \]

    Step 1. Writing both rates as weighted means,

    \[ \frac{\sum m_x^a P_x^a}{\sum P_x^a}\; C = \frac{\sum m_x^a P_x^S}{\sum P_x^S}, \] \[ C = \frac{\sum m_x^a P_x^S \big/ \sum P_x^S}{\sum m_x^a P_x^a \big/ \sum P_x^a}. \]

    Step 2. The rates \(m_x^a\) are usually not known, so \(C\) is approximated by putting the standard rates \(m_x^S\) in their place:

    \[ \hat C = \frac{\sum m_x^S P_x^S \big/ \sum P_x^S}{\sum m_x^S P_x^a \big/ \sum P_x^a}, \] \[ (\text{STDR})_A = \text{CDR}_A \times \hat C. \]

    The numerator of \(\hat C\) is the crude death rate \(\text{CDR}_S\) of the standard population; the denominator is the death rate region \(A\) would have, with its own age distribution, at the standard age-specific rates (the index death rate).

    Step 3 (the link with the SMR). At the standard rates, region \(A\) would expect \(E = \sum m_x^S P_x^a/1000\) deaths, so the index death rate is \(1000\,E/P^a\). Since \(\text{CDR}_A = 1000\,D^a/P^a\),

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{CDR}_A \times \hat C &= \frac{1000\,D^a}{P^a}\times\frac{\text{CDR}_S}{1000\,E/P^a} \\ &= \frac{D^a}{E}\,\text{CDR}_S = \text{SMR}\times\text{CDR}_S, \end{aligned} \]

    the indirect standardised rate of Section 3.3(b).

    NOTE

    The direct and indirect methods give the same standardised rate when the age-specific death rates of the given population are proportional to those of the standard population. If \(m_x^a = c\,m_x^S\) for every \(x\), the direct rate is \(c\sum m_x^S P_x^S/\sum P_x^S = c\,\text{CDR}_S\), and the indirect rate is \(\big(c\sum m_x^S P_x^a/\sum P_x^a\big)\,\hat C = c\,\text{CDR}_S\) as well.

    Worked Examples

    EXAMPLE 1 (Direct method)

    Compute standardised death rate of City X using the standard population below:

    Age groupCity X population (Px)City X deaths (Dx)ASDR mx per 1000Std. pop. (Pxstd)
    0–1420 000100530 000
    15–5950 000250555 000
    60+30 0009003015 000
    Total100 0001 250—100 000

    City X CDR = 1 250/100 000 × 1 000 = 12.5 per 1 000.

    Direct standardised = (30 000 × 5 + 55 000 × 5 + 15 000 × 30)/100 000 = (150 000 + 275 000 + 450 000)/100 000 = 875 000/100 000 = 8.75 per 1 000.

    Conclusion: After removing the effect of age structure, City X's "true" mortality is 8.75 — lower than its crude rate of 12.5.

    EXAMPLE 2 (Indirect method)

    City Y total deaths 800. Age distribution: 0–14 = 25 000, 15–59 = 60 000, 60+ = 15 000. Standard ASDRs (per 1 000): 4, 6, 25.

    Expected deaths = 25 000(0.004) + 60 000(0.006) + 15 000(0.025) = 100 + 360 + 375 = 835.

    SMR = 800/835 = 0.958.

    If standard CDR = 8 per 1 000, indirect SDR = 0.958 × 8 = 7.66 per 1 000.

    Age group\(P_x^a\)\(m_x^a\)\(m_x^a P_x^a\)\(m_x^b\)\(m_x^b P_x^a\)

    Crude death rates: \(\text{CDR}_A = \dfrac{150}{10\,000}\times 1000 = 15\) and \(\text{CDR}_B = \dfrac{120}{8\,000}\times 1000 = 15\) per thousand.

    Standardised death rates: \((\text{STDR})_A = \dfrac{150\,000}{10\,000} = 15\) per thousand, the same as its crude rate, as it must be for the standard population itself; \((\text{STDR})_B = \dfrac{118\,500}{10\,000} = 11.85\) per thousand.

    Conclusion. The crude rates are equal, yet \(B\) has the lower death rate in every age group. Its crude rate is raised by its older population: 25% of the people of \(B\) are aged 55 and above, against 10% of those of \(A\). Standardised, the rate of \(B\) is 11.85 against 15 for \(A\).

    PROBLEM 3 (Direct and indirect methods)

    Find the crude death rate of the local population below, and its standardised death rate by the direct and the indirect methods.

    Age groupStandard: populationStandard: deathsLocal: populationLocal: deaths
    0–108002060021
    10–201 20061 4007
    20–602 500252 20022
    60–1005003080040
    Total5 000815 00090

    Solution. Age-specific death rates per thousand: \(m_x^S\) for the standard and \(m_x^L\) for the local population.

    Age group\(m_x^S\)\(m_x^L\)\(m_x^L P_x^L\)\(m_x^L P_x^S\)\(m_x^S P_x^L\)
    0–10253521 00028 00015 000
    10–20557 0006 0007 000
    20–60101022 00025 00022 000
    60–100605040 00025 00048 000
    Total90 00084 00092 000

    Crude death rates: \(\text{CDR}_L = \dfrac{90}{5\,000}\times 1000 = 18\) per thousand (also \(\sum m_x^L P_x^L/\sum P_x^L = 90\,000/5\,000 = 18\)), and \(\text{CDR}_S = \dfrac{81}{5\,000}\times 1000 = 16.2\) per thousand.

    Direct method. \((\text{STDR})_L = \dfrac{\sum m_x^L P_x^S}{\sum P_x^S} = \dfrac{84\,000}{5\,000} = 16.8\) per thousand.

    Indirect method. The index death rate is \(\sum m_x^S P_x^L/\sum P_x^L = 92\,000/5\,000 = 18.4\), so

    \[ \hat C = \frac{\text{CDR}_S}{18.4} = \frac{16.2}{18.4} = 0.8804, \] \[ (\text{STDR})_L = \text{CDR}_L\times\hat C = 18\times 0.8804 = 15.85 \]

    per thousand (exactly \(18\times 81/92 = 15.848\)). As a check, the expected deaths at the standard rates are \(92\,000/1000 = 92\), \(\text{SMR} = 90/92 = 0.9783\), and \(\text{SMR}\times\text{CDR}_S = 0.9783\times 16.2 = 15.85\).

    Conclusion. Both standardised rates are below the local crude rate of 18, because the local population has more people in the high-mortality group 60–100 (800 against 500). The two methods differ, 16.8 against 15.85, because the local age-specific rates are not proportional to the standard rates: higher at ages 0–10 (35 against 25) but lower at 60–100 (50 against 60).

    Key Take-aways

    • Vital statistics deal with births, deaths, marriages, fertility & mortality.
    • Main sources: registration, census, sample surveys (SRS).
    • CDR = total deaths / total population × 1 000; simple but biased by age structure.
    • ASDR controls for age but gives one number per age group.
    • Standardised rates (direct/indirect) remove age-structure effect; allow fair comparison across populations.
    • Direct method needs ASDRs of study population; indirect needs standard ASDRs.
    • A crude death rate is the weighted mean \(\sum m_x P_x/\sum P_x\) of the age-specific rates, weighted by the population's own age distribution; standardising uses one common set of weights.
    • Indirect method: \(\text{STDR} = \text{CDR}\times\hat C\), with \(\hat C = \text{CDR}_S\big/\big(\sum m_x^S P_x/\sum P_x\big)\); this equals \(\text{SMR}\times\text{CDR}_S\). The two methods agree when the age-specific rates are proportional to the standard's.
