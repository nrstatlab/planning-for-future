    Practical — Design and Analysis of Experiments (STS-206, Section A)

    All sixteen prescribed Section A experiments of STS-206: where each is worked in the units, plus the three that are not — one-way and two-way analysis of covariance, and identifying or constructing a confounded arrangement in 2^3, 2^4 and 3^2 designs.

    Topics Covered

    About this paper. STS-206 is a 2-credit conventional practical in two sections: Section A, Design and Analysis of Experiments — the sixteen experiments below — and Section B, Sampling Theory, which belongs to Sampling Theory (STS-204) and will be written with that paper. Both sections are examined by hand, so every experiment is worked with full arithmetic.

    Practical 3 — Analysis of Covariance, One-Way Classification

    WHERE THE COVARIATE CHANGES THE ANSWER COMPLETELY

    Given. Three treatments, four units each. For every unit a covariate \(x\) was measured before the treatment was applied, and the response \(y\) after.

    Treatment\((x, y)\) pairs\(\sum x\)\(\sum y\)
    T₁(2, 8), (3, 10), (4, 11), (5, 13)1442
    T₂(3, 12), (4, 13), (5, 15), (6, 17)1857
    T₃(4, 11), (5, 13), (6, 14), (7, 16)2254
    Total54 153

    Step 1 — three correction factors, not one. With \(N = 12\),

    \[ \text{CF}_{xx} = \frac{54^{2}}{12} = 243, \qquad \text{CF}_{yy} = \frac{153^{2}}{12} = 1950.75, \qquad \text{CF}_{xy} = \frac{54 \times 153}{12} = 688.5. \]

    Step 2 — the total line. The raw sums are \(\sum x^{2} = 266\), \(\sum y^{2} = 2023\), \(\sum xy = 725\), so

    \[ T_{xx} = 23, \qquad T_{yy} = 72.25, \qquad T_{xy} = 36.5. \]

    Step 3 — the treatment line. From the treatment totals, each over four units,

    \[ R_{xx} = \frac{14^{2}+18^{2}+22^{2}}{4} - 243 = 251 - 243 = 8, \] \[ R_{yy} = \frac{42^{2}+57^{2}+54^{2}}{4} - 1950.75 = 1982.25 - 1950.75 = 31.5, \] \[ R_{xy} = \frac{14(42) + 18(57) + 22(54)}{4} - 688.5 = 700.5 - 688.5 = 12. \]

    Step 4 — the error line, by subtraction.

    \[ E_{xx} = 23 - 8 = 15, \qquad E_{yy} = 72.25 - 31.5 = 40.75, \qquad E_{xy} = 36.5 - 12 = 24.5. \]
    Sourcedf\(S_{xx}\)\(S_{xy}\)\(S_{yy}\)
    Treatments28.0012.0031.50
    Error915.0024.5040.75
    Total1123.00 36.5072.25

    Step 5 — the slope, from the error line only.

    \[ \hat\beta = \frac{E_{xy}}{E_{xx}} = \frac{24.5}{15} = \frac{49}{30} = 1.633333. \]

    It comes from the error line because that is the within-treatment relation between \(x\) and \(y\), which is the one the model assumes is common to all treatments.

    Step 6 — the adjusted error sum of squares.

    \[ E'_{yy} = E_{yy} - \frac{E_{xy}^{2}}{E_{xx}} = 40.75 - \frac{600.25}{15} = 40.75 - 40.016667 = 0.733333, \]

    on \(N - t - 1 = 12 - 3 - 1 = 8\) degrees of freedom, so \(MS'_E = 0.733333/8 = 0.091667\).

    Step 7 — the same adjustment on treatments plus error.

    \[ (T+E)'_{yy} = 72.25 - \frac{36.5^{2}}{23} = 72.25 - \frac{1332.25}{23} = 72.25 - 57.923913 = 14.326087, \]

    on \(N - 2 = 10\) degrees of freedom.

    Step 8 — the adjusted treatment sum of squares, by subtraction.

    \[ SS'_{\text{tr}} = 14.326087 - 0.733333 = 13.592754, \qquad \text{df } = 10 - 8 = 2. \]

    Step 9 — the test.

    \[ F = \frac{13.592754/2}{0.091667} = \frac{6.796377}{0.091667} = 74.142292 \qquad (p = 0.000007). \]

    Step 10 — the adjusted treatment means. With \(\bar x = 4.5\) and \(\hat\beta = 1.633333\),

    \[ \bar y'_i = \bar y_i - \hat\beta\left(\bar x_i - \bar x\right): \]
    Treatment\(\bar x_i\)\(\bar y_i\) \(\bar y'_i\)
    T₁3.5010.5012.133333
    T₂4.5014.2514.250000
    T₃5.5013.5011.866667

    Step 11 — and now the point of the whole exercise. Analyse \(y\) alone, ignoring the covariate:

    \[ F = \frac{31.5/2}{40.75/9} = \frac{15.75}{4.527778} = 3.478528 \qquad (p = 0.076). \]

    Interpretation. Two things have happened, and both are the reason analysis of covariance exists.

    • The error collapsed. \(40.75\) on 9 degrees of freedom became \(0.733333\) on 8: the covariate explained \(98.2\%\) of what had been called error. That alone turned a non-significant \(F\) of \(3.48\) into one of \(74.14\).
    • The ranking reversed. Unadjusted, T₃ \((13.50)\) beats T₁ \((10.50)\) comfortably. Adjusted, T₁ \((12.133333)\) beats T₃ \((11.866667)\). T₃'s units simply started higher — its mean \(x\) was \(5.5\) against T₁'s \(3.5\) — and once that head start is removed the order changes. Reporting the unadjusted means here would have been reporting the covariate, not the treatment.

    Practical 4 — Analysis of Covariance, Two-Way Classification

    THE SAME MACHINERY, ONE MORE LINE

    For a randomised block design with a covariate the model is

    \[ y_{ij} = \mu + \tau_i + \beta_j^{\text{blk}} + \gamma\left(x_{ij} - \bar x\right) + e_{ij}, \]

    and the working is identical to Practical 3 with one extra row in the table:

    1. Compute \(S_{xx}\), \(S_{xy}\), \(S_{yy}\) for blocks, treatments, error and total, exactly as the corresponding sums of squares are computed in an ordinary randomised block analysis.
    2. Take \(\hat\gamma = E_{xy}/E_{xx}\) from the error line, and \(E'_{yy} = E_{yy} - E_{xy}^{2}/E_{xx}\) on \((r-1)(t-1) - 1\) degrees of freedom.
    3. For the treatment test, adjust the treatment-plus-error line and subtract: \(SS'_{\text{tr}} = (T+E)'_{yy} - E'_{yy}\) on \(t-1\) degrees of freedom.
    4. For a block test, do the same with the block-plus-error line.
    5. Adjusted means are \(\bar y_i - \hat\gamma(\bar x_i - \bar x)\), as before.

    Two warnings that are part of the answer, not an aside. The slope is assumed the same for every treatment — parallel regressions — and that assumption can and should be tested by fitting separate slopes and comparing. And the covariate must be unaffected by the treatment; a covariate measured after treatment may already carry part of the treatment effect, and adjusting for it then removes the very thing being measured.

    Practical 7 — Identifying the Confounded Effect

    PART (a) — A \(2^{3}\) IN TWO BLOCKS

    Given. A \(2^{3}\) arranged as

    \[ \text{Block 1: } (1),\ ab,\ c,\ abc \qquad\qquad \text{Block 2: } a,\ b,\ ac,\ bc. \]

    Asked. Which effect is confounded?

    Step 1 — check that block 1 is a subgroup. Multiplying and reducing squares: \(ab \cdot c = abc\), \(ab \cdot abc = a^{2}b^{2}c = c\), \(c \cdot abc = ab\). Closed, and it contains \((1)\). \(\checkmark\) If it were not a subgroup the arrangement would not be a confounded design at all.

    Step 2 — test each effect in turn. An effect \(E\) is confounded exactly when every combination in the principal block shares an even number of letters with \(E\).

    Effect(1)abcabcAll even?
    \(A\)0101no
    \(B\)0101no
    \(C\)0011no
    \(AB\)0202yes
    \(AC\)0112no
    \(BC\)0112no
    \(ABC\)0213no

    Answer. \(AB\) is confounded. Note that this is a bad design: a two-factor interaction has been sacrificed when \(ABC\) was available.

    Step 3 — the same thing modulo 2. Block 1 is \((000), (110), (001), (111)\), and the equation these four satisfy is \(x_1 + x_2 \equiv 0 \pmod 2\) — which is the defining equation of \(AB\), read off directly.

    PART (b) — CONSTRUCTING A \(2^{4}\) WITH A CHOSEN EFFECT CONFOUNDED

    Asked. Arrange a \(2^{4}\) in two blocks of eight with \(ABCD\) confounded.

    Step 1 — write the equation. \(x_1 + x_2 + x_3 + x_4 \equiv 0 \pmod 2\), that is: an even number of letters.

    Step 2 — list.

    \[ \text{Block 1 (even): } (1),\ ab,\ ac,\ ad,\ bc,\ bd,\ cd,\ abcd, \] \[ \text{Block 2 (odd): } a,\ b,\ c,\ d,\ abc,\ abd,\ acd,\ bcd. \]

    Eight in each, as required, and block 1 contains \((1)\) and is closed under multiplication.

    Step 3 — four blocks instead of two. To get blocks of four, confound two effects, say \(ABC\) and \(BCD\). Their generalised interaction is

    \[ ABC \cdot BCD = AB^{2}C^{2}D = AD, \]

    so \(AD\) is confounded too — a two-factor interaction, which is almost certainly not acceptable. Choosing \(ABC\) and \(ABD\) instead gives \(ABC \cdot ABD = CD\): no better. The pair \(ABCD\) and \(ABC\) gives \(D\), a main effect, which is worse still. This is why the generalised interaction must be computed before the design is adopted, and it is the commonest thing to be asked for in an examination.

    PART (c) — A \(3^{2}\) IN THREE BLOCKS

    In a \(3^{2}\) the four interaction degrees of freedom split into two components of two degrees of freedom each, written \(AB\) and \(AB^{2}\). Blocks of three are formed by fixing a linear form modulo 3:

    \[ x_1 + x_2 \equiv 0, 1, 2 \pmod 3 \;\;\text{confounds } AB, \qquad x_1 + 2x_2 \equiv 0, 1, 2 \pmod 3 \;\;\text{confounds } AB^{2}. \]

    Worked, for \(AB\). Writing the nine combinations as \((x_1, x_2)\):

    \(x_1+x_2 \bmod 3\)Block
    0(0,0), (1,2), (2,1)
    1(0,1), (1,0), (2,2)
    2(0,2), (1,1), (2,0)

    Each block holds every level of A once and every level of B once, so both main effects are orthogonal to blocks; the two block degrees of freedom are exactly the \(AB\) component. Identifying which component is confounded from a given arrangement is the reverse operation: compute \(x_1 + x_2\) and \(x_1 + 2x_2\) modulo 3 for the members of one block, and whichever is constant names the confounded component.

    Working These by Hand in an Examination

    • Build every analysis of variance from the correction factor outwards, and check that the components add to the total before computing a single \(F\). In the incomplete-block designs the error is always found by subtraction, so an arithmetic slip anywhere lands in the error line and inflates or deflates every test at once.
    • Check the \(Q_i\) sum to zero in any incomplete block analysis. It costs one addition and catches most slips.
    • Check the degrees of freedom add up before the sums of squares. A degrees-of-freedom table can be written from the design alone, without any data, and is the fastest way to find a misunderstood design.
    • In a factorial, check one contrast the long way after running Yates's algorithm, and check that the first entry of the final column is the grand total.
    • In covariance analysis, keep three columns — \(S_{xx}\), \(S_{xy}\), \(S_{yy}\) — and adjust only at the end. Adjusting line by line is the commonest error, and it gives an answer that looks plausible.
    • State the design and its assumptions in the first line of the answer. A split-plot analysed as a factorial, or an incomplete block design analysed as a complete one, produces a tidy table and a wrong conclusion.

    What the Practical Record Should Contain

    FOR EACH EXPERIMENT
    1. The aim, and the design used, named.
    2. The layout, written out — blocks, replicates, which treatment where.
    3. The null hypothesis being tested.
    4. The full working: correction factor, every sum of squares, the degrees of freedom table.
    5. The analysis of variance table with \(F\) and the table value used.
    6. The conclusion in words, with adjusted means and their standard error where the design is incomplete.
