    1. Basic Concepts

    1.1 Random Experiment

    DEFINITION

    An experiment whose outcome cannot be predicted with certainty in advance, although the set of all possible outcomes is known, is called a random experiment (or a trial).

    • It can be repeated under identical conditions.
    • All possible outcomes are known beforehand.
    • The actual outcome on a particular trial is uncertain.
    EXAMPLE 1

    Tossing a fair coin: outcomes = {Head, Tail}. We don't know in advance which one will occur.

    EXAMPLE 2

    Drawing a card from a well-shuffled pack: outcomes = 52 cards. The drawn card cannot be predicted.

    1.2 Trial & Outcome

    • Trial: a single performance of a random experiment.
    • Outcome: the result of a trial.

    1.3 Sample Space (\(S\) or \(\Omega\))

    DEFINITION

    The set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment is called the sample space.

    EXAMPLE 1

    Tossing two coins: \(S = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}\); \(|S|=4\).

    EXAMPLE 2

    Throwing a die: \(S = \{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\); \(|S|=6\).

    1.4 Event

    DEFINITION

    An event is any subset of the sample space \(S\).

    • Simple (elementary) event — a single outcome.
    • Compound event — combination of two or more simple events.
    • Sure event = \(S\); Impossible event = \(\varnothing\).

    1.5 Types of Events

    • Mutually exclusive events — events that cannot occur together; \(A \cap B = \varnothing\).
    • Exhaustive events — events that together cover the whole sample space; \(A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots \cup A_n = S\).
    • Equally likely events — events that have the same chance of occurring.
    • Favourable outcomes — outcomes that result in the occurrence of the event of interest.
    • Complementary event \(\bar A\) — \(A\) does not occur; \(P(\bar A) = 1 - P(A)\).
    EXAMPLE 1 (Mutually exclusive)

    Throwing one die. \(A\) = "even number", \(B\) = "odd number". \(A \cap B = \varnothing\); they are mutually exclusive and also exhaustive.

    EXAMPLE 2 (Equally likely)

    Drawing one card from a pack: each of the 52 cards is an equally likely outcome.

    Deterministic and Random Experiments

    TWO KINDS OF EXPERIMENT

    An experiment is any activity that produces a result, called its outcome: tossing a coin, measuring the length of a table.

    • A deterministic experiment has a result that is certain in advance: measuring the length of a table, or the width of a wall, with the same instrument gives the same answer every time.
    • A random experiment is one that can be repeated under essentially homogeneous (identical) conditions, has more than one possible outcome, and does not allow the outcome of any one repetition to be predicted: tossing two coins, throwing a die, a game between two players.

    Probability is about random experiments only. A deterministic experiment has a single possible outcome, which happens with certainty, so there is nothing to measure.

    A trial is one performance of the experiment. Tossing three coins once is one trial of the experiment “toss three coins”; drawing one card from a pack is one trial of “draw a card”. If three players A, B and C play a game, the sample space of “who wins” is \(S = \{A, B, C\}\).

    The Kinds of Event, Illustrated

    DEFINITIONS WITH COINS, DICE AND CARDS
    • Event — a subset of \(S\), written \(E\). With two coins, “at least one head” is \(\{HH, HT, TH\}\). With two dice, “the same number on both” is \(\{(1,1), (2,2), \ldots, (6,6)\}\) and “sum 12” is \(\{(6,6)\}\).
    • Elementary (simple) event — cannot be split into smaller events: head, tail; each face of a die.
    • Compound (composite) event — a combination of simple events, so it can be split: “an odd number” \(= \{1, 3, 5\}\); with three coins, “exactly one head” \(= \{HTT, THT, TTH\}\).
    • Exhaustive events — events \(E_1, \ldots, E_k\) are exhaustive if at least one of them must occur: \(E_1 \cup \cdots \cup E_k = S\). Head and tail are exhaustive for a coin; the six faces for a die. The number of outcomes in \(S\) is the number of exhaustive cases, the \(n\) of the classical definition.
    • Mutually exclusive events — no two can occur in the same trial; the happening of one precludes all the others: \(E_i \cap E_j = \varnothing\) for \(i \ne j\).
    • Equally likely events — taking all relevant evidence into account, there is no reason to expect one in preference to the others: the two faces of a fair coin, the six faces of a fair die.
    • Favourable outcomes — the outcomes that entail the event. For “at least one head” with two coins: \(HT, TH, HH\). For “sum 4” with two dice: \((1,3), (2,2), (3,1)\).
    • Independent events — the happening or not of one does not change the chances of the other (§4). Heads on the first and second tosses of a coin are independent. Drawing two cards with replacement, the second draw is independent of the first; without replacement it is not: the chance that the second card is an ace is \(4/52 = 1/13\) on its own, but \(3/51 = 1/17\) once an ace has been drawn.
    • Complementary event — \(\bar E = S - E\), the outcomes of \(S\) not in \(E\), also written \(E^c\) or \(E'\). For a coin, the complement of head is tail; for a die, the complement of “odd” is \(\{2, 4, 6\}\).

    A Short History of Probability

    The theory of probability began in the 17th century with problems of gambling on dice, in the correspondence of Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat (1654). James (Jacob) Bernoulli's Ars Conjectandi (published in 1713, after his death) proved the first law of large numbers, and Abraham De Moivre's The Doctrine of Chances appeared in 1718. Bayes, Laplace, Chebyshev, Markov, Lyapunov, Lévy, von Mises, Fisher and Khinchin developed it further, and in 1933 Kolmogorov gave it the axioms of §2.3.

    2. Definitions of Probability

    2.1 Mathematical (Classical / A-priori) Definition

    LAPLACE

    If a random experiment has \(n\) mutually exclusive, exhaustive and equally likely outcomes, of which \(m\) are favourable to event \(A\), then

    \[ P(A) \;=\; \dfrac{m}{n} \;=\; \dfrac{\text{Number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{Total number of outcomes}} \]

    Limitations: assumes all outcomes equally likely; fails when \(n\) is infinite or outcomes are not equally likely.

    EXAMPLE 1

    Probability of getting a king from a pack of 52 cards.
    Favourable = 4 kings, total = 52. \(P(K) = 4/52 = 1/13\).

    EXAMPLE 2

    Probability of getting a sum of 7 on two dice.
    Total outcomes = 36. Favourable: (1,6),(2,5),(3,4),(4,3),(5,2),(6,1) = 6. \(P = 6/36 = 1/6\).

    2.2 Statistical (Empirical / Frequency) Definition

    VON MISES

    If a random experiment is repeated \(N\) times under identical conditions and event \(A\) occurs \(m\) times, then

    \[ P(A) \;=\; \lim_{N \to \infty} \dfrac{m}{N} \]
    EXAMPLE 1

    A coin is tossed 10 000 times and 5 037 heads are observed. The empirical probability of head is \(5037/10000 = 0.5037\), close to the theoretical value 0.5.

    EXAMPLE 2

    From last 1 000 customers, 320 ordered tea. Empirical probability that a new customer orders tea = 0.32.

    2.3 Axiomatic Definition (Kolmogorov, 1933)

    DEFINITION

    Let \(S\) be a sample space and \(\mathcal{F}\) a \(\sigma\)-field of events. A probability measure \(P\) is a function \(P : \mathcal{F} \to \mathbb{R}\) satisfying three axioms:

    AXIOMS
    1. Axiom of positivity (non-negativity): \(P(E_i) \ge 0\) for every event \(E_i\).
    2. Axiom of certainty: \(P(S) = 1\).
    3. Axiom of additivity: if \(E_1, E_2, \ldots, E_n\) are mutually disjoint events, \[ P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n} E_i\right) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(E_i). \]

    For an infinite sample space the third axiom is required for every sequence \(E_1, E_2, \ldots\) of mutually disjoint events (countable additivity); the finite form is the case \(E_{n+1} = E_{n+2} = \cdots = \varnothing\).

    Consequences of the Axioms

    • \(P(\varnothing) = 0\)
    • \(P(\bar A) = 1 - P(A)\)
    • \(0 \le P(A) \le 1\)
    • If \(A \subseteq B\) then \(P(A) \le P(B)\)
    • \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\) (Addition theorem)

    Probability Space

    THE TRIPLET \((S, \mathcal{B}, P)\)
    • \(S\) — the sample space.
    • \(\mathcal{B}\) — a \(\sigma\)-field of subsets of \(S\), the events: it contains \(S\), and it is closed under complements and under countable unions.
    • \(P\) — a probability function on \(\mathcal{B}\) satisfying the three axioms above.

    When \(S\) is finite (or countable), \(\mathcal{B}\) can be the class of all subsets of \(S\), and every subset is an event. That is the setting of every problem on this page.

    The Properties, Proved

    EACH FROM THE AXIOMS

    Result 1: \(P(\varnothing) = 0\). \(S = S \cup \varnothing\), and \(S\) and \(\varnothing\) are disjoint, so by additivity and certainty

    \[ 1 = P(S) = P(S) + P(\varnothing) = 1 + P(\varnothing) \quad\Rightarrow\quad P(\varnothing) = 0 . \]

    Result 2: \(P(\bar A) = 1 - P(A)\). \(A \cup \bar A = S\) with \(A\) and \(\bar A\) disjoint, so \(P(A) + P(\bar A) = P(S) = 1\).

    Result 3: \(P(\bar A \cap B) = P(B) - P(A \cap B)\). Split \(B\) by whether \(A\) happens: \(B = (A \cap B) \cup (\bar A \cap B)\), a union of two disjoint events (Fig 1.1, in §5). By additivity \(P(B) = P(A \cap B) + P(\bar A \cap B)\); subtract. In the same way, \(P(A \cap \bar B) = P(A) - P(A \cap B)\).

    Monotonicity. If \(A \subset B\) then \(A \cap B = A\), and Result 3 gives \(P(B) - P(A) = P(\bar A \cap B) \ge 0\). In particular \(A \subset S\) gives \(0 \le P(A) \le 1\).

    Result 9: \(P(A \cap B) \le P(A) \le P(A \cup B) \le P(A) + P(B)\).

    1. \(A = (A \cap \bar B) \cup (A \cap B)\), a union of disjoint events, so by additivity \(P(A) = P(A \cap \bar B) + P(A \cap B) \ge P(A \cap B)\), since \(P(A \cap \bar B) \ge 0\). …(1)
    2. \(P(\bar A \cap B) \ge 0\), and by Result 3 \(P(\bar A \cap B) = P(B) - P(A \cap B)\), so \(P(B) - P(A \cap B) \ge 0\). …(2)
    3. By the addition theorem (§5), \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + [P(B) - P(A \cap B)] \ge P(A)\), by (2). …(3)
    4. By Boole's inequality (§7), \(P(A \cup B) \le P(A) + P(B)\). …(4)

    Putting (1), (3) and (4) together gives the chain.

    3. Conditional Probability

    DEFINITION

    The conditional probability of event \(A\) given that event \(B\) has occurred (\(P(B) > 0\)) is

    \[ P(A \mid B) \;=\; \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \]
    EXAMPLE 1

    A die is rolled. Given that the outcome is even, what is the probability that it is 4?
    \(B\) = even = {2,4,6}; \(A \cap B\) = {4}.
    \(P(A|B) = (1/6)/(3/6) = 1/3\).

    EXAMPLE 2

    In a class of 60 students, 30 study Math, 24 study Statistics and 12 study both. Find probability that a randomly selected student studies Statistics, given that he studies Math.
    \(P(\text{Stat}|\text{Math}) = P(\text{Stat} \cap \text{Math})/P(\text{Math}) = (12/60)/(30/60) = 12/30 = 0.40\).

    Conditional Probability by Counting

    EQUALLY LIKELY OUTCOMES

    When the \(n(S)\) outcomes are equally likely, \(P(E) = n(E)/n(S)\) for every event, and the definition becomes a count:

    \[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{n(A \cap B)/n(S)}{n(B)/n(S)} = \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(B)} . \]

    The \(n(S)\) cancels: once \(B\) is known to have happened, \(B\) is the new sample space, and \(A\) is favoured by the outcomes of \(B\) that are also in \(A\). In Example 1 above, \(n(A \cap B) = 1\) and \(n(B) = 3\), so \(P(A \mid B) = 1/3\) at once. The counting form holds only for equally likely outcomes; the ratio of probabilities holds always.

    The Rules Still Hold Given C

    RESULTS 4 AND 5 (\(P(C) > 0\))

    For a fixed event \(C\) with \(P(C) > 0\), \(P(\,\cdot \mid C)\) obeys the same rules as \(P\).

    Result 4: \(P(A \cup B \mid C) = P(A \mid C) + P(B \mid C) - P(A \cap B \mid C)\). By the distributive law \((A \cup B) \cap C = (A \cap C) \cup (B \cap C)\). Apply the addition theorem to the events \(A \cap C\) and \(B \cap C\), whose intersection is \((A \cap C) \cap (B \cap C) = A \cap B \cap C\):

    \[ P[(A \cup B) \cap C] = P(A \cap C) + P(B \cap C) - P(A \cap B \cap C) . \]

    Divide every term by \(P(C)\); each ratio is a conditional probability.

    Result 5: \(P(A \cap \bar B \mid C) + P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C)\). By the definition of conditional probability,

    \[ P(A \cap \bar B \mid C) + P(A \cap B \mid C) = \frac{P(A \cap \bar B \cap C)}{P(C)} + \frac{P(A \cap B \cap C)}{P(C)} = \frac{P(A \cap C)}{P(C)} = P(A \mid C), \]

    because \(A \cap \bar B \cap C\) and \(A \cap B \cap C\) are disjoint and together make up \(A \cap C\), so their probabilities add to \(P(A \cap C)\).

    4. Independence of Events

    DEFINITION

    Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent if the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other:

    \[ P(A \cap B) \;=\; P(A) \cdot P(B) \]

    Equivalently, \(P(A|B) = P(A)\) and \(P(B|A) = P(B)\), provided both probabilities are positive.

    For three events \(A,B,C\) — mutual independence requires:

    • Pairwise: \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B),\; P(B \cap C) = P(B)P(C),\; P(A \cap C) = P(A)P(C)\)
    • Triple: \(P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A)P(B)P(C)\)
    EXAMPLE 1

    Two coins are tossed. \(A\) = "first coin H", \(B\) = "second coin H". \(P(A)=P(B)=1/2\), \(P(A \cap B) = 1/4 = P(A)P(B)\). Independent ✓.

    EXAMPLE 2

    A die is rolled. \(A\) = "even", \(B\) = "≥ 4".
    \(P(A) = 1/2,\; P(B) = 1/2,\; A \cap B = \{4,6\},\; P(A \cap B) = 2/6 = 1/3\).
    \(P(A)P(B) = 1/4 \ne 1/3\). Hence not independent.

    Complements of Independent Events

    RESULTS 6, 7 AND 8

    The two forms of the definition agree: if \(P(B \mid A) = P(B)\), the multiplication theorem (§6) gives \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B \mid A) = P(A)P(B)\), and in the same way \(P(A \mid B) = P(A)\) gives the product. If \(A\) and \(B\) are independent, so are their complements, in every combination.

    Result 6: \(A\) and \(\bar B\). By Result 3 and independence,

    \[ P(A \cap \bar B) = P(A) - P(A \cap B) = P(A) - P(A)P(B) = P(A)\,[1 - P(B)] = P(A)\,P(\bar B) . \]

    Result 7: \(\bar A\) and \(B\). The same with the roles exchanged: \(P(\bar A \cap B) = P(B) - P(A)P(B) = P(B)\,P(\bar A)\).

    Result 8: \(\bar A\) and \(\bar B\). By De Morgan's law \(\bar A \cap \bar B = \overline{A \cup B}\), so

    \[ P(\bar A \cap \bar B) = 1 - P(A \cup B) = 1 - P(A) - P(B) + P(A)P(B) = [1 - P(A)]\,[1 - P(B)] = P(\bar A)\,P(\bar B) . \]

    The last step is the factorisation \(1 - a - b + ab = (1 - a)(1 - b)\).

    Independent is not the same as mutually exclusive. If \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive with \(P(A), P(B) > 0\), then \(P(A \cap B) = 0 \ne P(A)P(B)\): knowing that \(A\) happened tells you that \(B\) did not, so they are dependent.

    5. Addition Theorem of Probability

    5.1 For Two Events

    \[ P(A \cup B) \;=\; P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \]

    If \(A,B\) are mutually exclusive: \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B)\).

    5.2 For Three Events

    \[ P(A \cup B \cup C) \;=\; P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(A \cap B) - P(B \cap C) - P(A \cap C) + P(A \cap B \cap C) \]

    5.3 For \(n\) Events (Inclusion–Exclusion)

    \[ P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i\right) = \sum_{i} P(A_i) - \sum_{i<j} P(A_i \cap A_j) + \sum_{i<j<k} P(A_i \cap A_j \cap A_k) - \cdots + (-1)^{n+1} P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_n) \]
    EXAMPLE 1

    From a deck, find the probability of drawing a king or a heart.
    \(P(K) = 4/52,\; P(H) = 13/52,\; P(K \cap H) = 1/52\).
    \(P(K \cup H) = 4/52 + 13/52 - 1/52 = 16/52 = 4/13\).

    EXAMPLE 2

    Probability that a student passes Math is 0.7, Physics 0.6 and both 0.5. Probability of passing at least one = 0.7 + 0.6 − 0.5 = 0.8.

    Proof of the Addition Theorem for Two Events

    PROOF

    Write \(A \cup B\) as the union of two disjoint events, \(A\) and \(\bar A \cap B\) (Fig 1.1):

    \[ A \cup B = A \cup (\bar A \cap B) \quad\Rightarrow\quad P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(\bar A \cap B) \]

    by additivity. Add and subtract \(P(A \cap B)\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} P(A \cup B) &= P(A) + P(\bar A \cap B) + P(A \cap B) - P(A \cap B) \\ &= P(A) + P[(\bar A \cap B) \cup (A \cap B)] - P(A \cap B) \qquad [\bar A \cap B \text{ and } A \cap B \text{ are disjoint}] \\ &= P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) \qquad [B = (\bar A \cap B) \cup (A \cap B), \text{ Fig 1.1}] . \end{aligned} \]

    Hence \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\). \(\square\)

    In words: adding \(P(A)\) and \(P(B)\) counts the overlap \(A \cap B\) twice, so it is taken away once.

    Addition Theorem for \(n\) Events, by Induction

    PROOF

    Statement. For \(n\) events \(A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_n\),

    \[ P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - \mathop{\sum\sum}_{1 \le i < j \le n} P(A_i \cap A_j) + \mathop{\sum\sum\sum}_{1 \le i < j < k \le n} P(A_i \cap A_j \cap A_k) - \cdots + (-1)^{n-1} P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_n) . \]

    Proof, by mathematical induction. For two events,

    \[ P(A_1 \cup A_2) = P(A_1) + P(A_2) - P(A_1 \cap A_2) , \qquad \ldots(1) \]

    so the theorem is true for \(n = 2\). Suppose it is true for \(n = r\):

    \[ P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r} A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{r} P(A_i) - \mathop{\sum\sum}_{1 \le i < j \le r} P(A_i \cap A_j) + \cdots + (-1)^{r-1} P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_r) . \qquad \ldots(2) \]

    We have to prove it for \(n = r + 1\). Apply (1) to the two events \(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r} A_i\) and \(A_{r+1}\):

    \[ P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r+1} A_i\right) = P\!\left[\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r} A_i\right) \cup A_{r+1}\right] = P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r} A_i\right) + P(A_{r+1}) - P\!\left[\bigcup_{i=1}^{r} (A_i \cap A_{r+1})\right] , \]

    where the distributive law gives \(\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r} A_i\right) \cap A_{r+1} = \bigcup_{i=1}^{r} (A_i \cap A_{r+1})\). Using (2) for the first union,

    \[ \begin{aligned} P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r+1} A_i\right) &= \sum_{i=1}^{r+1} P(A_i) - \mathop{\sum\sum}_{1 \le i < j \le r} P(A_i \cap A_j) + \cdots + (-1)^{r-1} P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_r) \\ &\quad - P\!\left[\bigcup_{i=1}^{r} (A_i \cap A_{r+1})\right] , \end{aligned} \]

    the term \(P(A_{r+1})\) joining the first sum. The last union is again a union of \(r\) events, \(A_1 \cap A_{r+1}, \ldots, A_r \cap A_{r+1}\), so (2) applies to it too:

    \[ \begin{aligned} P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r+1} A_i\right) &= \sum_{i=1}^{r+1} P(A_i) - \mathop{\sum\sum}_{1 \le i < j \le r} P(A_i \cap A_j) + \cdots + (-1)^{r-1} P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_r) \\ &\quad - \left[\sum_{i=1}^{r} P(A_i \cap A_{r+1}) - \mathop{\sum\sum}_{1 \le i < j \le r} P[(A_i \cap A_{r+1}) \cap (A_j \cap A_{r+1})] + \cdots + (-1)^{r-1} P[(A_1 \cap A_{r+1}) \cap \cdots \cap (A_r \cap A_{r+1})]\right] \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{r+1} P(A_i) - \mathop{\sum\sum}_{1 \le i < j \le r} P(A_i \cap A_j) + \cdots + (-1)^{r-1} P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_r) \\ &\quad - \left[\sum_{i=1}^{r} P(A_i \cap A_{r+1}) - \mathop{\sum\sum}_{1 \le i < j \le r} P(A_i \cap A_j \cap A_{r+1}) + \cdots + (-1)^{r-1} P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_r \cap A_{r+1})\right] . \end{aligned} \]

    Now collect terms of the same order. The pairs \(P(A_i \cap A_{r+1})\) from the bracket, which enter with a minus sign, are exactly the pairs with \(j = r + 1\) missing from \(\sum\sum_{1 \le i < j \le r}\); together they make the sum over \(1 \le i < j \le r + 1\). In the same way the triples \(P(A_i \cap A_j \cap A_{r+1})\), now with a plus sign, complete the triple sum, and so on; the last term, \(-(-1)^{r-1} = (-1)^{r}\) times \(P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_{r+1})\), stands alone. So

    \[ P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r+1} A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{r+1} P(A_i) - \mathop{\sum\sum}_{1 \le i < j \le r+1} P(A_i \cap A_j) + \mathop{\sum\sum\sum}_{1 \le i < j < k \le r+1} P(A_i \cap A_j \cap A_k) - \cdots + (-1)^{r+1-1} P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_{r+1}) . \]

    Hence the theorem is true for \(n = r + 1\), and by mathematical induction it is true for all \(n\). \(\square\)

    6. Multiplication Theorem of Probability

    6.1 For Two Events

    \[ P(A \cap B) \;=\; P(A)\, P(B \mid A) \;=\; P(B)\, P(A \mid B) \]

    If \(A,B\) are independent: \(P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B)\).

    6.2 For \(n\) Events

    \[ P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_n) \;=\; P(A_1)\, P(A_2 \mid A_1)\, P(A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2) \cdots P(A_n \mid A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_{n-1}) \]

    If events are mutually independent: \(P\!\left(\bigcap A_i\right) = \prod P(A_i)\).

    EXAMPLE 1

    Two cards are drawn one after another without replacement. Probability both are aces.
    \(P(A_1) = 4/52,\; P(A_2|A_1) = 3/51\). \(P(A_1 \cap A_2) = (4/52)(3/51) = 1/221\).

    EXAMPLE 2

    Probability of three independent machines functioning is 0.9, 0.8, 0.7. Probability all three function = 0.9 × 0.8 × 0.7 = 0.504.

    Proof of the Multiplication Theorem

    PROOF

    Let \(n(S)\) be the total number of (equally likely) outcomes in the sample space, \(n(A)\) and \(n(B)\) the numbers favourable to \(A\) and \(B\), and \(n(A \cap B)\) the number favourable to \(A \cap B\). By the definition of probability,

    \[ P(A) = \frac{n(A)}{n(S)}, \quad P(B) = \frac{n(B)}{n(S)}, \quad P(A \cap B) = \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(S)}, \quad P(A \mid B) = \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(B)}, \quad P(B \mid A) = \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(A)} . \]

    Consider \(P(A \cap B) = n(A \cap B)/n(S)\) and multiply and divide by \(n(B)\):

    \[ P(A \cap B) = \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(S)} \cdot \frac{n(B)}{n(B)} = \frac{n(B)}{n(S)} \cdot \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(B)} = P(B)\,P(A \mid B), \qquad P(B) > 0 . \]

    Now multiply and divide by \(n(A)\) instead:

    \[ P(A \cap B) = \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(S)} \cdot \frac{n(A)}{n(A)} = \frac{n(A)}{n(S)} \cdot \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(A)} = P(A)\,P(B \mid A), \qquad P(A) > 0 . \;\square \]

    When the outcomes are not equally likely, the theorem follows directly from the definition \(P(B \mid A) = P(A \cap B)/P(A)\), multiplied through by \(P(A)\).

    Multiplication Theorem for \(n\) Events, by Induction

    PROOF (\(P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_{n-1}) > 0\))

    \(n = 2\): \(P(A_1 \cap A_2) = P(A_1)\,P(A_2 \mid A_1)\), the theorem above. …(1)

    \(n = 3\): by (1), applied to \(A_1\) and \(A_2 \cap A_3\), and then to \(A_2\) and \(A_3\) given \(A_1\),

    \[ P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap A_3) = P[A_1 \cap (A_2 \cap A_3)] = P(A_1)\,P(A_2 \cap A_3 \mid A_1) = P(A_1)\,P(A_2 \mid A_1)\,P(A_3 \mid A_1 \cap A_2) . \]

    From \(r\) to \(r + 1\): suppose the theorem is true for \(n = r\) …(2). Treat \(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_r\) as one event and apply (1), then (2):

    \[ \begin{aligned} P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_r \cap A_{r+1}) &= P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_r)\; P(A_{r+1} \mid A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_r) \\ &= P(A_1)\, P(A_2 \mid A_1) \cdots P(A_r \mid A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_{r-1})\; P(A_{r+1} \mid A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_r) . \end{aligned} \]

    So the theorem holds for \(r + 1\) events, and by induction for all \(n\). The condition \(P(A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_{n-1}) > 0\) makes every conditional probability in the product defined. \(\square\)

    7. Boole's Inequality

    STATEMENT

    For any events \(A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_n\) (not necessarily disjoint or independent):

    (i)

    \[ P\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i\right) \;\ge\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - (n - 1) \]

    (ii)

    \[ P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i\right) \;\le\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) \]

    Since \(P(\bar A_i) = 1 - P(A_i)\), form (i) can also be written \(P\!\left(\bigcap A_i\right) \ge 1 - \sum P(\bar A_i)\) (Bonferroni's form).

    EXAMPLE 1

    If \(P(A) = 0.4, P(B) = 0.5, P(C) = 0.3\), then \(P(A \cup B \cup C) \le 0.4 + 0.5 + 0.3 = 1.2\) (so at most 1).

    EXAMPLE 2

    If three events have probabilities 0.05, 0.04, 0.03 (rare failures), the probability that at least one occurs is at most 0.12. This is widely used in reliability engineering.

    Both Forms, Proved by Induction

    PROOFS

    (i) \(P\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n} A_i\right) \ge \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) - (n - 1)\). By mathematical induction. For \(n = 2\),

    \[ \begin{aligned} &P(A_1 \cup A_2) \le 1 \\ &P(A_1) + P(A_2) - P(A_1 \cap A_2) \le 1 \\ &P(A_1) + P(A_2) - 1 \le P(A_1 \cap A_2) \\ &P(A_1 \cap A_2) \ge P(A_1) + P(A_2) - 1 , \qquad \ldots(1) \end{aligned} \]

    so the theorem is true for \(n = 2\). Suppose it is true for \(n = r\): \(P\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{r} A_i\right) \ge \sum_{i=1}^{r} P(A_i) - (r - 1)\) …(2). For \(n = r + 1\),

    \[ \begin{aligned} P\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{r+1} A_i\right) = P\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{r} A_i \cap A_{r+1}\right) &\ge P\!\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{r} A_i\right) + P(A_{r+1}) - 1 && [\text{from (1)}] \\ &\ge \sum_{i=1}^{r} P(A_i) - (r - 1) + P(A_{r+1}) - 1 && [\text{from (2)}] \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{r+1} P(A_i) - (r + 1 - 1) . \end{aligned} \]

    So the theorem is true for \(n = r + 1\), and by induction for all \(n\).

    (ii) \(P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n} A_i\right) \le \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i)\). By mathematical induction. For \(n = 2\),

    \[ P(A_1 \cup A_2) = P(A_1) + P(A_2) - P(A_1 \cap A_2) \le P(A_1) + P(A_2) \qquad [P(A_1 \cap A_2) \ge 0] \quad \ldots(1) \]

    Suppose it is true for \(n = r\): \(P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r} A_i\right) \le \sum_{i=1}^{r} P(A_i)\) …(2). Then

    \[ \begin{aligned} P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r+1} A_i\right) = P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r} A_i \cup A_{r+1}\right) &\le P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{r} A_i\right) + P(A_{r+1}) && [\text{from (1)}] \\ &\le \sum_{i=1}^{r} P(A_i) + P(A_{r+1}) && [\text{from (2)}] \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^{r+1} P(A_i) . \end{aligned} \]

    So the theorem is true for \(n = r + 1\), and by induction for all \(n\). \(\square\)

    8. Bayes' Theorem

    SET-UP

    Let \(B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_n\) be mutually exclusive and exhaustive events (a partition of \(S\)) with \(P(B_i) > 0\), and let \(A\) be any event with \(P(A) > 0\).

    TOTAL PROBABILITY \[ P(A) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(B_i)\, P(A \mid B_i) \]
    BAYES' THEOREM \[ P(B_k \mid A) \;=\; \dfrac{P(B_k)\, P(A \mid B_k)}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} P(B_i)\, P(A \mid B_i)} \]

    Prior \(P(B_k)\) updated by data \(A\) gives the posterior \(P(B_k|A)\).

    DERIVATION — one line from two earlier rules

    Bayes' theorem is just conditional probability with its numerator and denominator each rewritten:

    \[ P(B_k \mid A) = \frac{P(B_k \cap A)}{P(A)} = \frac{\overbrace{P(B_k)P(A\mid B_k)}^{\text{multiplication rule}}} {\underbrace{\sum_i P(B_i)P(A\mid B_i)}_{\text{total probability}}}. \]

    The numerator is the multiplication theorem (Section 6); the denominator is the total-probability law above. Bayes therefore introduces nothing new — it reverses the conditioning from \(P(A\mid B_k)\) (which we can measure) to \(P(B_k\mid A)\) (which we want).

    EXAMPLE 1 (Defective items)

    Three machines \(M_1,M_2,M_3\) produce 25 %, 35 %, 40 % of items with defective rates 5 %, 4 %, 2 % respectively. A random item is found defective. Find probability it came from \(M_1\).

    \(P(D) = 0.25(0.05) + 0.35(0.04) + 0.40(0.02) = 0.0125 + 0.0140 + 0.0080 = 0.0345\).

    \(P(M_1 | D) = 0.0125 / 0.0345 = 0.362\) (≈ 36.2 %).

    EXAMPLE 2 (Medical test)

    1 % of population has a disease. Test sensitivity = 0.99 (true positive), false-positive rate = 0.05. Given a positive test, what is the probability the person has the disease?

    \(P(D|+) = \dfrac{(0.01)(0.99)}{(0.01)(0.99) + (0.99)(0.05)} = \dfrac{0.0099}{0.0594} \approx 0.167\).

    Despite the test being 99 % sensitive, only ~17 % of positives are truly diseased — the rare base rate matters!

    Proof of Bayes' Theorem

    PROOF

    Statement. If \(E_1, E_2, \ldots, E_n\) are \(n\) mutually disjoint events with \(P(E_i) \ne 0\), \(i = 1, 2, \ldots, n\), then for any event \(A\) which is a subset of \(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n} E_i\) and has \(P(A) > 0\),

    \[ P(E_i \mid A) = \frac{P(E_i)\,P(A \mid E_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} P(E_j)\,P(A \mid E_j)}, \qquad i = 1, 2, \ldots, n . \]

    (A partition of \(S\) satisfies the condition for every \(A\).)

    Step 1: split \(A\). Since \(A \subset \bigcup_i E_i\),

    \[ A = A \cap \bigcup_{i=1}^{n} E_i = (A \cap E_1) \cup (A \cap E_2) \cup \cdots \cup (A \cap E_n), \]

    by the distributive law. The \(E_i\) are disjoint, so the pieces \(A \cap E_i\) are disjoint too.

    Step 2: total probability. By additivity, then the multiplication theorem \(P(A \cap E_i) = P(E_i)\,P(A \mid E_i)\),

    \[ P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \cap E_i) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(E_i)\,P(A \mid E_i) . \qquad \ldots(1) \]

    Step 3. By the definition of conditional probability, the multiplication theorem in the numerator and (1) in the denominator,

    \[ P(E_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap E_i)}{P(A)} = \frac{P(E_i)\,P(A \mid E_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} P(E_j)\,P(A \mid E_j)}, \qquad i = 1, \ldots, n . \;\square \]

    Worked Problems on Elementary Probability

    Twenty-two problems: nine on the classical definition, four on the addition theorem, six on the multiplication theorem and independence, and three on Bayes' theorem, followed by exercises with answers. Each one names its sample space, counts (or multiplies) the favourable cases, and states the answer as a fraction in lowest terms. The counting tools are the ones from algebra: \(^{n}C_r = \binom{n}{r}\) ways to choose \(r\) things from \(n\), and \(n!\) ways to arrange \(n\) different things. Where a sample space is small enough to list, the count has been checked by listing it.

    A. The Classical Definition

    WORKED PROBLEM 1 — at least two heads in three tosses

    Three coins are tossed. Find the probability of at least two heads.

    \(S = \{HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT\}\), \(n = 8\) equally likely outcomes. Let \(E\) = at least two heads \(= \{HHH, HHT, HTH, THH\}\), \(m = 4\).

    \[ P(E) = \frac{4}{8} = \dfrac{1}{2} . \]

    By symmetry this had to be \(\tfrac12\): “at least two heads” and “at least two tails” are complements, and swapping H and T maps one onto the other.

    WORKED PROBLEM 2 — sum 8 with two dice

    Two dice are thrown. Find the probability that the sum is 8.

    Each die has 6 faces, so \(n = 6 \times 6 = 36\) ordered pairs. Sum 8: \(E = \{(2,6), (3,5), (4,4), (5,3), (6,2)\}\), \(m = 5\).

    \[ P(E) = \frac{5}{36} = \dfrac{5}{36} . \]

    Note that \((2,6)\) and \((6,2)\) are different outcomes: the dice are told apart (say, red and blue), which is what makes the 36 outcomes equally likely.

    WORKED PROBLEM 3 — one green and one red ball

    A bag contains 4 red, 5 green and 6 yellow balls. Two balls are drawn. Find the probability that they are one green and one red.

    15 balls; two can be chosen in \(n = \binom{15}{2} = \dfrac{15 \times 14}{2} = 105\) ways. One green from 5 and one red from 4: \(m = \binom{5}{1}\binom{4}{1} = 5 \times 4 = 20\).

    \[ P(E) = \frac{20}{105} = \dfrac{4}{21} . \]
    WORKED PROBLEM 4 — two cards: two hearts; a jack and a ten

    Two cards are drawn from a well-shuffled pack of 52. Find the probability that they are (i) two hearts, (ii) a jack and a ten.

    \(n = \binom{52}{2} = \dfrac{52 \times 51}{2} = 1326\).

    (i) Two of the 13 hearts: \(m = \binom{13}{2} = 78\), so \(P = \dfrac{78}{1326} = \dfrac{1}{17}\).

    (ii) One of 4 jacks and one of 4 tens: \(m = \binom41\binom41 = 16\), so \(P = \dfrac{16}{1326} = \dfrac{8}{663}\).

    Check of (i) by the multiplication theorem (§6): \(\dfrac{13}{52} \times \dfrac{12}{51} = \dfrac{1}{17}\).

    WORKED PROBLEM 5 — 53 Fridays in a leap year

    Find the probability that a leap year has 53 Fridays.

    366 days \(= 52\) weeks \(+ 2\) days. The 52 weeks give 52 Fridays whatever happens; a 53rd needs one of the two extra, consecutive days to be a Friday. The pair can be

    (Sun, Mon), (Mon, Tue), (Tue, Wed), (Wed, Thu), (Thu, Fri), (Fri, Sat), (Sat, Sun),

    \(n = 7\), taken as equally likely. Two contain a Friday: (Thu, Fri) and (Fri, Sat), so \(m = 2\) and

    \[ P = \frac{2}{7} . \]

    The model takes the year to start on any day of the week with equal chance; any named day gives the same \(\tfrac27\).

    WORKED PROBLEM 6 — four-digit numbers ending in 5

    Four-digit numbers are formed from the digits 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, no digit repeated. Find the probability that the number ends in 5.

    The first digit cannot be 0: 5 choices, then 5, 4 and 3 for the remaining places, so \(n = 5 \times 5 \times 4 \times 3 = 300\).

    Fix 5 in the units place. The first digit is now one of 1, 2, 3, 7 (4 choices, still not 0), then 4 and 3 choices for the middle places: \(m = 4 \times 4 \times 3 = 48\).

    \[ P = \frac{48}{300} = \dfrac{4}{25} . \]

    Note why the answer is not simply \(\tfrac16\): the zero cannot lead, so numbers ending in 5 (which free the zero for the middle) are slightly over-represented.

    WORKED PROBLEM 7 — keys on the wrong hooks

    Five keys are hung at random on five labelled hooks. Find the probability that at least one key is on the wrong hook.

    \(n = 5! = 120\) ways to hang the keys. The complement, “every key on its own hook”, happens in exactly \(m = 1\) way.

    \[ P(\text{at least one wrong}) = 1 - \frac{1}{120} = \dfrac{119}{120} . \]
    WORKED PROBLEM 8 — the four S's of ASSESSMENT together

    The letters of ASSESSMENT are arranged at random. Find the probability that the four S's come together.

    10 letters: S four times, E twice, and A, M, N, T once each. Arrangements of letters with repeats divide out the orders of the identical letters:

    \[ n = \frac{10!}{4!\,2!} = \frac{3628800}{24 \times 2} = 75600 . \]

    Glue the four S's into one block. Then there are 7 units (the block, two E's, A, M, N, T):

    \[ m = \frac{7!}{2!} = \frac{5040}{2} = 2520, \qquad P = \frac{2520}{75600} = \dfrac{1}{30} . \]
    WORKED PROBLEM 9 — the two O's of BIOLOGY apart

    The letters of BIOLOGY are arranged at random. Find the probability that the two O's do not come together.

    7 letters with O twice: \(n = 7!/2!\). For the complement, glue the two O's together: 6 units, all different, \(6!\) arrangements.

    \[ P(\text{together}) = \frac{6!}{7!/2!} = \frac{2}{7}, \qquad P(\text{apart}) = 1 - \frac27 = \frac57 . \]

    A quicker view: only the two positions of the O's matter. Of the \(\binom72 = 21\) pairs of positions, 6 are next to each other, and \(6/21 = 2/7\).

    B. The Addition Theorem

    WORKED PROBLEM 10 — sum 9 or 12 with two dice

    Two dice are thrown. Find the probability that the sum is either 9 or 12.

    \(n(S) = 36\). \(A\) = sum 9 \(= \{(3,6), (4,5), (5,4), (6,3)\}\), \(n(A) = 4\); \(B\) = sum 12 \(= \{(6,6)\}\), \(n(B) = 1\). No pair has both sums, so \(A \cap B = \varnothing\).

    \[ P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B) = \frac{4}{36} + \frac{1}{36} - 0 = \dfrac{5}{36} . \]
    WORKED PROBLEM 11 — a spade or a face card

    A card is drawn from a pack of 52. Find the probability that it is a spade or a face card (jack, queen or king).

    \(A\) = spade: 13 cards; \(B\) = face card: 12 cards; \(A \cap B\) = face card of spades: 3 cards.

    \[ P(A \cup B) = \frac{13}{52} + \frac{12}{52} - \frac{3}{52} = \frac{22}{52} = \dfrac{11}{26} . \]

    Without the subtraction the three face cards of spades would be counted twice.

    WORKED PROBLEM 12 — tokens divisible by 4 or 6

    A token is drawn at random from 30 tokens numbered 1 to 30. Find the probability that its number is divisible by 4 or 6.

    \(A = \{4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28\}\), \(B = \{6, 12, 18, 24, 30\}\), \(A \cap B = \{12, 24\}\) (divisible by both, i.e. by 12, the least common multiple — not by 24).

    \[ P(A \cup B) = \frac{7}{30} + \frac{5}{30} - \frac{2}{30} = \frac{10}{30} = \dfrac{1}{3} . \]
    WORKED PROBLEM 13 — two balls of one colour

    A box contains 7 white and 5 blue balls. Two are drawn at random. Find the probability that they are both white or both blue.

    \(n = \binom{12}{2} = 66\). \(A\) = both white: \(\binom{7}{2} = 21\); \(B\) = both blue: \(\binom{5}{2} = 10\). Two balls cannot be both white and both blue, so \(A \cap B = \varnothing\).

    \[ P(A \cup B) = \frac{21}{66} + \frac{10}{66} = \frac{31}{66} = \dfrac{31}{66} . \]

    C. The Multiplication Theorem and Independence

    WORKED PROBLEM 14 — two green balls, with and without replacement

    A bag contains 5 green and 7 white balls. Two are drawn in succession. Find the probability that both are green if the first ball is (i) replaced, (ii) not replaced.

    Let \(A\) = green at the first draw, \(B\) = green at the second.

    (i) Replaced. The bag is the same for both draws, so \(A\) and \(B\) are independent: \(P(A) = P(B) = 5/12\) and

    \[ P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B) = \frac{5}{12} \cdot \frac{5}{12} = \dfrac{25}{144} . \]

    (ii) Not replaced. After a green ball is taken, 11 balls remain, 4 of them green: \(P(B \mid A) = 4/11\). By the multiplication theorem,

    \[ P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A) = \frac{5}{12} \cdot \frac{4}{11} = \frac{20}{132} = \dfrac{5}{33} . \]
    WORKED PROBLEM 15 — three girls in a row

    Three students are selected one after another from a class of 14 boys and 6 girls. Find the probability that all three are girls.

    \(A\), \(B\), \(C\) = a girl at the first, second, third selection. Each selection leaves one girl and one student fewer:

    \[ P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)\,P(C \mid A \cap B) = \frac{6}{20} \cdot \frac{5}{19} \cdot \frac{4}{18} = \frac{120}{6840} = \dfrac{1}{57} . \]

    Check by counting: \(\binom{6}{3} \big/ \binom{20}{3} = 20/1140 = \dfrac{1}{57}\).

    WORKED PROBLEM 16 — two archers at a target

    Two archers shoot at a target in turn, hitting it with probabilities 0.4 and 0.25. The second shoots only if the first misses. Find the probability that (i) the target is hit, (ii) both miss.

    \(A\) = first hits, \(B\) = second hits, the two attempts independent.

    (i) The target is hit if the first hits, or the first misses and the second hits. These are mutually exclusive, so

    \[ P = P(A) + P(\bar A \cap B) = P(A) + P(\bar A)\,P(B) = 0.4 + (0.6)(0.25) = 0.55 . \]

    (ii) \(P(\bar A \cap \bar B) = P(\bar A)\,P(\bar B) = (0.6)(0.75) = 0.45\) (Result 8). As a check, (i) and (ii) are complements: \(0.55 + 0.45 = 1\).

    WORKED PROBLEM 17 — will the puzzle be solved?

    Three friends, working independently, solve a puzzle with probabilities \(\tfrac13\), \(\tfrac25\) and \(\tfrac12\). Find the probability that it is solved.

    It is solved if at least one solves it: \(P(A \cup B \cup C)\). By the addition theorem for three events, with independence turning each intersection into a product,

    \[ P(A \cup B \cup C) = \frac13 + \frac25 + \frac12 - \frac13\cdot\frac25 - \frac25\cdot\frac12 - \frac13\cdot\frac12 + \frac13\cdot\frac25\cdot\frac12 . \]

    Over the common denominator 30,

    \[ P(A \cup B \cup C) = \frac{10 + 12 + 15 - 4 - 6 - 5 + 2}{30} = \dfrac{4}{5} . \]

    Another way. It is unsolved only if all three fail, and (Result 8) the failures are independent too:

    \[ P(\bar A \cap \bar B \cap \bar C) = \frac23 \cdot \frac35 \cdot \frac12 = \dfrac{1}{5}, \qquad P(A \cup B \cup C) = 1 - \dfrac{1}{5} = \dfrac{4}{5} . \]
    WORKED PROBLEM 18 — at least one ball of each colour

    A box contains 5 red, 3 white and 4 black balls. Four are drawn at random. Find the probability that there is at least one of each colour.

    \(n = \binom{12}{4} = 495\). Four balls with all three colours means one colour twice and the others once — three disjoint cases:

    • 1 red, 1 white, 2 black: \(\binom{5}1\binom{3}1\binom{4}2 = 5 \times 3 \times 6 = 90\);
    • 1 red, 2 white, 1 black: \(\binom{5}1\binom{3}2\binom{4}1 = 5 \times 3 \times 4 = 60\);
    • 2 red, 1 white, 1 black: \(\binom{5}2\binom{3}1\binom{4}1 = 10 \times 3 \times 4 = 120\).
    \[ P = \frac{90 + 60 + 120}{495} = \frac{270}{495} = \dfrac{6}{11} = 0.5455 . \]
    WORKED PROBLEM 19 — bounds from two probabilities

    \(P(A) = \tfrac{2}{3}\) and \(P(B) = \tfrac{3}{5}\). Show that (i) \(P(A \cup B) \ge \tfrac{2}{3}\), (ii) \(\tfrac{4}{15} \le P(A \cap B) \le \tfrac{3}{5}\).

    (i) \(A \subset A \cup B\), so by monotonicity (§2) \(P(A \cup B) \ge P(A) = \tfrac{2}{3}\).

    (ii) \(A \cap B \subset B\), so \(P(A \cap B) \le P(B) = \tfrac{3}{5}\). For the lower bound, \(P(A \cup B) \le 1\) and the addition theorem give

    \[ P(A \cap B) \ge P(A) + P(B) - 1 = \tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{3}{5} - 1 = \dfrac{4}{15} , \]

    which is Boole's inequality in its intersection form (§7) with \(n = 2\). The two probabilities add to more than 1, so the events must overlap by at least the excess, \(\tfrac{4}{15}\).

    D. Bayes' Theorem

    WORKED PROBLEM 20 — three bags, a white and a red ball

    Bag I holds 2 white, 3 black and 1 red balls; bag II 1 white, 2 black and 3 red; bag III 3 white, 4 black and 5 red. A bag is chosen at random and two balls drawn: one white, one red. Find the probability that they came from each bag.

    \(E_1, E_2, E_3\) = bag I, II, III chosen, each with prior \(\tfrac13\). \(A\) = one white and one red.

    \[ P(A \mid E_1) = \frac{\binom{2}1\binom{1}1}{\binom{6}2} = \frac{2}{15} = \dfrac{2}{15}, \quad P(A \mid E_2) = \frac{\binom{1}1\binom{3}1}{\binom{6}2} = \frac{3}{15} = \dfrac{1}{5}, \quad P(A \mid E_3) = \frac{\binom{3}1\binom{5}1}{\binom{12}2} = \frac{15}{66} = \dfrac{5}{22} . \]

    Total probability:

    \[ P(A) = \frac13\left(\dfrac{2}{15} + \dfrac{1}{5} + \dfrac{5}{22}\right) = \dfrac{37}{198} . \]

    Bayes' theorem divides each path by this total:

    \[ P(E_1 \mid A) = \dfrac{44}{185}, \qquad P(E_2 \mid A) = \dfrac{66}{185}, \qquad P(E_3 \mid A) = \dfrac{15}{37} . \]

    They add to 1, as they must. Bag III, the most likely source, is the one where a white-red pair is easiest to draw.

    WORKED PROBLEM 21 — which line made the faulty switch?

    Assembly lines A, B, C make 25%, 35% and 40% of a factory's switches; 5%, 4% and 2% of their output is faulty. A switch drawn at random is faulty. Find the probability that it was made on each line.

    Priors \(P(E_1) = 0.25\), \(P(E_2) = 0.35\), \(P(E_3) = 0.40\); likelihoods \(P(A \mid E_i) = 0.05, 0.04, 0.02\).

    \[ P(A) = (0.25)(0.05) + (0.35)(0.04) + (0.40)(0.02) = 0.0125 + 0.0140 + 0.0080 = 0.0345 , \] \[ \begin{aligned} P(E_1 \mid A) &= \frac{0.0125}{0.0345} = \dfrac{25}{69} = 0.3623, \\ P(E_2 \mid A) &= \frac{0.0140}{0.0345} = \dfrac{28}{69} = 0.4058, \\ P(E_3 \mid A) &= \frac{0.0080}{0.0345} = \dfrac{16}{69} = 0.2319 . \end{aligned} \]

    Line C makes the most switches but is the least likely culprit: its fault rate is the lowest. Line B, with a high share and a high rate, leads.

    WORKED PROBLEM 22 — six balls from one of three large urns

    Urn I holds 40 red, 30 white and 30 black balls (100); urn II 25 red, 45 white and 20 black (90); urn III 35 red, 50 white and 55 black (140). An urn is chosen at random and 6 balls drawn: 4 red and 2 white. Find the probability that they came from urn I, and from urn III.

    Priors \(\tfrac13\) each. The likelihoods choose 4 of the reds and 2 of the whites out of all \(\binom{N}{6}\) samples:

    \[ P(A \mid E_1) = \frac{\binom{40}{4}\binom{30}{2}}{\binom{100}{6}} = 0.0333, \quad P(A \mid E_2) = \frac{\binom{25}{4}\binom{45}{2}}{\binom{90}{6}} = 0.0201, \quad P(A \mid E_3) = \frac{\binom{35}{4}\binom{50}{2}}{\binom{140}{6}} = 0.0068 , \] \[ P(A) = \tfrac13(0.0333 + 0.0201 + 0.0068) = 0.02010 . \]

    Carrying full precision rather than the four-place values,

    \[ P(E_1 \mid A) = 0.5531, \qquad P(E_2 \mid A) = 0.3336, \qquad P(E_3 \mid A) = 0.1134 , \]

    and the probability that the balls came from urn I or urn III is \(0.5531 + 0.1134 = 0.6664\). With likelihoods this small, rounding them to four places before dividing can move the third decimal of the answers, so keep the exact values until the end.

    Key Take-aways from Unit 1

    • Sample space + sigma-field + probability measure = probability space.
    • Three definitions of probability: Classical, Statistical, Axiomatic.
    • Conditional probability \(P(A|B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)\); independence ⇔ \(P(A\cap B) = P(A)P(B)\).
    • Addition: \(P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)\).
    • Multiplication: \(P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B|A)\).
    • Boole: \(P(\cup A_i) \le \sum P(A_i)\).
    • Bayes' theorem reverses conditional probabilities — heart of statistical inference.
    • Every property of \(P\) follows from the three axioms: \(P(\varnothing) = 0\), \(P(\bar A) = 1 - P(A)\), \(P(\bar A \cap B) = P(B) - P(A \cap B)\), and \(P(A \cap B) \le P(A) \le P(A \cup B) \le P(A) + P(B)\).
    • If \(A\) and \(B\) are independent, so are \(A\) and \(\bar B\), \(\bar A\) and \(B\), and \(\bar A\) and \(\bar B\). Mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are never independent.
    • “At least one” is usually easiest through the complement: \(1 - P(\text{none})\).
    • Counting: \(\binom{n}{r}\) chooses, \(n!\) arranges, and repeated letters divide out their own orders.
