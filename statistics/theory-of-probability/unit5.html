    1. Moment Generating Function (MGF)

    DEFINITION

    The moment generating function of a random variable \(X\) is

    \[ M_X(t) \;=\; E(e^{tX}), \quad t \in \mathbb{R}, \]

    provided the expectation exists in some neighbourhood of \(t = 0\).

    DISCRETE / CONTINUOUS \[ M_X(t) \;=\; \sum_x e^{tx}\, p(x) \quad \text{or} \quad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{tx}\, f(x)\,dx \]

    Generating Moments

    Expanding \(e^{tX} = 1 + tX + \dfrac{t^2 X^2}{2!} + \cdots\) and taking expectation:

    \[ M_X(t) \;=\; \sum_{r=0}^{\infty} \dfrac{t^r}{r!}\, \mu'_r, \qquad \mu'_r \;=\; \dfrac{d^r}{dt^r} M_X(t)\Big|_{t=0} \]

    Properties of MGF

    1. \(M_X(0) = 1\).
    2. Origin shift & scale: \(M_{aX+b}(t) = e^{bt} M_X(at)\).
    3. Sum of independent r.v.: \(M_{X+Y}(t) = M_X(t)\, M_Y(t)\).
    4. Uniqueness: If \(M_X(t) = M_Y(t)\) in a neighbourhood of 0, then \(X\) and \(Y\) have the same distribution.
    5. MGF may not exist for all distributions (e.g., Cauchy).
    EXAMPLE 1 (Bernoulli)

    \(X\) ~ Bernoulli(\(p\)): \(M_X(t) = q + p e^t\) where \(q = 1-p\).
    \(M_X'(t) = pe^t,\; M_X'(0) = p = E(X)\).
    \(M_X''(t) = pe^t,\; M_X''(0) = p = E(X^2);\) Var = \(p - p^2 = pq\).

    EXAMPLE 2 (Exponential)

    \(X\) ~ Exp(\(\lambda\)) with \(f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x},\; x \ge 0\).
    \(M_X(t) = \int_0^\infty \lambda e^{-(\lambda - t)x}\,dx = \dfrac{\lambda}{\lambda - t},\; t < \lambda\).
    \(E(X) = M'(0) = 1/\lambda,\; E(X^2) = 2/\lambda^2;\) Var = \(1/\lambda^2\).

    Proofs of the MGF Properties

    1. \(M_{CX}(t) = M_X(Ct)\), \(C\) A CONSTANT

    Proof. \(M_{CX}(t) = E\big(e^{tCX}\big) = E\big[e^{X(Ct)}\big] = M_X(Ct)\).

    2. ADDITIVE PROPERTY OF THE M.G.F.

    Statement. The moment generating function of the sum of \(n\) independent random variables is equal to the product of their respective m.g.f.s: if \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n\) are independent,

    \[ M_{X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n}(t) = M_{X_1}(t)\,M_{X_2}(t) \cdots M_{X_n}(t) . \]

    Proof.

    \[ \begin{aligned} M_{X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n}(t) &= E\big[e^{t(X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n)}\big] \qquad [\text{definition}] \\ &= E\big[e^{tX_1}\,e^{tX_2} \cdots e^{tX_n}\big] \\ &= E\big(e^{tX_1}\big)\,E\big(e^{tX_2}\big) \cdots E\big(e^{tX_n}\big) \qquad [\text{independence}] \\ &= M_{X_1}(t)\,M_{X_2}(t) \cdots M_{X_n}(t) . \end{aligned} \]

    The second line uses the multiplication theorem of expectation (Unit 4): \(e^{tX_1}, \ldots, e^{tX_n}\) are independent because \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) are.

    3. EFFECT OF CHANGE OF ORIGIN AND SCALE ON THE M.G.F.

    Statement. The m.g.f. is not independent of a change of origin and scale.

    Proof. Let \(U = (X - a)/h\). Then

    \[ M_U(t) = E\big(e^{tU}\big) = E\big[e^{t(X - a)/h}\big] = E\big[e^{tX/h}\,e^{-ta/h}\big] = e^{-ta/h}\,E\big(e^{tX/h}\big) = e^{-ta/h}\,M_X(t/h) . \]

    So the m.g.f. is affected by a change of origin and scale: the origin brings in the factor \(e^{-ta/h}\), and the scale rescales the argument.

    4. UNIQUENESS THEOREM OF THE M.G.F.

    The moment generating function of a distribution, if it exists, uniquely determines the distribution: to a given probability distribution there corresponds only one m.g.f., and to a given m.g.f. only one probability distribution. So \(M_X(t) = M_Y(t)\) (for \(t\) in an interval around 0) \(\Rightarrow\) \(X\) and \(Y\) are identically distributed.

    Limitations of the MGF

    WHAT CAN GO WRONG
    • The MGF may not exist although every moment does. The lognormal distribution (\(X = e^Z\), \(Z \sim N(0,1)\)) has \(E(X^r) = e^{r^2/2}\) for every \(r\) (\(1.6487\), \(7.3891\), …), yet \(E(e^{tX}) = \infty\) for every \(t > 0\).
    • The MGF may not exist, and no moment either. The Cauchy distribution has neither; that is why the characteristic function (§3) is needed.
    • Even when all moments exist, they need not determine the distribution. The lognormal again: other distributions share all its moments. When the MGF exists, by contrast, it does determine the distribution (uniqueness).

    2. Cumulant Generating Function (CGF)

    DEFINITION

    The cumulant generating function is the natural logarithm of the MGF:

    \[ K_X(t) \;=\; \ln M_X(t) \]
    \[ K_X(t) \;=\; \sum_{r=1}^{\infty} \dfrac{t^r}{r!}\, k_r, \quad k_r = \dfrac{d^r}{dt^r}K_X(t)\Big|_{t=0} \]

    The first few cumulants are:

    • \(k_1 = \mu\) (mean)
    • \(k_2 = \sigma^2\) (variance)
    • \(k_3 = \mu_3\)
    • \(k_4 = \mu_4 - 3\mu_2^2\)

    Properties

    1. \(K_{aX+b}(t) = bt + K_X(at)\).
    2. For independent \(X, Y\): \(K_{X+Y}(t) = K_X(t) + K_Y(t)\) — cumulants are additive.
    EXAMPLE 1 (Bernoulli)

    \(K(t) = \ln(q + p e^t)\). \(K'(0) = p,\; K''(0) = pq\). Confirms mean = \(p\), var = \(pq\).

    EXAMPLE 2 (Exponential)

    \(K(t) = \ln \lambda - \ln(\lambda - t)\). \(K'(0) = 1/\lambda;\; K''(0) = 1/\lambda^2\).

    Cumulants in Terms of Moments

    DERIVATION

    Let \(K_1, K_2, \ldots, K_r, \ldots\) be the cumulants. Then

    \[ \begin{aligned} K_X(t) &= K_1 t + K_2\frac{t^2}{2!} + \cdots + K_r\frac{t^r}{r!} + \cdots = \log M_X(t) \\ &= \log\left[1 + \mu_1't + \mu_2'\frac{t^2}{2!} + \cdots + \mu_r'\frac{t^r}{r!} + \cdots\right] , \end{aligned} \]

    and the \(r\)th cumulant is \(K_r\) = the coefficient of \(t^r/r!\) in \(K_X(t)\). Using \(\log(1 + x) = x - \dfrac{x^2}{2} + \dfrac{x^3}{3} - \cdots\),

    \[ \begin{aligned} &K_1t + K_2\frac{t^2}{2!} + K_3\frac{t^3}{3!} + K_4\frac{t^4}{4!} + \cdots \\ &\quad = \left(\mu_1't + \mu_2'\frac{t^2}{2!} + \mu_3'\frac{t^3}{3!} + \mu_4'\frac{t^4}{4!} + \cdots\right) \\ &\quad - \frac12\left(\mu_1't + \mu_2'\frac{t^2}{2!} + \mu_3'\frac{t^3}{3!} + \cdots\right)^2 \\ &\quad + \frac13\left(\mu_1't + \mu_2'\frac{t^2}{2!} + \cdots\right)^3 - \frac14\left(\mu_1't + \cdots\right)^4 + \cdots \end{aligned} \]

    Comparing the coefficients of the powers of \(t\) on both sides:

    \[ \begin{aligned} t:&\quad \mu_1' &&\Longrightarrow\; K_1 = \mu_1' = \text{mean}, \\ t^2:&\quad \tfrac12\mu_2' - \tfrac12\mu_1'^{\,2} &&\Longrightarrow\; K_2 = \mu_2' - \mu_1'^{\,2} = \mu_2 = \text{variance}, \\ t^3:&\quad \tfrac16\mu_3' - \tfrac12\mu_1'\mu_2' + \tfrac13\mu_1'^{\,3} &&\Longrightarrow\; K_3 = \mu_3' - 3\mu_2'\mu_1' + 2\mu_1'^{\,3} = \mu_3 . \end{aligned} \]

    For \(t^4\): the first bracket gives \(\mu_4'/24\); the square gives \(\mu_2'^{\,2}/4 + \mu_1'\mu_3'/3\); the cube gives \(\tfrac32\mu_1'^{\,2}\mu_2'\); the fourth power gives \(\mu_1'^{\,4}\). With the signs and divisors of the log series, and multiplied by \(4! = 24\),

    \[ \begin{aligned} K_4 &= \mu_4' - 3\mu_2'^{\,2} - 4\mu_3'\mu_1' + 12\mu_2'\mu_1'^{\,2} - 6\mu_1'^{\,4} \\ &= \big(\mu_4' - 4\mu_3'\mu_1' + 6\mu_2'\mu_1'^{\,2} - 3\mu_1'^{\,4}\big) - 3\big(\mu_2'^{\,2} - 2\mu_2'\mu_1'^{\,2} + \mu_1'^{\,4}\big) \\ &= \mu_4 - 3\big(\mu_2' - \mu_1'^{\,2}\big)^2 = \mu_4 - 3\mu_2^2 . \end{aligned} \]

    So \(K_4 = \mu_4 - 3K_2^2\), and the moments from the cumulants are: mean \(= K_1\), variance \(\mu_2 = K_2\), \(\mu_3 = K_3\), \(\mu_4 = K_4 + 3K_2^2\).

    A shortcut. Cumulants after the first do not depend on the origin (below), so the origin may be put at the mean, \(\mu_1' = 0\). Then raw and central moments coincide and the formulae collapse at once to \(K_2 = \mu_2\), \(K_3 = \mu_3\), \(K_4 = \mu_4 - 3\mu_2^2\).

    Cumulants under a Change of Origin and Scale

    1. ADDITIVE PROPERTY OF THE C.G.F.

    Statement. The c.g.f. of the sum of \(n\) independent random variables is the sum of their c.g.f.s: \(K_{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}(t) = K_{X_1}(t) + K_{X_2}(t) + \cdots + K_{X_n}(t)\).

    Proof. By the additive property of the m.g.f., \(M_{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}(t) = M_{X_1}(t)\,M_{X_2}(t) \cdots M_{X_n}(t)\). Taking logarithms,

    \[ \begin{aligned} \log M_{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}(t) &= \log\big[M_{X_1}(t)\,M_{X_2}(t) \cdots M_{X_n}(t)\big] \\ K_{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}(t) &= \log M_{X_1}(t) + \log M_{X_2}(t) + \cdots + \log M_{X_n}(t) \\ &= K_{X_1}(t) + K_{X_2}(t) + \cdots + K_{X_n}(t) . \end{aligned} \]
    2. EFFECT OF CHANGE OF ORIGIN AND SCALE ON THE C.G.F.

    Statement. The c.g.f. is not independent of a change of origin and scale.

    Proof. Let \(U = (X - a)/h\). Then

    \[ \begin{aligned} K_U(t) &= \log M_U(t) = \log\Big[E\big(e^{t(X - a)/h}\big)\Big] = \log\Big[E\big(e^{tX/h}\,e^{-ta/h}\big)\Big] = \log\Big[e^{-ta/h}\,E\big(e^{tX/h}\big)\Big] \\ &= \log\big[e^{-ta/h}\,M_X(t/h)\big] = \log e^{-ta/h} + \log M_X(t/h) = -\frac{ta}{h} + K_X(t/h) . \end{aligned} \]
    3. ADDITIVE PROPERTY OF CUMULANTS

    Statement. The \(r\)th cumulant of the sum of \(n\) independent random variables is the sum of their \(r\)th cumulants: \(K_r(X_1 + \cdots + X_n) = K_r(X_1) + \cdots + K_r(X_n)\).

    Proof. From the additive property of the c.g.f., \(K_{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}(t) = K_{X_1}(t) + \cdots + K_{X_n}(t)\). Differentiating \(r\) times with respect to \(t\) and putting \(t = 0\),

    \[ \left[\frac{d^r}{dt^r}K_{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}(t)\right]_{t=0} = \left[\frac{d^r}{dt^r}K_{X_1}(t)\right]_{t=0} + \cdots + \left[\frac{d^r}{dt^r}K_{X_n}(t)\right]_{t=0} , \]

    that is,

    \[ K_r(X_1 + \cdots + X_n) = K_r(X_1) + \cdots + K_r(X_n) . \]

    For \(r = 1\) and \(r = 2\) this is the familiar addition of means and of variances.

    4. EFFECT OF CHANGE OF ORIGIN AND SCALE ON CUMULANTS

    Statement. Cumulants, except the first, are independent of a change of origin but not of scale.

    Proof. Let \(U = (X - a)/h\). From property 2, \(K_U(t) = -\dfrac{at}{h} + K_X(t/h)\), so

    \[ K_1't + K_2'\frac{t^2}{2!} + \cdots + K_r'\frac{t^r}{r!} + \cdots = -\frac{at}{h} + K_1\frac{t}{h} + K_2\frac{(t/h)^2}{2!} + \cdots + K_r\frac{(t/h)^r}{r!} + \cdots \]

    where \(K_r'\) and \(K_r\) are the \(r\)th cumulants of \(U\) and \(X\). Comparing coefficients,

    \[ K_1' = \frac{K_1 - a}{h}, \qquad K_r' = \frac{K_r}{h^r}, \quad r = 2, 3, \ldots \]

    The first cumulant depends on both origin and scale; every other cumulant is independent of the origin but depends on the scale.

    3. Characteristic Function (CF)

    DEFINITION

    The characteristic function of \(X\) is

    \[ \phi_X(t) \;=\; E(e^{itX}), \quad t \in \mathbb{R},\; i = \sqrt{-1}. \]

    Why use CF?

    • Always exists for any distribution (\(|e^{itx}| = 1\)).
    • Uniquely determines the distribution.
    • Used to prove CLT and many limit theorems.

    Properties

    1. \(\phi_X(0) = 1\) and \(|\phi_X(t)| \le 1\).
    2. \(\phi_X(t)\) is uniformly continuous on \(\mathbb{R}\).
    3. \(\phi_{aX+b}(t) = e^{ibt}\phi_X(at)\).
    4. For independent \(X,Y\): \(\phi_{X+Y}(t) = \phi_X(t)\, \phi_Y(t)\).
    5. Inversion theorem: distribution of \(X\) is recovered from \(\phi_X\).
    EXAMPLE 1 (Bernoulli)

    \(\phi(t) = q + p e^{it}\).

    EXAMPLE 2 (Standard Normal)

    \(\phi(t) = e^{-t^2/2}\) for \(X \sim N(0,1)\).

    Proofs of the CF Properties

    1. \(\phi_X(0) = 1\)

    Proof. \(\phi_X(t) = E\big(e^{itX}\big)\); at \(t = 0\), \(\phi_X(0) = E(e^0) = E(1) = 1\).

    2. \(|\phi_X(t)| \le 1\)

    Proof. Let \(X\) be a continuous random variable, so \(\phi_X(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{itx} f(x)\,dx\). Then

    \[ \begin{aligned} |\phi_X(t)| &= \left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{itx} f(x)\,dx\right| \le \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |e^{itx} f(x)|\,dx \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |e^{itx}|\,|f(x)|\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |e^{itx}|\,f(x)\,dx \qquad [f(x) \ge 0] \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1 , \end{aligned} \]

    because \(|e^{itx}| = \sqrt{\cos^2 tx + \sin^2 tx} = 1\). So \(|\phi_X(t)| \le 1\). Since \(\phi_X(t)\) is in general a complex number, this says that it lies in the unit disc of the complex plane.

    3. \(\phi_{CX}(t) = \phi_X(Ct)\), \(C\) A CONSTANT

    Proof. \(\phi_{CX}(t) = E\big(e^{itCX}\big)\) (by the definition) \(= E\big(e^{i(Ct)X}\big) = \phi_X(Ct)\).

    4. ADDITIVE PROPERTY OF THE C.F.

    Statement. The characteristic function of the sum of \(n\) independent random variables is the product of their characteristic functions: \(\phi_{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}(t) = \phi_{X_1}(t)\,\phi_{X_2}(t) \cdots \phi_{X_n}(t)\).

    Proof.

    \[ \begin{aligned} \phi_{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}(t) &= E\big[e^{it(X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n)}\big] = E\big(e^{itX_1}\,e^{itX_2} \cdots e^{itX_n}\big) \\ &= E\big(e^{itX_1}\big)\,E\big(e^{itX_2}\big) \cdots E\big(e^{itX_n}\big) \qquad [\text{independence}] \\ &= \phi_{X_1}(t)\,\phi_{X_2}(t) \cdots \phi_{X_n}(t) . \end{aligned} \]
    5. EFFECT OF CHANGE OF ORIGIN AND SCALE ON THE C.F.

    Statement. The characteristic function is not independent of a change of origin and scale.

    Proof. Let \(U = (X - a)/h\). Then

    \[ \phi_U(t) = E\big(e^{itU}\big) = E\big[e^{it(X - a)/h}\big] = E\big[e^{itX/h}\,e^{-ita/h}\big] = e^{-ita/h}\,E\big(e^{itX/h}\big) = e^{-ita/h}\,\phi_X(t/h) . \]
    6. UNIQUENESS THEOREM OF THE C.F.

    The characteristic function uniquely determines the distribution: if \(\phi_X(t) = \phi_Y(t)\) for all \(t\), then \(X\) and \(Y\) are identically distributed.

    MOMENTS FROM THE CF

    If \(E|X|^r < \infty\), differentiating under the expectation gives \(\phi_X^{(r)}(0) = E\big[(iX)^r\big] = i^r\mu_r'\), so \(\mu_r' = \phi_X^{(r)}(0)/i^r\). The CF always exists, and gives every moment that exists. It cannot produce moments that do not exist: the Cauchy CF, \(e^{-|t|}\), is not differentiable at 0, matching the Cauchy distribution's lack of a mean. For such distributions the CF is used to identify the distribution (by uniqueness), not to compute moments.

    4. Probability Generating Function (PGF)

    DEFINITION

    For a non-negative integer-valued r.v. \(X\) with PMF \(p_k = P(X=k)\), the probability generating function is

    \[ P_X(s) \;=\; E(s^X) \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} p_k\, s^k, \quad |s| \le 1. \]

    Properties

    1. \(P_X(1) = 1\).
    2. \(p_k = \dfrac{1}{k!}\dfrac{d^k}{ds^k}P_X(s)\Big|_{s=0}\).
    3. \(E(X) = P_X'(1)\); Factorial moment of order 2: \(E[X(X-1)] = P_X''(1)\).
    4. For independent \(X,Y\): \(P_{X+Y}(s) = P_X(s)\, P_Y(s)\).
    EXAMPLE 1 (Binomial)

    \(X \sim B(n,p)\): \(P_X(s) = (q + ps)^n\).
    \(P'(s) = np(q+ps)^{n-1};\; P'(1) = np = E(X)\) ✓.

    EXAMPLE 2 (Poisson)

    \(X \sim \) Poisson(\(\lambda\)): \(P_X(s) = e^{\lambda(s-1)}\).
    \(P'(s) = \lambda e^{\lambda(s-1)};\; P'(1) = \lambda = E(X)\) ✓.

    Proofs of the PGF Properties

    1. ADDITIVE PROPERTY OF THE P.G.F.

    Statement. The p.g.f. of the sum of \(n\) independent random variables is the product of their p.g.f.s: \(P_{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}(s) = P_{X_1}(s)\,P_{X_2}(s) \cdots P_{X_n}(s)\).

    Proof.

    \[ \begin{aligned} P_{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}(s) &= E\big(s^{X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n}\big) = E\big(s^{X_1}\,s^{X_2} \cdots s^{X_n}\big) \\ &= E\big(s^{X_1}\big)\,E\big(s^{X_2}\big) \cdots E\big(s^{X_n}\big) \qquad [\text{independence}] \\ &= P_{X_1}(s)\,P_{X_2}(s) \cdots P_{X_n}(s) . \end{aligned} \]
    2. EFFECT OF CHANGE OF ORIGIN AND SCALE ON THE P.G.F.

    Statement. The p.g.f. is not independent of a change of origin and scale.

    Proof. Let \(U = (X - a)/h\). Then

    \[ \begin{aligned} P_U(s) &= E\big(s^U\big) = E\big(s^{(X - a)/h}\big) = E\big(s^{X/h}\,s^{-a/h}\big) = s^{-a/h}\,E\big(s^{X/h}\big) \\ &= s^{-a/h}\,E\big[\big(s^{1/h}\big)^X\big] = s^{-a/h}\,P_X\big(s^{1/h}\big) . \end{aligned} \]

    A p.g.f. is meant for a variable on \(0, 1, 2, \ldots\), and \(U\) is one only for special \(a, h\); the useful case is a whole-number shift, \(h = 1\): \(P_{X + a}(s) = s^a P_X(s)\).

    5. Modes of Convergence

    5.1 Convergence in Probability

    DEFINITION

    A sequence \(\{X_n\}\) converges in probability to \(X\) if for every \(\epsilon > 0\):

    \[ \lim_{n \to \infty} P(|X_n - X| \ge \epsilon) \;=\; 0. \]

    Notation: \(X_n \xrightarrow{P} X\).

    5.2 Convergence in Distribution

    DEFINITION

    \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} X\) if the CDFs converge:

    \[ F_{X_n}(x) \to F_X(x) \quad \text{at every continuity point of } F_X. \]

    Relationship: Convergence in probability ⇒ convergence in distribution. The reverse is false in general, but it holds when the limit is a constant: \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} c\) implies \(X_n \xrightarrow{P} c\).

    5.3 Almost Sure Convergence

    DEFINITION

    \(\{X_n\}\) converges to \(X\) almost surely (with probability one) if the set of outcomes on which the sequence of numbers \(X_n(\omega)\) converges to \(X(\omega)\) has probability 1:

    \[ P\Big(\big\{\omega \in S : \lim_{n \to \infty} X_n(\omega) = X(\omega)\big\}\Big) = 1, \qquad\text{written}\qquad X_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} X . \]

    The three modes are nested:

    \[ X_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} X \;\Longrightarrow\; X_n \xrightarrow{P} X \;\Longrightarrow\; X_n \xrightarrow{d} X . \]

    Convergence in probability to a constant \(a\) is the case used by the laws of large numbers: \(\lim_{n\to\infty} P(|X_n - a| < \epsilon) = 1\) for every \(\epsilon > 0\).

    6. Weak Law of Large Numbers (WLLN)

    STATEMENT

    Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) be i.i.d. random variables with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2\). Let \(\bar{X}_n = (X_1 + \cdots + X_n)/n\). Then for every \(\epsilon > 0\):

    \[ P(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon) \;\to\; 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty, \]

    i.e., \(\bar X_n \xrightarrow{P} \mu\).

    Proof (using Chebyshev's inequality). Let \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) be independent with common mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), and \(\bar X_n = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\).

    1. Mean of the sample mean. By linearity of expectation, \[ E(\bar X_n) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n E(X_i) = \frac1n\,(n\mu) = \mu. \]
    2. Variance of the sample mean. Because the \(X_i\) are independent their variances add, and a constant factor comes out squared: \[ \operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n) = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \frac{1}{n^2}\,(n\sigma^2) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}. \]
    3. Apply Chebyshev's inequality to \(\bar X_n\) (whose mean is \(\mu\) from step 1). For any \(\epsilon > 0\), \[ P\big(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon\big) \;\le\; \dfrac{\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n)}{\epsilon^2} \;=\; \dfrac{\sigma^2/n}{\epsilon^2} = \dfrac{\sigma^2}{n\,\epsilon^2}. \]
    4. Take the limit. With \(\epsilon\) fixed, \(\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n\,\epsilon^2}\to 0\) as \(n\to\infty\). Since a probability cannot be negative, it is squeezed to \(0\): \[ P\big(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon\big) \;\to\; 0 \quad\text{as } n\to\infty, \] which is exactly the statement \(\bar X_n \xrightarrow{P} \mu\). \(\blacksquare\)
    EXAMPLE 1

    Toss a fair coin many times. Let \(X_i = 1\) if head, 0 otherwise. \(\bar X_n\) = proportion of heads. By WLLN, \(\bar X_n \to 0.5\) in probability — empirical frequency justifies the classical definition.

    EXAMPLE 2

    The sample mean of 1 000 dice rolls is very likely close to the true mean \(\mu = 3.5\): by Chebyshev with \(\sigma^2 = 35/12 \approx 2.92\), \(P(|\bar X_{1000} - 3.5| \ge 0.1) \le 2.92/(1000 \cdot 0.01) \approx 0.29\).

    The WLLN without Identical Distributions

    STATEMENT AND PROOF

    Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n\) be a sequence of random variables with means \(\mu_1, \mu_2, \ldots, \mu_n\), and let \(B_n = \operatorname{Var}(X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n) < \infty\). Then

    \[ P\left\{\left|\frac{X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n}{n} - \frac{\mu_1 + \mu_2 + \cdots + \mu_n}{n}\right| < \epsilon\right\} \ge 1 - \eta \]

    for all \(n > n_0\),

    where \(\epsilon\) and \(\eta\) are arbitrary small positive numbers, provided \(\lim_{n\to\infty} B_n/n^2 = 0\).

    Proof. The average \(\bar X_n\) has mean \(\bar\mu_n = (\mu_1 + \cdots + \mu_n)/n\) and variance \(B_n/n^2\). Chebyshev's inequality with \(c = \epsilon\) gives

    \[ P\big(|\bar X_n - \bar\mu_n| < \epsilon\big) \ge 1 - \frac{B_n}{n^2\epsilon^2} , \]

    and since \(B_n/n^2 \to 0\), the right side exceeds \(1 - \eta\) once \(n\) is beyond some \(n_0\).

    For i.i.d. variables, \(E(X_i) = \mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2\) for all \(i\), so \(B_n = \operatorname{Var}(X_1 + \cdots + X_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \operatorname{Var}(X_i) = n\sigma^2\) (the covariance terms vanish since the variables are independent), and

    \[ \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{B_n}{n^2} = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n\sigma^2}{n^2} = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sigma^2}{n} = 0 . \]

    The weak law for i.i.d. variables is then \(P\{|\bar X_n - \mu| < \epsilon\} \ge 1 - \eta\) for all \(n > n_0\): that is, \(P\{|\bar X_n - \mu| < \epsilon\} \to 1\), or \(\bar X_n \xrightarrow{P} \mu\), the theorem above.

    7. Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN)

    STATEMENT (Kolmogorov)

    Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) be i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\). Then

    \[ P\!\left(\lim_{n \to \infty} \bar X_n = \mu\right) \;=\; 1, \]

    i.e., \(\bar X_n \to \mu\) almost surely.

    WLLN vs. SLLN:

    PropertyWLLNSLLN
    Mode of convergenceIn probabilityAlmost sure (with prob 1)
    StrengthWeakerStronger (implies WLLN)
    ConditionsFinite variance (sufficient)Finite mean (Kolmogorov's)

    8. Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

    STATEMENT (Lindeberg–Lévy)

    Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) be i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2 > 0\). Define \(\bar X_n = \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\). Then

    \[ Z_n \;=\; \dfrac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \;\xrightarrow{d}\; N(0, 1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty. \]

    Equivalently, the standardized sum \(\dfrac{\sum X_i - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\) converges to standard normal.

    Implications

    • Sample mean from any distribution (with finite variance) is approximately normal for large \(n\).
    • Standard rule of thumb: \(n \ge 30\) is "large enough".
    • Forms the foundation of statistical inference (z-tests, confidence intervals).
    EXAMPLE 1 (Quality control)

    Diameter of bolts has mean 5 mm, SD 0.1 mm. For a sample of 100 bolts, the sample mean is approximately \(N(5,\; 0.1^2/100) = N(5, 0.0001)\), i.e., SE = 0.01 mm.

    \(P(|\bar X - 5| < 0.02) \approx P(|Z| < 2) \approx 0.9544\).

    EXAMPLE 2 (Polling)

    Suppose true proportion of voters supporting party A is 0.6. From a sample of 400, the sample proportion \(\hat p \sim N(0.6,\; 0.6 \times 0.4/400) = N(0.6, 0.000\,6)\), SE ≈ 0.0245.

    \(P(|\hat p - 0.6| < 0.05) \approx P(|Z| < 2.04) \approx 0.96\) — explains the typical "± 5 % margin of error".

    History and the General Statement

    FROM DE MOIVRE TO LIAPOUNOFF

    De Moivre found the normal approximation to the binomial with \(p = \tfrac12\) in 1733; Laplace extended it and stated the central limit theorem in 1812; Liapounoff (Lyapunov) gave the first rigorous general proof in 1901. In its general form: if \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) are independent with means \(\mu_i\) and variances \(\sigma_i^2\), then under very general conditions \(S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n\) is asymptotically normal with

    \[ \text{mean } \mu = \sum_{i=1}^n \mu_i, \qquad \text{variance } \sigma^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2 . \]

    Three Forms of the Lindeberg–Lévy Theorem

    I.I.D. VARIABLES, MEAN \(\mu_1\), FINITE VARIANCE \(\sigma_1^2\)

    \(S_n\) has mean \(n\mu_1\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_1\sqrt n\); \(\bar X_n = S_n/n\) has mean \(\mu_1\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_1/\sqrt n\). With \(\Phi\) the standard normal distribution function, for all \(-\infty < a < b < \infty\):

    \[ \text{(i)}\quad \lim_{n\to\infty} P\left\{a \le \frac{S_n - n\mu_1}{\sigma_1\sqrt n} \le b\right\} = \Phi(b) - \Phi(a) = \int_a^b \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}\,dx, \] \[ \text{(ii)}\quad \lim_{n\to\infty} P\left\{a \le \frac{S_n - E(S_n)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(S_n)}} \le b\right\} = \Phi(b) - \Phi(a), \] \[ \text{(iii)}\quad \lim_{n\to\infty} P\left\{a \le \frac{\bar X_n - \mu_1}{\sigma_1/\sqrt n} \le b\right\} = \Phi(b) - \Phi(a) . \]

    All three standardise the same quantity: dividing the numerator and denominator of (i) by \(n\) gives (iii).

    Liapounoff's Central Limit Theorem

    INDEPENDENT, NOT IDENTICALLY DISTRIBUTED

    Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) be independent with means \(\mu_i\), variances \(\sigma_i^2\), and finite third absolute central moments \(\rho_i^3 = E|X_i - \mu_i|^3\). Put

    \[ \rho^3 = \sum_{i=1}^n \rho_i^3, \qquad \sigma^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2 . \]

    If \(\lim_{n\to\infty} \rho/\sigma = 0\), then \(S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n\) is asymptotically \(N\big(\sum\mu_i, \sum\sigma_i^2\big)\).

    The condition says that no single variable dominates the sum. For i.i.d. variables \(\rho/\sigma = (n\rho_1^3)^{1/3}/(n\sigma_1^2)^{1/2} = (\rho_1/\sigma_1)\,n^{-1/6} \to 0\), so Lindeberg–Lévy (with a finite third moment) is a special case.

    The CLT and the WLLN Compared

    WHAT EACH ONE SAYS

    For i.i.d. variables with finite \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\), both laws hold. The WLLN says only that \(P(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon) \to 0\). The CLT says how fast, by giving the probability itself, approximately, for large \(n\):

    \[ P\big(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \epsilon\big) = P\left(\left|\frac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\right| \ge \frac{\epsilon\sqrt n}{\sigma}\right) \approx 1 - \left[\Phi\Big(\frac{\epsilon\sqrt n}{\sigma}\Big) - \Phi\Big(-\frac{\epsilon\sqrt n}{\sigma}\Big)\right] . \]

    Worked Problem 5 puts numbers on it.

    For sequences that are independent but not identically distributed, the two laws can part company:

    1. For independent, uniformly bounded variables the WLLN always holds (since \(B_n \le nc^2\), \(B_n/n^2 \to 0\)), and the CLT holds provided \(B_n = \sigma_1^2 + \cdots + \sigma_n^2 \to \infty\).
    2. The CLT may hold while the WLLN fails. Take independent \(X_i \sim N(0, i)\). Every sum is exactly normal, so the CLT holds trivially. But \(B_n = n(n + 1)/2\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n) = (n + 1)/(2n) \to \tfrac12\), not 0: \(\bar X_n\) never settles at 0, and \(P(|\bar X_n| \ge 0.5) \to 0.4795\).

    Worked Problems on Generating Functions and Limit Theorems

    A generating-function problem, then three applications of the central limit theorem, then one problem that puts numbers on the comparison of the CLT with the weak law. The method in the CLT problems is always the same: write the variable as a sum of i.i.d. pieces, find the mean and variance of the sum, and standardise. Each result here was checked symbolically (the MGF by differentiation, the cumulants by series expansion, the sums by convolution) and the limits numerically.

    A. A Generating Function Computed

    WORKED PROBLEM 1 — the MGF of the number of heads in three tosses

    A coin is tossed three times and \(X\) is the number of heads, with distribution \(P(X = 0, 1, 2, 3) = \tfrac18, \tfrac38, \tfrac38, \tfrac18\). Find the MGF of \(X\).

    \[ M_X(t) = \sum_x e^{tx}P(X = x) = \frac{1 + 3e^{t} + 3e^{2t} + e^{3t}}{8} = \Big(\frac{1 + e^t}{2}\Big)^3 . \]

    The cube is no accident: \(X\) is the sum of three independent Bernoulli(\(\tfrac12\)) variables, each with MGF \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12e^t\), and by the additive property the MGF of the sum is the product.

    Moments from it. Differentiate and put \(t = 0\):

    \[ M_X'(t) = \frac{3e^t(1 + e^t)^2}{8} \Rightarrow \mu_1' = M_X'(0) = \frac{3}{2}, \qquad \mu_2' = M_X''(0) = 3, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X) = 3 - \tfrac94 = \tfrac34 . \]

    Cumulants from it. \(K_X(t) = 3\log\frac{1 + e^t}{2}\), so \(K_X'(t) = \dfrac{3e^t}{1 + e^t}\) and \(K_X''(t) = \dfrac{3e^t}{(1 + e^t)^2}\), giving \(K_1 = \frac{3}{2}\) and \(K_2 = \tfrac34\), the mean and variance again. Carrying on, \(K_3 = 0\) (the distribution is symmetric) and \(K_4 = - \frac{3}{8}\): three times the \(-\tfrac18\) of a single fair Bernoulli, because cumulants of independent pieces add.

    PGF. \(P_X(s) = \big(\tfrac{1 + s}{2}\big)^3\), and \(P_X'(1) = 3\cdot\tfrac14\cdot 2 = \tfrac32\), the mean once more.

    B. Applications of the Central Limit Theorem

    WORKED PROBLEM 2 — the sum of \(n\) i.i.d. binomial variates

    Discuss the distribution of the sum of \(n\) i.i.d. binomial variates.

    Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) be i.i.d. \(B(r, p)\), so \(E(X_i) = rp\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = rpq\). For \(S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n\), the addition theorem and independence give

    \[ E(S_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n rp = nrp, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n rpq = nrpq . \]

    By the Lindeberg–Lévy theorem, form (ii),

    \[ \lim_{n\to\infty} P\left\{a \le \frac{S_n - nrp}{\sqrt{nrpq}} \le b\right\} = \Phi(b) - \Phi(a), \qquad 0 < p < 1 . \]

    The range must exclude \(p = 0\) and \(p = 1\): there every \(X_i\) is constant, the variance \(nrpq\) is 0, and the standardised variable is not defined. The theorem needs \(0 < \sigma^2 < \infty\).

    An exact check. Here the limit is not even needed for the shape: by the PGF, \(P_{S_n}(s) = \big[(q + ps)^r\big]^n = (q + ps)^{nr}\), so \(S_n \sim B(nr, p)\) exactly (for \(r = 2\), \(n = 5\), \(p = \tfrac13\): the convolution of five \(B(2, \tfrac13)\) is \(B(10, \tfrac13)\), with mean \(\tfrac{10}{3}\) and variance \(\tfrac{20}{9}\)). The CLT then says that a binomial with many trials is close to normal.

    WORKED PROBLEM 3 — the binomial tends to the normal

    Prove that if \(Y_n \sim B(n, p)\), then \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} P\left\{a \le \frac{Y_n - np}{\sqrt{np(1 - p)}} \le b\right\} = \Phi(b) - \Phi(a)\), \(0 < p < 1\).

    Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) be i.i.d. Bernoulli, \(B(1, p)\): each has mean \(p\) and variance \(pq\). Their sum counts the successes in \(n\) independent trials, so it has the same distribution as \(Y_n\), with

    \[ E(Y_n) = np, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y_n) = npq = np(1 - p) . \]

    The Lindeberg–Lévy theorem applied to this sum gives the result. This is the De Moivre–Laplace theorem, the oldest case of the CLT.

    WORKED PROBLEM 4 — a Poisson limit: \(P(Y_n \le n) \to \tfrac12\)

    If \(Y_n \sim\) Poisson(\(n\)), show that \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty} P(Y_n \le n) = \tfrac12\), i.e. \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{e^{-n}n^k}{k!} \to \frac12\).

    Write \(Y_n\) as a sum. Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) be i.i.d. Poisson(1). By the PGF, \(P_{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}(s) = \big[e^{(s - 1)}\big]^n = e^{n(s - 1)}\), the PGF of Poisson(\(n\)). So \(Y_n\) is such a sum, with \(E(Y_n) = \operatorname{Var}(Y_n) = n\).

    Apply the CLT. \((Y_n - n)/\sqrt n\) tends in distribution to \(N(0, 1)\). Since \(\Phi\) is continuous, the distribution functions converge at every point, in particular at 0:

    \[ P(Y_n \le n) = P\left\{\frac{Y_n - n}{\sqrt n} \le 0\right\} \to \Phi(0) = \frac12 . \]

    (In the notation of the theorem, \(a = -\infty\), \(b = 0\): \(\Phi(0) - \Phi(-\infty) = \tfrac12\).) Writing out the Poisson probabilities, \(\sum_{k=0}^{n} e^{-n}n^k/k! \to \tfrac12\).

    How fast? Exactly computed:

    \(n\)11010010005000
    \(P(Y_n \le n)\)0.73580.58300.52660.50840.5038

    (At \(n = 1\) it is \(2e^{-1}\).) The approach is slow, and from above, for two reasons that push the same way: the event \(Y_n \le n\) counts the whole probability at the single value \(n\), about \(1/\sqrt{2\pi n}\), and the Poisson is skewed to the right. Together they leave an excess of about \(2/(3\sqrt{2\pi n})\) over \(\tfrac12\) (\(0.5266\) predicted and found at \(n = 100\)).

    WORKED PROBLEM 5 — how much the CLT adds to the WLLN

    A fair coin is tossed \(n = 900\) times and \(\bar X\) is the proportion of heads. Bound \(P(|\bar X - \tfrac12| \ge 0.04)\) by Chebyshev's inequality, approximate it by the CLT, and compare with the exact value.

    Each toss has \(\mu = \tfrac12\), \(\sigma^2 = \tfrac14\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X) = \tfrac{1}{4 \cdot 900}\) and \(\sigma/\sqrt n = \tfrac{0.5}{30} = 0.01667\).

    Chebyshev (the WLLN's tool):

    \[ P\big(|\bar X - \tfrac12| \ge 0.04\big) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2} = \frac{0.25}{900 \times 0.0016} = 0.1736 . \]

    CLT: \(\epsilon\sqrt n/\sigma = 0.04 \times 30/0.5 = 2.4\), so

    \[ P\big(|\bar X - \tfrac12| \ge 0.04\big) \approx P(|Z| \ge 2.4) = 2\big[1 - \Phi(2.4)\big] = 0.0164 . \]

    Exact (binomial, 414 heads or fewer, or 486 or more): \(0.0179\).

    The WLLN guarantees only that this probability goes to 0; Chebyshev caps it at 0.17. The CLT gives its actual size, within a fraction of a percentage point.

    Key Take-aways from Unit 5

    • MGF generates moments; for independent r.v., \(M_{X+Y} = M_X M_Y\).
    • CGF \(K = \ln M\); cumulants are additive for independent r.v.
    • Characteristic function \(\phi(t) = E(e^{itX})\) always exists and uniquely identifies the distribution.
    • PGF \(P(s) = E(s^X)\) is the natural tool for non-negative integer r.v.
    • WLLN: sample mean → population mean in probability; SLLN: same, almost surely.
    • CLT: standardized sample mean → \(N(0,1)\) for large \(n\), regardless of original distribution.
    • An MGF that exists near 0 guarantees every moment and generates them; moments can exist without an MGF (lognormal).
    • Cumulants after the first ignore the origin and scale as \(K_r/h^r\); \(K_4 = \mu_4 - 3\mu_2^2\).
    • Standardise the sum by its own standard deviation \(\sigma\sqrt n\), the mean by \(\sigma/\sqrt n\).
    • The WLLN says the error probability tends to 0; the CLT says how large it is.
