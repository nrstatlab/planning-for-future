    Practical 1 — MLE of the Mean Vector and Covariance Matrix

    1. Problem

    Four observations on two variables, \((2, 5), (4, 9), (6, 7), (8, 11)\), are a sample from a bivariate normal population. Find the maximum likelihood estimates \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\), the unbiased \(\mathbf{S}\), both generalized variances, and the correlation.

    2. Aim

    To estimate the mean vector and the dispersion matrix of a multivariate normal population by maximum likelihood.

    3. Formula

    \[ \hat{\boldsymbol\mu} = \bar{\mathbf{x}}, \qquad \mathbf{A} = \sum_{i}(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})', \qquad \hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma} = \frac{\mathbf{A}}{n}, \quad \mathbf{S} = \frac{\mathbf{A}}{n-1}, \qquad r = \frac{a_{12}}{\sqrt{a_{11}a_{22}}} \]

    Applying it:

    1. Compute the mean vector and the deviations.
    2. Form the matrix of sums of squares and products \(\mathbf{A}\).
    3. Divide by \(n\) and by \(n-1\); take determinants from the exact values, not the rounded decimals.

    4. Calculation

    \[ \bar x_1 = \frac{2+4+6+8}{4} = 5, \qquad \bar x_2 = \frac{5+9+7+11}{4} = 8; \qquad \text{deviations } (-3, -3),\ (-1, 1),\ (1, -1),\ (3, 3). \] \[ a_{11} = 9 + 1 + 1 + 9 = 20, \quad a_{22} = 20, \quad a_{12} = 9 - 1 - 1 + 9 = 16, \qquad \mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix}20 & 16\\ 16 & 20\end{pmatrix}. \] \[ \hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma} = \frac{\mathbf{A}}{4} = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 4\\ 4 & 5\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{S} = \frac{\mathbf{A}}{3} = \begin{pmatrix}6.666667 & 5.333333\\ 5.333333 & 6.666667\end{pmatrix}. \] \[ |\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}| = 25 - 16 = 9, \qquad |\mathbf{S}| = \frac{|\mathbf{A}|}{3^{2}} = \frac{400 - 256}{9} = 16, \qquad r = \frac{16}{20} = 0.8. \]

    A warning worth taking. Computing \(|\mathbf{S}|\) from the six-decimal entries gives \(44.444449 - 28.444441 = 16.000008\), not 16: a determinant multiplies its entries together, so rounding error multiplies too. Always take a determinant from the exact values.

    5. Result

    \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu} = (5, 8)'\), \(\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma} = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 4\\ 4 & 5\end{pmatrix}\), \(|\hat{\boldsymbol\Sigma}| = 9\), \(|\mathbf{S}| = 16\) and \(r = 0.8\). The ratio \(9/25 = 0.36 = 1 - r^{2}\) is not a coincidence: for \(p = 2\), \(|\boldsymbol\Sigma| = \sigma_{11}\sigma_{22}(1 - \rho^{2})\), so a generalized variance collapses towards zero as the variables approach an exact linear relation (worked also in Unit 1, Example 1.3).

    Practical 2 — Writing the Density, and Reading It Backwards

    1. Problem

    (a) Write the bivariate normal density with \(\boldsymbol\mu = (5, 8)'\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 4\\ 4 & 5\end{pmatrix}\), the estimates of Practical 1. (b) A bivariate normal density is

    \[ f(x_1, x_2) = k\,\exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{12}\left[2(x_1-3)^{2} - 4(x_1-3)(x_2-4) + 5(x_2-4)^{2}\right]\right\}. \]

    Find \(\boldsymbol\mu\), \(\boldsymbol\Sigma\), \(\rho\) and the constant \(k\).

    2. Aim

    To write a multivariate normal density from its parameters, and to read the parameters back out of a given density.

    3. Formula

    \[ f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{p/2}|\boldsymbol\Sigma|^{1/2}} \exp\!\left\{-\tfrac12(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)\right\}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix}a & b\\ b & d\end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - b^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}d & -b\\ -b & a\end{pmatrix} \]

    Applying it:

    1. (a) Find \(|\boldsymbol\Sigma|\) and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\) (swap the diagonal, negate the off-diagonal, divide by the determinant); expand the quadratic form; assemble.
    2. (b) Read \(\boldsymbol\mu\) from the centring; take \(-\tfrac12\) out of the exponent to get \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\), halving the printed cross-term; invert; verify.

    4. Calculation

    (a) \(|\boldsymbol\Sigma| = 25 - 16 = 9\), \(|\boldsymbol\Sigma|^{1/2} = 3\), and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{9}\begin{pmatrix}5 & -4\\ -4 & 5\end{pmatrix}\). The constant is \(1/(2\pi \times 3) = 1/18.849556 = 0.053052\). With \(u = x_1 - 5\), \(v = x_2 - 8\), \((\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)'\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu) = \frac{1}{9}(5u^{2} - 8uv + 5v^{2})\), the cross-term coefficient being \(2 \times (-4) = -8\) because the matrix contributes \(-4\) twice. So

    \[ f(x_1, x_2) = 0.053052\, \exp\!\left\{-\frac{1}{18}\left[5(x_1-5)^{2} - 8(x_1-5)(x_2-8) + 5(x_2-8)^{2}\right]\right\}, \]

    the 18 being \(2 \times 9\). At \(\mathbf{x} = \boldsymbol\mu\) the exponent is zero, so \(f(5, 8) = 0.053052\), the largest value the density takes. \(\checkmark\)

    (b) The exponent is a quadratic form in \(x_1 - 3\) and \(x_2 - 4\), so \(\boldsymbol\mu = (3, 4)'\). Writing \(-\tfrac{1}{12} = -\tfrac12 \times \tfrac16\),

    \[ \boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix}2 & -2\\ -2 & 5\end{pmatrix}, \]

    the off-diagonal entry being \(-2\) and not \(-4\), because the printed cross term is the sum of the \((1,2)\) and \((2,1)\) contributions. Its determinant is \((1/6)^{2}(10 - 4) = 1/6\), so \(|\boldsymbol\Sigma| = 6\) and

    \[ \boldsymbol\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 2\\ 2 & 2\end{pmatrix}; \qquad \boldsymbol\Sigma\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix}10 - 4 & -10 + 10\\ 4 - 4 & -4 + 10\end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{I}. \checkmark \] \[ \rho = \frac{2}{\sqrt{5 \times 2}} = \frac{2}{3.162278} = 0.632456, \qquad k = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt 6} = \frac{1}{15.390598} = 0.064975. \]

    5. Result

    (a) \(f(x_1, x_2) = 0.053052\,\exp\{-\frac{1}{18}[5(x_1-5)^{2} - 8(x_1-5)(x_2-8) + 5(x_2-8)^{2}]\}\). (b) \(\boldsymbol\mu = (3, 4)'\), \(\boldsymbol\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 2\\ 2 & 2\end{pmatrix}\), \(\rho = 0.6325\), \(k = 0.064975\).

    Interpretation. This is the matrix analysed in Practical 7, whose principal components are \(\lambda_1 = 6\) and \(\lambda_2 = 1\): the density's contours have their long axis along \((2, 1)'\), \(\sqrt 6 = 2.449\) times the short one. Every geometric fact about the distribution is in those four numbers.

    Practical 3 — Hotelling's \(T^{2}\) for One Sample

    1. Problem

    \(n = 10\) observations on \(p = 2\) variables have \(\bar{\mathbf{x}} = (7, 12)'\) and \(\mathbf{S} = \begin{pmatrix}4 & 3\\ 3 & 9\end{pmatrix}\). Test \(H_0: \boldsymbol\mu = (5, 9)'\) at 5%.

    2. Aim

    To test a hypothesis about a mean vector by Hotelling's T-squared.

    3. Formula

    \[ T^{2} = n(\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \boldsymbol\mu_0)'\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \boldsymbol\mu_0), \qquad F = \frac{n-p}{(n-1)p}\,T^{2} \sim F_{p,\,n-p} \text{ under } H_0 \]

    The test assumes multivariate normality.

    Applying it:

    1. Form \(\mathbf{d} = \bar{\mathbf{x}} - \boldsymbol\mu_0\) and invert \(\mathbf{S}\).
    2. Compute the quadratic form and \(T^{2}\).
    3. Convert to \(F\) and compare with \(F_{0.05;\,2,\,8}\).

    4. Calculation

    \[ \mathbf{d} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\3\end{pmatrix}, \qquad |\mathbf{S}| = 36 - 9 = 27, \qquad \mathbf{S}^{-1} = \frac{1}{27}\begin{pmatrix}9 & -3\\ -3 & 4\end{pmatrix}. \] \[ \mathbf{d}'\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{d} = \frac{1}{27}\left[9(2)^{2} - 2(3)(2)(3) + 4(3)^{2}\right] = \frac{36 - 36 + 36}{27} = \frac{4}{3}, \qquad T^{2} = 10 \times \frac{4}{3} = 13.333333. \] \[ F = \frac{8}{18} \times \frac{40}{3} = \frac{160}{27} = 5.925926 \text{ on } (2, 8) \text{ d.f.}; \qquad F_{0.05;\,2,\,8} = 4.4590, \quad p = 0.026373. \]

    Variable by variable, \(t_1 = 2/\sqrt{4/10} = 3.1623\) and \(t_2 = 3/\sqrt{9/10} = 3.1623\), each on 9 d.f., each significant.

    CHECK IN R 
    xb <- c(7, 12); S <- matrix(c(4, 3, 3, 9), 2); n <- 10; p <- 2
d <- xb - c(5, 9)
T2 <- n * drop(t(d) %*% solve(S) %*% d); F <- (n - p) / ((n - 1) * p) * T2
c(T2 = T2, F = F, F05 = qf(0.95, p, n - p), p = pf(F, p, n - p, lower.tail = FALSE))
             T2           F         F05           p 
13.33333333  5.92592593  4.45897011  0.02637278

    5. Result

    \(F = 5.926 > 4.459\) (\(p = 0.026\)): reject \(H_0\); the mean vector is not \((5, 9)'\). Here the separate tests agree. Notice the cancellation \(36 - 36\): because \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are positively correlated, a joint shift in the same direction is ordinary and counts for little. \(T^{2}\) measures distance in the metric of \(\mathbf{S}^{-1}\) (worked also in Unit 3, Example 3.1).

    Practical 4 — Mahalanobis \(D^{2}\) for One Sample

    1. Problem

    For the data of Practical 3 (\(n = 10\), \(\bar{\mathbf{x}} = (7, 12)'\), \(\mathbf{S} = \begin{pmatrix}4 & 3\\ 3 & 9\end{pmatrix}\), \(\boldsymbol\mu_0 = (5, 9)'\)), find the Mahalanobis distance of the sample mean from \(\boldsymbol\mu_0\), relate it to \(T^{2}\), and compare it with the Euclidean distance.

    2. Aim

    To measure the distance of a mean vector from a hypothesised value in the metric of the dispersion matrix.

    3. Formula

    \[ D^{2} = (\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \boldsymbol\mu_0)'\mathbf{S}^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \boldsymbol\mu_0), \qquad T^{2} = n\,D^{2}, \qquad \|\mathbf{d}\| = \sqrt{\mathbf{d}'\mathbf{d}} \]

    Applying it:

    1. Form the deviation vector and \(\mathbf{S}^{-1}\).
    2. Compute \(D^{2}\) and \(D\); relate to \(T^{2}\).
    3. Compute the Euclidean distance, and \(D^{2}\) for a shift of the same size in the opposite direction.

    4. Calculation

    \(\mathbf{d} = (2, 3)'\); \(|\mathbf{S}| = 27\), \(\mathbf{S}^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{27}\begin{pmatrix}9 & -3\\ -3 & 4\end{pmatrix}\).

    \[ D^{2} = \frac{9(4) - 6(2)(3) + 4(9)}{27} = \frac{36 - 36 + 36}{27} = \frac{4}{3} = 1.333333, \qquad D = 1.154701. \] \[ T^{2} = n\,D^{2} = 10 \times \frac{4}{3} = 13.333333, \]

    the statistic of Practical 3: \(F = 5.925926\) on \((2, 8)\) d.f., \(p = 0.026373\).

    \[ \|\mathbf{d}\| = \sqrt{2^{2} + 3^{2}} = \sqrt{13} = 3.605551; \qquad \text{for } (2, -3)': \ \frac{36 + 36 + 36}{27} = \frac{108}{27} = 4.000000. \]
    CHECK IN R 
    S <- matrix(c(4, 3, 3, 9), 2); d <- c(7, 12) - c(5, 9)
D2 <- drop(t(d) %*% solve(S) %*% d)
c(D2 = D2, D = sqrt(D2), T2 = 10 * D2, euclid = sqrt(sum(d^2)),
  opposite = drop(t(c(2, -3)) %*% solve(S) %*% c(2, -3)))
           D2         D        T2    euclid  opposite 
 1.333333  1.154701 13.333333  3.605551  4.000000

    5. Result

    \(D^{2} = 1.3333\), \(D = 1.1547\), and \(T^{2} = nD^{2} = 13.33\), significant at 5% (Practical 3).

    Interpretation. The Euclidean distance 3.606 and the Mahalanobis distance 1.155 are not comparable numbers, and the difference is the whole point. The Euclidean figure changes if \(x_1\) is rescaled; the Mahalanobis figure does not. \(D^{2}\) is small because the shift \((2, 3)'\) runs along the direction in which the variables already co-vary; the shift \((2, -3)'\), the same Euclidean size, is three times as far (\(D^{2} = 4\)) in the metric that matters.

    Practical 5 — Hotelling's \(T^{2}\) for Equality of Two Mean Vectors

    1. Problem

    Two samples, \(n_1 = 12\) and \(n_2 = 15\), on \(p = 2\) variables have \(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_1 = (10, 14)'\), \(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_2 = (8, 11)'\) and pooled dispersion matrix \(\mathbf{S}_p = \begin{pmatrix}4 & 2\\ 2 & 5\end{pmatrix}\). Test \(H_0: \boldsymbol\mu_1 = \boldsymbol\mu_2\) at 5%.

    2. Aim

    To test the equality of two mean vectors by the two-sample Hotelling's T-squared.

    3. Formula

    \[ T^{2} = \frac{n_1n_2}{n_1+n_2}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_1 - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_2)'\mathbf{S}_p^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_1 - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_2), \qquad F = \frac{n_1+n_2-p-1}{(n_1+n_2-2)p}\,T^{2} \sim F_{p,\,n_1+n_2-p-1} \]

    The test assumes multivariate normality and equal dispersion matrices.

    Applying it:

    1. Form \(\mathbf{d} = \bar{\mathbf{x}}_1 - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_2\) and invert \(\mathbf{S}_p\).
    2. Compute \(D^{2} = \mathbf{d}'\mathbf{S}_p^{-1}\mathbf{d}\), then \(T^{2}\) with the multiplier \(n_1n_2/(n_1+n_2)\).
    3. Convert to \(F\) on \((2, 24)\) d.f. and decide.

    4. Calculation

    \[ \mathbf{d} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\3\end{pmatrix}, \qquad |\mathbf{S}_p| = 20 - 4 = 16, \qquad \mathbf{S}_p^{-1} = \frac{1}{16}\begin{pmatrix}5 & -2\\ -2 & 4\end{pmatrix}. \] \[ D^{2} = \frac{20 - 24 + 36}{16} = 2, \qquad \frac{n_1n_2}{n_1+n_2} = \frac{180}{27} = \frac{20}{3}, \qquad T^{2} = \frac{20}{3} \times 2 = 13.333333. \] \[ F = \frac{24}{25 \times 2} \times \frac{40}{3} = 0.48 \times 13.333333 = 6.4 \text{ on } (2, 24) \text{ d.f.}; \qquad F_{0.05;\,2,\,24} = 3.4028, \quad p = 0.005921. \]
    CHECK IN R 
    Sp <- matrix(c(4, 2, 2, 5), 2); d <- c(10, 14) - c(8, 11); n1 <- 12; n2 <- 15; p <- 2
D2 <- drop(t(d) %*% solve(Sp) %*% d); T2 <- n1 * n2 / (n1 + n2) * D2
F <- (n1 + n2 - p - 1) / ((n1 + n2 - 2) * p) * T2
c(D2 = D2, T2 = T2, F = F, F05 = qf(0.95, p, n1 + n2 - p - 1), p = pf(F, p, n1 + n2 - p - 1, lower.tail = FALSE))
              D2           T2            F          F05            p 
 2.000000000 13.333333333  6.400000000  3.402826105  0.005920537

    5. Result

    \(F = 6.4 > 3.403\) (\(p = 0.0059\)): reject \(H_0\); the two mean vectors differ (worked also in Unit 3, Example 3.2).

    Practical 6 — Mahalanobis \(D^{2}\) for Equality of Two Mean Vectors

    1. Problem

    For the two samples of Practical 5, find the Mahalanobis distance between the two mean vectors, and explain how it differs from \(T^{2}\).

    2. Aim

    To measure the distance between two population centres in the metric of their common dispersion matrix.

    3. Formula

    \[ D^{2} = (\bar{\mathbf{x}}_1 - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_2)'\mathbf{S}_p^{-1}(\bar{\mathbf{x}}_1 - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_2), \qquad T^{2} = \frac{n_1n_2}{n_1+n_2}\,D^{2} \]

    Applying it:

    1. Compute \(D^{2}\) from \(\mathbf{d}\) and \(\mathbf{S}_p^{-1}\).
    2. Take the square root; relate to \(T^{2}\) through the multiplier.

    4. Calculation

    \[ D^{2} = \frac{1}{16}\left[5(2)^{2} - 2(2)(2)(3) + 4(3)^{2}\right] = \frac{20 - 24 + 36}{16} = \frac{32}{16} = 2, \qquad D = \sqrt 2 = 1.414214. \] \[ T^{2} = \frac{20}{3} \times 2 = 13.333333 \text{ (Practical 5)}. \]
    CHECK IN R 
    Sp <- matrix(c(4, 2, 2, 5), 2); d <- c(10, 14) - c(8, 11)
D2 <- mahalanobis(c(10, 14), c(8, 11), Sp)   # the same quadratic form
c(D2 = D2, D = sqrt(D2), T2 = 12 * 15 / 27 * D2)
           D2         D        T2 
 2.000000  1.414214 13.333333

    5. Result

    \(D^{2} = 2\), \(D = 1.414\): the two population centres are about one and a half "standard multivariate units" apart. That is a statement about the populations and does not change with sample size. \(T^{2} = 13.333\) is a statement about the evidence, and it grows with \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) through the multiplier. A large \(T^{2}\) with a small \(D^{2}\) means a reliably detected but practically trivial difference (worked also in Unit 3, Example 3.2, Step 3).

    Practical 7 — Computation of Principal Components

    1. Problem

    (a) Find the principal components of \(\boldsymbol\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix}5 & 2\\ 2 & 2\end{pmatrix}\), the proportion of variance each carries, and the loadings of the first. (b) Find the principal components of the correlation matrix

    \[ \mathbf{R} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0.6 & 0.5\\ 0.6 & 1 & 0.4\\ 0.5 & 0.4 & 1\end{pmatrix} \]

    and decide how many to keep.

    2. Aim

    To find the principal components of a dispersion or correlation matrix from its eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

    3. Formula

    \[ |\boldsymbol\Sigma - \lambda\mathbf{I}| = 0, \quad (\boldsymbol\Sigma - \lambda_k\mathbf{I})\mathbf{e}_k = \mathbf{0}, \quad Y_k = \mathbf{e}_k'\mathbf{X}, \quad \frac{\lambda_k}{\sum\lambda}, \quad \operatorname{Corr}(Y_k, X_j) = \frac{e_{kj}\sqrt{\lambda_k}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{jj}}} \] \[ 2 \times 2: \ \lambda^{2} - (\operatorname{tr})\lambda + \det = 0; \qquad 3 \times 3: \ \lambda^{3} - c_1\lambda^{2} + c_2\lambda - c_3 = 0 \]

    \(c_1\) is the trace, \(c_2\) the sum of the three \(2 \times 2\) principal minors and \(c_3\) the determinant. Check every eigenvalue set against the trace and the determinant.

    Applying it:

    1. Form and solve the characteristic equation.
    2. Check: the eigenvalues sum to the trace and multiply to the determinant.
    3. Find the normalised eigenvectors; the components, the proportions and the loadings follow.

    1. Problem

    2. Aim

    To find the linear combinations of two sets of variables that are most highly correlated with each other.

    3. Formula

    A matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}\alpha & \beta\\ \beta & \alpha\end{pmatrix}\) has eigenvalues \(\alpha \pm \beta\) with eigenvectors \((1, 1)'\) and \((1, -1)'\).

    Applying it:

    1. Invert \(\mathbf{R}_{11}\) and \(\mathbf{R}_{22}\).
    2. Form \(\mathbf{M}\); its eigenvalues are the squared canonical correlations.
    3. Scale each canonical vector to unit variance; verify the correlation directly.

    1. Problem

    For three variables with \(\rho_{12} = 0.6\), \(\rho_{13} = 0.5\), \(\rho_{23} = 0.4\), fit the orthogonal factor model with one common factor, and find the communalities and uniquenesses.

    To fit a one-factor orthogonal factor model and split each variable's variance into its common and unique parts.

    3. Formula

    \[ X_j = l_j F + \varepsilon_j, \qquad \rho_{ij} = l_il_j \ (i \ne j), \qquad h_j^{2} = l_j^{2}, \qquad \psi_j = 1 - h_j^{2}; \qquad l_1^{2} = \frac{\rho_{12}\rho_{13}}{\rho_{23}} \]

    Practical 13 — Multidimensional Scaling

    1. Problem

    Three objects have distances \(d_{12} = 3\), \(d_{13} = 5\), \(d_{23} = 4\), and no coordinates. Recover a configuration of points by classical scaling.

    2. Aim

    To recover a configuration of points from their distances alone, by classical (metric) multidimensional scaling.

    3. Formula

    \[ b_{ij} = -\tfrac12\left(d_{ij}^{2} - \bar d_{i\cdot}^{2} - \bar d_{\cdot j}^{2} + \bar d_{\cdot\cdot}^{2}\right), \qquad \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{E}\boldsymbol\Lambda\mathbf{E}', \qquad \text{coordinates} = \mathbf{E}\boldsymbol\Lambda^{1/2} \]

    Applying it:

    1. Square the distances; find the row, column and grand means.
    2. Double-centre to get \(\mathbf{B}\); check that its rows sum to zero.
    3. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors; scale the eigenvectors by \(\sqrt{\lambda}\) for the coordinates.
    4. Rebuild the distances.

    4. Calculation

    Working These by Hand in an Examination

    • Invert \(2 \times 2\) matrices by the swap-negate-divide rule and keep the determinant outside as a fraction. Carrying \(\tfrac{1}{27}\) through to the end is far safer than converting to decimals early, and it makes the final cancellation visible.
    • Check every inverse by multiplying back to \(\mathbf{I}\). It costs four multiplications and catches the most common arithmetic slip in the course.
    • Check every eigenvalue set against the trace and the determinant: they must sum to \(\operatorname{tr}\) and multiply to \(\det\). Two checks, no extra work.
    • Check a discriminant function by confirming that \(\bar y_1 - \bar y_2 = D^{2}\); if it does not, the coefficient vector is wrong.
    • Check a scaling solution by rebuilding one or two distances from the recovered coordinates.
    • State the assumption you are relying on. Hotelling's \(T^{2}\) needs multivariate normality; the two-sample version and discriminant analysis need equal dispersion matrices as well. Writing that down is part of the answer, not an aside.

    3. 3. Formula — the formula being used, with the null hypothesis where there is one, and the steps that apply it.
    4. 4. Calculation — every intermediate matrix (the inverse, the pooled matrix, the eigenvalues), not only the final number, and the arithmetic check that applies to that method, from the list above.
    5. 5. Result — the conclusion in words, naming the assumption it rests on.
